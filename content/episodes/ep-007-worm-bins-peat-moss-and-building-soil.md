# Ep 7: Worm Bins, Peat Moss, and Building Soil

> **Quick take:** Part two with Jim Bennett ([[clackamas-coot|Clackamas Coot]]) — a consumer-awareness masterclass on neem quality, the peat and potting soil industries, kelp meal versus seaweed extract, botanical teas, and why your worm bin needs mass, not kitchen scraps.

**Guest:** [[clackamas-coot|Jim Bennett (Clackamas Coot)]] — organic grower and gardener
**Host:** [[tad-hussey|Tad Hussey]], KIS Organics
**Series:** Early Episodes — continuation of [[ep-003-worm-castings-malted-barley-and-the-coot-mix|part one]]

## What the Episode Covers

**Neem, deeply.** Neem is wild-harvested, quality varies by district and supplier (Coot once received a bag adulterated with wood chips), and azadirachtin-only products miss the point — 368 active compounds identified, ~110 researched. Neem disrupts insect feeding and reproduction rather than poisoning; it suppresses pathogenic nematodes and fungi while *enhancing* mycorrhizal colonies; the trees even fix nitrogen. Coot's foliar protocol: 2:1 neem-to-karanja, emulsified with soap nut tea (25% saponins), four applications 72 hours apart, sprayed before dark. India's tradition — and Coot's practice — puts the meal in the soil, not on the leaves. → [[neem-and-karanja]]

**The peat industry from the inside.** Canadian sphagnum peat is quota-managed by the CSPMA; ~8–9 licensed harvesters; SunGro takes 54% of the harvest. Compressed 3.8 cu ft bales are cut frozen and shipped untouched (alive); peat for bagged mixes is kiln-sterilized and steam-injected (dead). Commercial potting soil is a subcontracted loss leader — a truckload mixed in eight minutes — padded with allelopathic timber waste sold as "composted forest materials" at 18¢/cu ft. "Natural" and "topsoil" mean nothing. → [[sphagnum-peat-and-potting-soils]]

**Worm bins need mass.** A Rubbermaid with holes and kitchen scraps isn't vermicomposting — hydration is the enemy, and real bins need thermophilic compost or quality manure as mass. Neem meal and basalt in the bin produced castings that Tad's microscope work showed *increased* beneficial bacteria and fungi. The Northwest Red Worms story: 500 yards of castings and 3,000 pounds of worms sold in a year. → [[vermicomposting]]

**Kelp meal vs. seaweed extract.** A late-1950s treaty defines the terms: extracts are made with sodium/potassium hydroxide and lose the hormones and vitamins. Meal goes in the soil; extract is a soluble transition tool off bottled nutrients (cold-processed *Ascophyllum nodosum* only); never extract in compost tea. Dr. T.L. Senn's 55 years of Clemson research is the evidence base. → [[kelp-and-seaweed]]

**Botanical teas.** One cup alfalfa + ¼ cup kelp per 5 gallons, aerated overnight — "never a garden that didn't see vast improvement within 48 hours." Alfalfa carries ~30,000 ppm saponins; neem meal tea (2 cups/5 gal, 24 h) drenches out fungus gnat larvae; the spent muck feeds the worm bin since teas extract only 45–50% of the material. → [[botanical-teas]]

**Cheap, honest fungicide.** Sodium bicarbonate — plain baking soda — is 80% as effective as the potassium bicarbonate in $30 retail fungicides (per Steve Diver's analysis). One tablespoon per gallon for outdoor powdery mildew; must be fresh each season. PNW reality check: organically PM-free squash is close to impossible. → [[biocontrols-and-ipm]]

**Coot's raised-bed spring routine:** kelp meal, neem meal, basalt, then an inch of vermicompost on top. That's the program.
