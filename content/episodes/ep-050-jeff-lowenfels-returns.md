# Episode 50 — Jeff Lowenfels Returns

> **Quick take:** For the podcast's 50th episode, [[tad-hussey]] brings back his very first guest, [[jeff-lowenfels]], for a wide-ranging conversation: rhizophagy, chitin-enriched designer compost teas, a revised answer on bacterial vs. fungal soil for cannabis, microbial biomass testing, heavy-metal regulation gaps, and why autoflowering cannabis might be "the next tomato."

## Episode Overview

Jeff Lowenfels — author of the Teaming trilogy and the podcast's first-ever guest — returns to mark episode 50. The conversation is a grab bag of what's new and what's been corrected since his first appearance, ending with a preview of his then-upcoming fourth book on autoflowering cannabis.

## Key Topics

### Rhizophagy and endophytes

Jeff recounts stumbling onto the [[rhizophagy-cycle]] via an article about tundra plants ingesting organic molecules — surprising because the soil food web model assumes microbes must put a charge on nutrients before plants can take them up. He walks through the cycle (bacteria enter the root meristem, shed their cell walls, are oxidatively stripped of nutrients, then exit through a root hair tip and rebuild their walls) and explains the two transport routes through a plant: the **symplastic pathway** (cell to cell, through cells) and the **apoplastic pathway** (traveling within the cell walls). He notes that if rhizophagy proves to be a significant nutrition route, he'll "have to revise the books."

Above ground, every plant hosts up to ~300–350 endophytic organisms. Commercial endophytic bacteria products are emerging — Tad mentions trials at a local cannabis facility using an endophyte (sourced from Yellowstone's thermal vents) marketed for abiotic stress mitigation.

### Designer compost teas

A speaker Jeff heard in Baltimore — James Sotillo (rendered "Cotillo" in the transcript), the compost tea brewer who maintained New York's High Line and Battery Park plantings and whose talk inspired the microBIOMETER — made **specialized teas** from composts deliberately enriched with chitin (via coastal kelp/seaweed soils and chitin-bodied organisms) and with metabolites of mass-reared beneficial insects. Chitin and the chitinase-producing bacteria that break it down deter root-feeding nematodes and insects; Tad notes insect frass works on the same principle, and Korean Natural Farming's indigenous-microbe teas are a parallel trend. See [[compost-tea]].

### Bacterial vs. fungal soil — Jeff revises his answer

Jeff revisits a controversy from his first appearance: he had said cannabis, as an annual, likes bacterially dominated soil, while [[dr-elaine-ingham]]'s research suggests it prefers slightly fungal. His updated rule of thumb: **in the ground more than a year → more fungal; less than a year → more bacterial** — and variety matters (a 9-week autoflower clearly favors bacterial soil; a 9-month Afghani wants more fungi). "Bacterially dominated" never means fungus-free. Both he and Tad land on the same deeper point: **the plant is in control** — it adjusts its exudates to recruit the community it needs, so tea-making should aim for microbial diversity and nutrient cycling, not engineered dominance ratios. See [[soil-food-web]] and [[compost-tea]].

### pH, buffering, and testing

Microbes buffer pH much of the time (Tad estimates growers "get away with it" 75–80% of the time), but pH still matters — specifically at the rhizosphere. Extreme source water (7.5+) warrants acidification (e.g., citric acid), and commercial growers should run baseline soil tests (Mehlich 3 + saturated paste) and track trends with consistent methods. Jeff doesn't test pH routinely but checks it first when a plant looks wrong. See [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]].

### microBIOMETER and biological testing

Jeff endorses the microBIOMETER, an in-field microbial biomass test (scale ~0–2000; 285 is poor, 1700 excellent). It doesn't identify species or separate bacteria from fungi (a beta version aims to add a bacteria:fungi ratio and pH), so it complements rather than replaces chemical tests and microscopy. Its real use is **trend tracking**: before/after comparisons to see whether an input or tea actually raises biomass, and watching seasonal exudate surges or flowering-stage shifts. Solvita CO2 respiration tests are good but expensive; PLFA is the lab standard but slow and ~$500. See [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis]].

### Heavy metals and the regulation gap

A listener's raised bed (built with bagged potting soil) tested at 40 ppm lead vs. 0 ppm in native soil. Digging into it, Tad found that products registered as *fertilizers* require heavy-metal testing, but *potting soils and soil amendments* largely don't (Washington), and Oregon only tests bagged — not bulk — product, against a ~238–243 ppm lead threshold derived from decades-old FDA consulting work. Jeff's points: lead exposure is **cumulative**; radioactivity isn't tested in potting soil at all; Alaska doesn't even test cannabis for pesticides. Cannabis and root crops are dynamic accumulators, and extraction concentrates whatever is in the leaf into the oil. Moral: **know your sources and test** — including for "good" things like phosphorus, which at ~250 ppm (vs. a target of ≤50) can suppress [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for decades. See [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis]].

### Autoflowering cannabis — "the next tomato"

Jeff previews *DIY Autoflowering Cannabis: A New Way to Grow* (then due in October): photoperiod-independent plants descended from *Cannabis ruderalis*, going seed-to-harvest in 7–9 weeks, now nearly matching photoperiod strains in potency and yield. He predicts they'll do for gardening what snap peas did for vegetables — a genuinely new hobby plant. See [[autoflowering-cannabis]].

### Odds and ends

- Watering remains the #1 and #2 gardening mistake (over- and under-watering); we water as much to keep microbes alive as to hydrate the plant. Tad attributes ~75% of "deficiency" complaints to watering habits. See [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship]].
- Looking back at the trilogy, Jeff says his biggest omission was **algae** as part of the soil food web; the books are now in 11 languages.
- A study found 511 bacterial taxa that appear in soils everywhere on Earth — pointing toward a future of cheap at-home microbial identification.

## Key Takeaways

- Rhizophagy may be a major, still-uncharacterized route of plant nutrition; watch this space
- Compost tea goal: diversity and nutrient cycling — the plant, not the brewer, controls the rhizosphere
- Crop duration rule of thumb: <1 year in the ground favors bacterial soil, >1 year favors fungal
- Microbial buffering covers most pH situations, but extreme water or inputs still need correction
- Biomass meters are trend tools, not species IDs — pair them with smell, microscopy, and chemistry
- Potting soils and amendments are barely regulated for heavy metals; test what you grow in
- Autoflowers: 7–9 weeks seed-to-harvest, no photoperiod requirement, breeding cycles 4× faster
