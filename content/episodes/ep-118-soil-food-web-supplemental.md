## The Soil Food Web, In Brief

Tad frames the [[soil-food-web]] as established, universally accepted soil science that has been wrapped in unnecessary controversy by dogmatic practitioners. His tour of the fundamentals:

- **Why a web, not a chain:** the processes are too complex and interwoven for a linear trophic ladder, and because most participants are microscopic, research is still catching up. The famous teaspoon-of-soil statistics — over a billion bacteria, several yards of fungal hyphae, thousands of protozoa, and 40–50+ nematodes — hint at the scale.
- **The engine is plant carbon:** plants photosynthesize, then excrete sugars, amino acids, organic acids, vitamins, and enzymes as [[photosynthesis-and-root-exudates|root exudates]]. Exudates feed symbiotic bacteria and archaea, attract organisms antagonistic to pathogens, and buffer rhizosphere pH — one reason organic growers tolerate wider pH ranges than hydroponic growers (see [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]]).
- **Archaea count too:** single-celled organisms originally classified as bacteria and first found at deep-sea vents, archaea are now known to live everywhere — soil, oceans, our stomachs and skin — filling roles much like bacteria.
- **The microbial loop:** bacteria consume exudates; predators like bacteria-feeding nematodes, flagellates, and ciliates eat the bacteria and excrete plant-available ionic nutrients (phosphate, nitrate) — *identical* to the forms in chemical fertilizers. A single bacteria-feeding nematode can consume up to 5,000 bacterial cells a day, about six and a half times its body weight.
- **Decomposition and humus:** bacteria and fungi are the primary decomposers, releasing enzymes that break organic matter into recyclable inorganic forms. Stable humus is organic matter broken down past the point of recognizing its source.
- **Rhizophagy:** Tad highlights [[dr-james-white]]'s [[rhizophagy-cycle]] research (popularized in [[jeff-lowenfels]]' *Teaming with Bacteria*) — endophytic bacteria enter root cells, shed their cell walls, surrender nutrients, then exit through root hair tips to recharge in the soil. The founding observation dates only to 2008, when University of Queensland researchers found a non-pathogenic *E. coli* cycling with brewer's yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) — a reminder of how recent this science is.

## What Microbes Can and Cannot Do

Tad's recurring theme: the soil food web is crucial — it is the primary way plants receive nutrients in an organic system, and microbes help protect plants from pathogens and build [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|soil structure]]. But he openly does not subscribe to everything promoted by self-described "soil food web consultants," and he questions whether fungal-to-bacterial ratios are a meaningful management metric. He groups dogmatic soil-food-web thinking with biodynamics, Korean natural farming, mineral balancing, and permaculture as methods that can drift from cultivation practice into identity and religion.

## Supporting the Food Web in Practice

The practical heart of the episode — four straightforward habits:

1. **Moisture first.** Maintaining optimal moisture is "the absolute most important thing" for microbial life. Treat fallow soil as if it still holds a plant and keep [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|watering]] it. To reactivate soil that has dried out, work in a handful or two of organic alfalfa meal or water with one tablespoon per gallon of unsulfured blackstrap molasses before planting.
2. **Use high-quality compost or castings.** Look for biological diversity and microbial biomass *with* balanced mineral content. Bad compost carries real risks: incomplete composting, persistent herbicides or pesticides, weed seeds, and pathogens. Know the inputs, know the process, trust your source (see [[composting-and-organic-inputs]] and [[vermicomposting]]).
3. **Mind soil disturbance — without dogma.** Mechanical tilling can wreck soil structure and biology, but digging amendments into a bed is not the same thing, and some disturbance can even encourage microbial diversity (see [[reusing-soil]]).
4. **Keep a living plant in the soil** whenever possible, so root exudates keep the nutrient-cycling community fed.

## Key Takeaways

- The soil food web is complex, dynamic, and constantly changing — and it is settled science, not a belief system
- Nutrient cycling through the microbial loop is the primary way plants in organic systems receive nutrients, in the same ionic forms chemical fertilizers supply
- Moisture management is the single most important practice for keeping soil biology active
- Compost quality varies enormously; biological diversity must come with balanced minerals and a trustworthy process
- Tilling with a machine and digging in amendments are different magnitudes of disturbance — and not all disturbance is harmful
- Healthy skepticism applies even to the soil food web itself: microbes cannot do everything consultants claim, and F:B ratios are questionable as a management target
