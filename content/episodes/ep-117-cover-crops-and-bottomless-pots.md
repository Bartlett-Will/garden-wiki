# Episode 117: Cover Crops and Soil Testing with Steve Cantwell (Part 2)

> Guest: [[steve-cantwell|Steve Cantwell]] (Green Life Productions)

## Summary

[[tad-hussey|Tad Hussey]] talks with [[steve-cantwell|Steve Cantwell]], former UFC fighter and founder of the Las Vegas living-soil cannabis facility Green Life Productions. The two friends hold opposite positions on indoor cover crops and use the disagreement to map the real trade-offs: Cantwell is a "cover crop guy" who runs purpose-driven companion and trap plants, while Tad sees indoor cover crops as one more crop to manage, a pest/disease vector, and a complication in fast (five-cycle) commercial systems. The conversation moves through hop latent viroid and "dudding," the roots-in-vs-roots-out debate, Cantwell's signature bottomless-pot transplant technique, the importance of SOPs, and the surprisingly strict regulatory rules around compost tea.

## Key Topics

- [[cover-crops-and-crop-rotation]] — indoor cover cropping done with purpose: dichondra as a neutral blanket cover, companion/trap plants placed deliberately, why Cantwell dropped clover indoors, and mint as a rat repellent (and invasive hazard)
- [[hop-latent-viroid]] — dudding, latency, keeping plants unstressed, culling affected genetics, and whether tissue culture is worth it
- [[bottomless-pots]] — the accidental discovery and full method of Cantwell's no-dig transplant system
- [[compost-tea]] — NOP/USDA water-quality rules that gate legal compost tea use in commercial facilities

## Key Takeaways

- **Cover crops need a purpose and a place.** Start with a neutral blanket like dichondra; add companion or trap plants only with a defined reason and location (corners, high-traffic areas easy to monitor). "You can't judge a fish by how well it climbs a tree" — right plant, right place.
- **Clover is out indoors.** Cantwell culled all clover: too much pest and disease risk (thrips, pest outbreaks feed off it) for the nitrogen reward. Mint repelled a rat infestation effectively but is dangerously invasive and must be chopped and culled aggressively.
- **Tad's case against indoor cover crops:** it's another crop to manage; he prefers mixing nutrients straight into the rhizosphere for fast five-cycle turns; tolerates minor soil disturbance (digging in nutrients ≠ tilling); indoor agriculture is inherently artificial, so pull practices from nature *strategically*; and cover crops add pest pressure and HLVd risk.
- **Hop latent viroid is latent — manage stress, not just infection.** Healthy, unstressed plants can carry HLVd without dudding. Culling chronically affected genetics (his "pure sunshine"/"shiners") ended his dudding problem. 100% tissue culture works but is often overkill.
- **Roots in or out?** Tad pulls root balls to lower viral load (higher in roots) and remove heavy metals; Cantwell leaves them to preserve worms and biology, using a grid-planting pattern so old root balls break down in place.
- **Bottomless pots.** Cut the bottom off the pot, root the plant until it holds its soil, set it on bare bed soil — no hole. Cut placing a room from 1.5 days to a half day, reduced transplant shock, and raised yields. Water pots directly for ~2 weeks until roots reach the bed.
- **SOPs separate good growers from great ones.** "I grow employees, not cannabis." SOPs let you trace failures and satisfy regulators unfamiliar with living soil — otherwise they "deem everything a pest and disease and shut you down."
- **Compost tea is regulated.** NOP/USDA water-quality guidelines require validated (thermophilic) compost plus third-party lab testing of two separate brews below set E. coli thresholds before a tea is legal to use; unbrewed extracts from thermophilic compost are unrestricted. Standing NOP rule: no compost tea within 60 days of harvest, especially with manure inputs. Both hosts note the actual contamination risk of well-aerated tea is lower than commonly stated (Tad's father's testing with [[dr-elaine-ingham|Elaine Ingham]] found E. coli couldn't survive highly aerobic conditions), but the regulatory exposure is real.
