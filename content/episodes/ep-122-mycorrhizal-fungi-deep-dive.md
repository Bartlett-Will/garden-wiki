# Episode 122 — Mycorrhizal Fungi: A Deep Dive

> **Quick take:** A solo [[tad-hussey]] AMA episode dedicated to clearing up the misinformation around [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] — proper terminology, the four main types and which one gardeners actually need, how the plant–fungus relationship stays honest, why endomycorrhizal products are so much weaker than ectomycorrhizal ones, and what the (mixed) cannabis research actually shows.

## Episode Overview

This is Tad Hussey's second AMA-style solo episode, prompted by the volume of "misinformation and claims" he sees about mycorrhizal fungi. He frames the topic around his friend [[jeff-lowenfels]] (a stickler for terminology) and points listeners to his earlier interview with mycorrhizal researcher Dr. Yoram Kapolnik in episode 77.

## Key Topics

### Terminology — getting the words right

- **Myco** = fungus; **rhizo** = root; **mycology** = the study of fungi; mushrooms are the fruiting body of some fungi.
- **Mycorrhizal fungi** = the fungi themselves. **Mycorrhizae** = the actual association/relationship between roots and fungi. Use the right word for the right thing.
- The fungal–root relationship was first written about as early as **1885**; the symbiosis itself is over **400 million years** old and occurs in nearly all ecosystems, from deserts to tropical forests. Many desert plants cannot survive without it. The fungal biomass acts as a virtual root system, increasing the root's absorbing surface area by up to **40×**.

### The four types — and the one that matters for gardeners

1. **Endomycorrhizal** (the one gardeners and cannabis growers want) — grows *within* the cortical cells, intercellularly.
2. **Ectomycorrhizal** — forms a sheath *around* roots (ecto = outer); the white hyphae you see in root photos. Basidiomycetes/ascomycetes that fruit as mushrooms, puffballs, toadstools — truffles are ectomycorrhizal. Associated with conifers and forest trees; great for forest restoration.
3. **Ericoid** — blueberries, cranberries, rhododendrons, azaleas; only one commercial product known; irrelevant to most gardeners. (Itself a form of endomycorrhizal fungi.)
4. **Orchid** — orchids only. (Also a form of endomycorrhizal fungi.)

The endomycorrhizal type most growers hear about is **VAM** (Vesicular Arbuscular Mycorrhiza), shortened to **AM / arbuscular mycorrhiza** — the most common and abundant endomycorrhizal fungi. See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for the structural detail (arbuscules, hyphal coils, cortical cells).

### How the relationship stays mutualistic

Citing the 2011 paper *Reciprocal Rewards Stabilize Cooperation in the Mycorrhizal Symbiosis* (Kiers et al., Netherlands): plants can **detect, discriminate, and reward** the best fungal partners with more carbohydrates, while the fungi reciprocate by routing more nutrients to the roots that pay the most carbon. This checks-and-balances system is what keeps an inherently exploitable relationship fair and lets the partners co-evolve.

### Why endo products are weak — culturing and strains

- Ectomycorrhizal fungi can be grown **in vitro** in a lab; endomycorrhizal fungi **require a living plant host**, making them far harder to produce. That's why commercial endo products have much **lower spore counts**.
- Buying caution: it's not enough that a product *contains* mycorrhizae. Many fertilizer/soil companies add a token amount just to get it on the label — too little to cause actual infection in a useful timeframe. You need the **right type** at a **high enough concentration**.
- **Species and strain matter.** Most common: *Glomus intraradices* (= *Rhizophagus irregularis*); also *G. mosseae*, *G. aggregatum*, *G. deserticola*. The same species sourced differently yields different strains — e.g. the product DenoMyco isolated a resilient native Israeli strain effective across a wider pH range (per Dr. Yoram Kapolnik, Volcani Institute). Strains differ in drought, flood, and temperature tolerance, so identical species names on two containers don't guarantee identical results.
- Few companies actually produce endomycorrhizal fungi commercially; most North American supply traces back to a handful of producers, with everyone else repackaging.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> The cannabis research is genuinely **mixed**, but two papers stand out:
> - **2022, Thailand** (Simakram et al.) — compared *Rhizophagus proliferus* and *R. aggregatus* against a synthetic-fertilizer control. After 60 days, mycorrhizal plants beat the control on stem height, stem diameter, stem dry weight, branch count, and flower count. *R. aggregatus* outperformed *R. proliferus* and produced the highest CBD and THC concentrations.
> - **2021, Agricultural University of Athens** — *R. irregularis* on cannabis seedlings. Root biomass, fresh weight, and nitrogen content were unaffected, but root length (+5%), stem dry weight (+24.3%), survival rate, and phosphorus content (+12.4%) all rose significantly — at high inoculation rates (40/80/120 spores per liter of nutrient solution), far above any label rate.

## Key Takeaways

- Say "mycorrhizal fungi" for the organism, "mycorrhizae" for the relationship
- Of the four types (endo, ecto, ericoid, orchid), gardeners and cannabis growers want **endomycorrhizal / arbuscular (VAM/AM)**
- The plant rewards the most productive fungal partners with more carbon — cooperation is enforced, not assumed
- Endo products have low spore counts because the fungi need a plant host to grow; check type, concentration, species, and strain — not just "contains mycorrhizae"
- Cannabis benefits are real but inconsistent across studies; species and strain choice and high inoculation rates matter
