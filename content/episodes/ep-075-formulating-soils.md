# Episode 75: Formulating Soils and New Products with Rob Knechtel

> **Quick take:** Rob Knechtel of Black Swallow Living Soils and Tad Hussey walk through how a commercial living soil actually gets formulated — sourcing and vetting every input, balancing the mix around your compost, iterating through lab tests and plant trials, and cooking the finished soil before anything goes in it.

**Guest:** [[rob-knechtel]] — founder, Black Swallow Living Soils (Brantford, Ontario)
**Host:** [[tad-hussey]]

*Rob and Tad collaborated for two to three years to develop the Canadian "KISS Mix," blended by Black Swallow from Tad's formulation — this episode is the story of that process, told as a how-to for anyone formulating their own soil.*

## Key Topics

### Formulating a Soil Mix
- Tad's three pillars: **physical** properties (drainage, CEC, water saturation), **biological** properties (the microbiology that cycles nutrients), and **chemical** properties (mineral content)
- **Formulate around your compost** — the quality and analysis of your compost/castings determines how much of everything else goes in; online recipes fail because you don't have their compost
- Base mix ratios: the common third/third/third works, but Tad leans toward ~20% compost, ~33% aeration, and the balance peat
- The KISS Mix uses three compost sources (fish, leaf, worm castings — later a fourth, horse compost from a producer who studied under [[dr-elaine-ingham|Elaine Ingham]]) for biological diversity
- Rice hulls lesson: imperfectly blended rice hulls channel water and leave dry pockets — perlite won on performance, price, and availability
- 19 amendments in the final mix; complexity enhances performance, but only when balanced — beware the "kitchen sink" approach
- Mix → lab test → tweak → retest → plant trials; you will not nail it on the first batch

### Sourcing and Vetting Inputs
- Buy from producers, not distributors; Rob visited facilities across multiple provinces to compare suppliers
- Lab-test inputs, especially for **heavy metals** — anything mined (rock dusts) and any compost is a potential source
- Geography drives formulation: Ontario's Canadian Shield rock dusts are cheap and excellent, while pumice is a "luxury amendment" that must be trucked in
- Compost is judged on its **biology contribution**, not just nutritional analysis

### Cooking and Storing Soil
- Freshly mixed soil heats up 2–3 days after mixing as bacteria and archaea bloom on the new amendments — an exothermic "cooking" process
- Above 131°F kills weed seeds and pathogens; keep below 160°F to preserve beneficials; turn it like a compost pile until it returns to ambient (a week or more), then plant
- Small batches won't spike in temperature but still need about a week to begin nutrient cycling
- Store soil outdoors in the elements, "like it has a plant in it" — it does not lose viability, but nitrogen and potassium leach, so reamend mobile nutrients and wake the biology (compost tea, alfalfa meal, diluted molasses) a week or two before use

### Lab Testing Discipline
- **Stay with one lab** — results aren't comparable across labs; Logan Labs (Mehlich 3 + saturated paste) is the de facto US standard, Rob uses A&L Labs in Ontario, and Tad calibrated the two against each other
- Sap and tissue testing let larger producers dial in fertility mid-cycle

### Water-Only, Vegan Mixes, and Common Mistakes
- "Water only" implies a soil ideally balanced from sprout to harvest regardless of genetics — a useful simplification for beginners, but experienced growers gain from targeted teas and top-dressings
- Veganic mixes are formulatable but cost more — vegan inputs run low in N and P; the counterargument is that bone, blood, and feather meal recycle waste streams that would otherwise be landfill
- Prilling amendments with a lignin binder cuts dust and makes handling safer
- The two biggest grower problems: hydroponic habits carried into living soil ("one foot in each pool"), and doing too much at once — start with water only, change one variable at a time, and put the money into tools (PAR meter, moisture meter) instead of products
- Genetics play one of the biggest roles in final yield and potency — no mix is perfect for every cultivar
