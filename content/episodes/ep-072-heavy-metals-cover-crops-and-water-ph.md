# Episode 72: Heavy Metals, Cover Crops, and Water pH with Bryant Mason

> **Quick take:** Certified crop advisor [[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]] ("the Soil Doctor") covers why soilless media breaks the rules of classical soil science, how to manage nitrogen and potassium round after round, what makes a good compost, why alkalinity matters more than water pH, which inputs bring in heavy metals, and when cover crops do and don't make sense.

[[tad-hussey|Tad Hussey]] hosts Bryant Mason, a certified crop advisor (American Association of Agronomy) specializing in organic cannabis nutrition, soilless media, and data-driven soil testing through his practice, Soil Doctor Consulting. The conversation is a rapid tour of practical agronomy: testing, nitrogen and potassium management, compost evaluation, water chemistry, heavy metals, and cover crops.

## Key Topics

### Soilless media is not topsoil

Traditional soil science was developed in mineral topsoil (sand, silt, clay). Soilless media has no clay colloid, larger and less homogeneous particles, more pore space, and faster drainage — so CEC and organic matter numbers must be interpreted differently, and nutrient holding comes from organic matter rather than mineral colloids. Bryant thinks in terms of **solubility** rather than balancing the mineral colloid. See [[soilless-media-vs-topsoil]].

### The three pillars, and which to manage

Physical, chemical, and biological properties form a triangle, and every management decision changes all three. Bryant's position: science shows long-term microbial community composition is dictated by physical and chemical conditions (especially pH), so he optimizes those and "lets the biology fall into place." Biological tests can measure but not yet target. A 2020 meta-analysis of 1,200+ experiments found **pH is the single biggest driver of soil microbial species diversity** ([[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]]).

### Nitrogen and potassium: the mined nutrients

Nitrogen and potassium are removed in the largest quantities by heavy-yielding crops and deplete first. Nitrogen is mobile, ephemeral, and behaves like a gas tank — too much invites pests, disease, and quality loss; running out halts metabolism. Targets and the amino-acid nitrogen strategy are covered in [[nitrogen-and-phosphorus]]. Potassium is highly soluble and leachable, antagonistic with calcium (load calcium in veg, feed potassium through flower), and best buffered by regular small compost additions.

### Compost as the slow-release bank

Compost is Bryant's winner for slow-release N and K — "putting money in the bank" each round. Quality criteria: well-aged (low ammonium:nitrate ratio), C:N at or below ~18:1, low sodium/salinity, known total NPK, and ideally manure-based or fungal-rich (Johnson-Su bioreactor). Soil recipes are only as good as the compost that goes into them — the same recipe can produce a nearly ideal soil or garbage ([[the-coot-mix]], [[composting-and-organic-inputs]]).

### Water chemistry: alkalinity over pH

The pH of irrigation water matters less than its **alkalinity** — its resistance to pH change, driven by bicarbonates. Low-alkalinity water buffers instantly to the soil's pH no matter its own reading; high-alkalinity water shifts soil pH over time. Acidify when total alkalinity exceeds ~120 ppm. Acid choice (citric, phosphoric, sulfuric) involves trade-offs. See [[water-chemistry-and-alkalinity]].

### Heavy metals: know your inputs

Bryant compiled heavy metal test data across input categories. If you're failing for arsenic, **look at your kelp meal first**; alfalfa, some rock dusts, and some gypsum brands also contribute. Cultivar genetics strongly affect uptake, and cannabis/hemp are excellent bioaccumulators. Don't panic unless you're actually failing. See [[heavy-metals-management]].

### Cover crops: context is everything

Outdoors: biomass production, weed suppression, and nitrogen fixation are the big three benefits. Indoors at commercial scale, both Bryant and Tad lean against cover crops — pest pressure, labor, fertility complexity, and tillage for re-amending outweigh the benefits (trap/banker plants in separate containers are fine). A 2020 meta-analysis of 27 studies found cover crop **blends performed no better than the best single species 88% of the time** ([[cover-crops-and-crop-rotation]]).

### Tools

Bryant calls the microscope "the least useful of all the agronomic tools" for the average grower — buy a pulse meter (or dino-lite scope for pests, dissolved oxygen meter, pH pen) and a tensiometer first. Tad adds an affordable soil sampler probe, though a trowel works better in soilless media.

### Biostimulants

Exciting for quality and genetic expression, but they cannot replace nutrition — plants need actual nutrient quantity, and most biostimulants don't supply it. Crop responses to teas and ferments may be partly the nitrogen they contain, not just the microbes — an experiment worth running.
