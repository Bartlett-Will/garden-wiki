# Episode 3: Worm Castings, Malted Barley, and the Coot Mix

> **Quick take:** Jim Bennett — Clackamas Coot — explains how neem and malted barley supercharge worm bins, why his famous soil mix is really a modified 1930s Cornell mix that lives or dies on casting quality, and how a bread-baking course led him to discover malted grain enzymes for soil.

**Guest:** [[clackamas-coot]] — organic grower, creator of the Coot Mix, malted barley pioneer
**Host:** [[tad-hussey]]

*Part one of a two-part interview. Part two covers neem in depth and the 40-year-old cultivar Coot maintains.*

## Key Topics

### Vermicomposting with Neem and Barley
- Adding **neem meal** to worm bedding remarkably increased both reproduction rates and worm length/girth
- Doug at Northwest Red Worms added nothing but **malted barley** to a manure windrow and saw the best worm reproduction of his career — the grain enzymes deconstruct material so microbes (the worms' food) multiply faster
- Coot's 100-gallon fabric-pot bins: three-year-old black leaf mold layered lasagna-style with kelp meal, barley, neem, and karanja
- **European nightcrawlers** work low in the bedding (good finishers, wrong choice for vertical flow-through bins); **red wigglers** mass near the top
- Cocoon math: ~3.8 worms per cocoon, sexual maturity in 42 days, ~3 cocoons per worm per week — under perfect conditions a pound of worms could become a thousand pounds in a year (Edwards, *Vermiculture Technology*)
- Aged manure as foodstock: 4–6 weeks of aging deconstructs the vermicides given to livestock
- Castings exit the worm coated in **calcium carbonate slime** — Doug's raw castings tested at pH 6.8 with no amendments
- Cold (non-thermophilic) processing preserves **secondary metabolites** from neem and karanja — keeping their insect- and disease-suppression properties in the finished castings
- Even mediocre bagged compost comes out good after a pass through the worms — vermicomposting as value-add

### The Coot Mix
- Equal thirds sphagnum peat, aeration (pumice/scoria preferred over perlite, which floats to the top), and quality castings — amended with kelp, neem, basalt rock dust; crab meal now largely replaced by barley's chitin
- A modified **Cornell mix** (1937–38) — predating perlite
- "You didn't make my mix... you don't have my castings" — substituting poor compost is the universal failure mode
- Giant plant, tiny pot doesn't work organically; auto-pruning fabric pots beat rigid pots for biomass but won't double it

### Malted Barley and Enzymes
- Discovered via diastatic malt in artisan bread baking (San Francisco Baking Institute)
- ~8 enzymes: amylase (all brewers know), plus protease, urease, phosphatase, and **chitinase** — the one behind insect-egg destruction and the chitinase–salicylic acid SAR pathway
- Observed with a 33-year clone control: ~15% faster flowering, dramatic resin set, thicker stronger branches, accelerated rooting on transplants
- Whole grains top-dressed in a public garden produced explosive visible mycelium within two weeks — outstanding fungal food
- Buy any malted grain at a brew store on price (rye = slightly more phosphorus); grind fine for top-dressing; never substitute spent brewery grain
- Two-row vs six-row barley: six-row's extra amylase doesn't extend to the soil-relevant enzymes — don't pay the premium

### Peat Moss, Politics, and Product Quality
- Peat sustainability arguments largely reflect British Isles politics (peat as rural heating fuel) — Canadian harvest is strictly quota-controlled by the CSPMA; Sun Gro harvested 54% of the total allowance
- 3.8 cu ft bales are cut and wrapped on site; bagged peat is kiln-dried (microbes killed), ground, and steam-rewetted for shelf appeal
- "Composted forest products" / "forest humus" on labels = code for sawdust
- Peat's mild acidity is a feature: it leaves headroom to add calcium to target
- Compost value is mostly a function of **cure time** — the best local product sits in static piles for 2–3 years

### Dynamic Accumulators
- **Kelp**: 83 elements in balance, excludes salt, harvested as fertilizer since 1372; alginic acid holds 250–300% of its weight in water; powders/liquids lose hormones and metabolites
- **Comfrey**: Bocking 14 history (Lawrence Hills, Essex, 1930s; research station in Canby, Oregon); roots twice as deep as a fruit tree; permanent once planted; chop-and-drop beats fermented teas
- **Alfalfa**: 30+ foot roots into volcanic soils by year four or five
- Add accumulators to compost in the mesophilic stage so heat doesn't destroy their compounds
- Excess magnesium tightens soil and restricts air/water flow — beware single-mineral dosing
