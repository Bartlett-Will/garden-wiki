## Overview

This episode covers much of the same ground as [[ep-041-applied-horticulture]] — unsurprisingly, since it is the same educator — but framed around Don's career story and his core teaching philosophy: be a *critical thinker* and a *discerning practitioner*, always asking what is actually happening rather than following recipes. He recounts leaving a "science only" plant-science program that couldn't relate the science to real plants, then leaving industry practitioners who told him to stop asking "why," and finally finding a home at Lake Washington marrying the two for 40 years.

## Key Concepts Covered

- **Root hairs as the real interface.** All water and nutrient absorption happens at one-cell-thick [[root-hairs-and-nutrient-uptake|root hairs]], not the visible roots. Damage from over/under-watering, over-fertilization, or high salt kills them and produces identical above-ground symptoms (yellowing, marginal leaf necrosis, gray-green foliage).
- **Overwatering is drought stress.** A waterlogged plant isn't "suffocating" or "drowning" — dead root hairs simply can't take up water. See [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship]].
- **Opportunistic fungi feed on already-dead roots.** A fungus on a declining plant is often a *decomposer of dead tissue*, not the cause of death — so a fungicide won't fix what was a watering problem.
- **Active uptake via cation/anion exchange.** Roots release H+ and take up nutrient ions; the process is powered by respiration (ATP), not passive "drinking." See [[root-hairs-and-nutrient-uptake]].
- **pH governs availability, not just presence.** Plants take up nitrate (NO3-) not nitrite; the right pH keeps nutrients in a takeable form and lowers the energy cost of exchange. See [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]].
- **Transplanting and root scoring.** Roots lack the node/internode structure of stems, so they branch wherever cut — score the root ball to force outward colonization. Progressive potting preserves the air-water relationship; a cutting dropped into a huge pot stalls. See [[transplanting]].
- **Root girdling.** As roots thicken via their lateral meristem, circling roots strangle one another's vascular tissue (like an un-loosened stake tie on a stem), choking water and nutrient flow — another reason to score at every repot. See [[transplanting#Root Girdling]].
- **Media boundaries.** Hydrophobic or differently-textured rooting media create barriers water won't cross; commingle old and new media ("don't make a pot inside the ground"). See [[transplanting]].
- **Apical dominance and pruning.** Auxins from the apical meristem suppress lateral buds; remove the tip and you get branching (Christmas tree → candelabra). Point-of-origin removal vs. tipping determines whether you reduce or multiply growth. See [[pruning-and-training]] and [[plant-hormones-and-growth]].
- **Auxin phototropism.** Plants don't "reach" for light — light degrades auxin on the lit side, the shady side elongates, and the stem nods toward the source. See [[plant-hormones-and-growth]].
- **Think like a plant — seasonal timing.** Spring growth runs on food stored in roots the previous fall, so don't fertilize in Feb/March (it just leaches); fertilize fruiting plants around Memorial Day when reserves bottom out. Prune in February for a rejuvenating growth surge; prune a hedge in July (after the surge, lowest reserves) to keep it tidy for nearly a year. Don't prune in fall while sugars are migrating back to the roots — and the old "fall pruning triggers tender growth that freezes" rule is "a crock." See [[pruning-and-training#Seasonal Timing]].
- **Fruiting wood.** Know which wood your crop fruits on. Single-crop (floricane) raspberries fruit on second-year canes then that cane dies (cut it to the ground); everbearing raspberries fruit on the tip of first-year canes and the midsection the next year. Canes are biennial; roots are perennial. Scratch the bark — green means the cane is still alive.
- **Critical thinking about products.** "Product X made my plants take off" usually means it fixed an underlying deficiency (often nitrogen) — the same product can do nothing, or harm, in a soil that doesn't have that deficiency. High-N inputs "work" partly by feeding microbes breaking down woody/bark-heavy mixes.
- **Morning wellness check.** Walk the facility first thing, coffee in hand, as a "well-person check" — no task list — to catch problems early; knock plants out of pots to inspect, smell, and tug roots (white and turgid = healthy; sliming off = root death). See [[field-observation]].

## Practical Stories & Examples

- **Blueberry in biochar soil:** Don planted a blueberry into alkaline biochar-amended soil near pH 7.0 and it sat for two years — abundant nutrients present but unavailable at the wrong pH. Blueberries are a bog plant: they want pH ~4.5-5.5, high moisture *and* high oxygen at the roots. See [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]].
- **Winter lawn nitrate test:** A homeowner tests in a rainy Northwest winter, sees zero nitrate, and concludes the lawn is starved. But nitrate is an anion that leaches with groundwater — there's little in winter regardless. Don't fertilize then; the nitrogen just washes away.
- **Moss in lawns and on roofs:** Moss isn't only a shade problem — it indicates low pH, poor nutrition, compaction, and excess water. Moss thrives in full sun too where those conditions exist; killing the moss without fixing the cause solves nothing.
- **Black pots in a greenhouse:** Roots die on the hot, sunny side of a black nursery pot and migrate to the cooler side — a cue to shade the south side rather than assume the heat was helping.
