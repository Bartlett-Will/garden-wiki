# Mycorrhizal Fungi

> **Quick take:** Mycorrhizal fungi form a partnership with plant roots — the plant feeds the fungus carbon, and the fungus extends the root system's reach for water and nutrients, especially phosphorus.

About 96% of all plant species form mycorrhizal associations. These relationships are ancient — ectomycorrhizal fungi are roughly 400 million years old and helped plants colonize terrestrial land; endomycorrhizal fungi evolved about 200 million years ago. Despite their ubiquity, we now know these partnerships aren't guaranteed to happen on their own and often need deliberate inoculation.

## Endo vs. Ecto

There are seven or eight types of mycorrhizal fungi, classified by how they enter and interact with the plant root. The two major groups for gardeners:

**Endomycorrhizal (arbuscular)** — the type most gardeners need. These fungi are invisible without staining, though their spores are large enough to see with a magnifying glass. They associate with most garden crops, row crops, and cannabis. The fungi don't invade plant cells — instead, they are *invaginated*, forming a membrane interface where nutrients transfer between fungus and plant. One key species for cannabis and most garden crops is *Rhizophagus irregularis*.

**Ectomycorrhizal** — visible to the naked eye as they wrap around roots. Their fruiting bodies are mushrooms or underground structures. They associate primarily with conifers, oaks, and other forest trees. Their spores can travel 3–4 miles on wind, but not much further.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis uses endomycorrhizal fungi, specifically *Rhizophagus irregularis*. When buying inoculant products, check the label for this species. Apply at transplant — roll roots in the inoculant or sprinkle directly on the root ball. Use lower-phosphorus propagation media in early stages so the plant signals for the mycorrhizal relationship rather than getting "lazy" with easy phosphorus.

## How the Partnership Forms

The plant controls whether the relationship begins. It sends a chemical signal through its [[photosynthesis-and-root-exudates|root exudates]] to attract the fungus. Critically, the plant won't send this signal if it's already getting enough phosphorus or nitrogen — there's no reason to trade carbon for nutrients it already has.

This is why excess fertilization can prevent mycorrhizal colonization. If you're giving the plant everything it needs through synthetic inputs, it has no incentive to form the partnership.

**Inoculation timing matters.** You want the earliest possible colonization:
- **Seeds:** Roll seeds in mycorrhizal inoculant so the fungus is present at germination
- **Transplants:** Apply directly to the root zone at transplant — roll roots in inoculant or sprinkle on the root ball
- **Colonization window:** Allow 10–14 days minimum (up to two months for full colonization) before applying phosphorus-heavy amendments

## What the Fungus Provides

Beyond the well-known phosphorus exchange, mycorrhizal fungi:

- **Extend the root system** — fungal hyphae explore far more soil volume than roots alone can reach
- **Store carbon** — endomycorrhizal fungi are coated with **glomalin**, a glycoprotein responsible for roughly 30–35% of soil carbon. This is why tilling damages carbon storage — it destroys the hyphal network and its glomalin coating
- **Improve soil structure** — glomalin acts as a biological glue that helps form [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|soil aggregates]]
- **Connect plants** — mycorrhizal networks can link plants together, enabling nutrient and signal transfer

## What Destroys Mycorrhizal Networks

- **Tilling/rototilling** — physically severs hyphal networks. The deeper and more aggressive, the worse the damage
- **Excess phosphorus or nitrogen** — prevents the plant from signaling for the relationship
- **Certain fungicides** — not all, but some are incompatible with mycorrhizal fungi
- **Removing and replacing soil** — discards the established network (see [[reusing-soil]])

## Taxonomy in Flux

Until recently, mycorrhizal fungi couldn't be reliably identified to species. Now that RNA and DNA testing is available, proper names are being assigned. Labels on commercial products may use outdated names for the next several years as taxonomy catches up. When in doubt, check current nomenclature online.

## Key Takeaways
- 96% of plants need mycorrhizal fungi — if you're not inoculating, your plants may be missing a critical partner
- The plant controls the relationship through chemical signaling — excess fertilizer shuts it down
- Inoculate as early as possible, directly at the root zone
- Protect existing networks by minimizing soil disturbance and avoiding excess P and N
- Glomalin from mycorrhizal fungi is a major source of soil carbon
