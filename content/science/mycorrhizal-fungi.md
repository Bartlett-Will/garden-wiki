# Mycorrhizal Fungi

> **Quick take:** Mycorrhizal fungi form a partnership with plant roots — the plant feeds the fungus carbon, and the fungus extends the root system's reach for water and nutrients, especially phosphorus.

About 96% of all plant species form mycorrhizal associations. These relationships are ancient — mycorrhizal fungi have existed for over 400 million years, and it is the arbuscular (endo)mycorrhizal partnership that is generally credited with helping the first plants colonize land; the ectomycorrhizal association of conifers and other woody trees evolved later. Despite their ubiquity, we now know these partnerships aren't guaranteed to happen on their own and often need deliberate inoculation.

> **A word on words:** *Myco* means fungus and *rhizo* means root, so **mycorrhizal fungi** are the fungi themselves, while **mycorrhizae** is the actual *association* between roots and fungi. Use "mycorrhizal fungi" for the organism and "mycorrhizae" for the relationship. The fungal biomass acts as a virtual root system, increasing the root's absorbing surface area by up to **40×**, and the symbiosis occurs in nearly all ecosystems — many desert plants cannot survive without it.

## The Four Types

Mycorrhizal fungi are classified by how they enter and interact with the root. There are four main types, but only one matters for most gardeners:

1. **Endomycorrhizal (arbuscular)** — the type most gardeners and cannabis growers need (detailed below).
2. **Ectomycorrhizal** — forms a sheath *around* roots (ecto = outer); these are the white hyphae visible in root photos. Mostly basidiomycetes and ascomycetes that fruit as mushrooms, puffballs, and toadstools — truffles are ectomycorrhizal. They associate with conifers, oaks, and other forest trees (great for forest restoration). Their spores travel 3–4 miles on wind, but not much further.
3. **Ericoid** — partners with blueberries, cranberries, rhododendrons, and azaleas. Only one commercial product is known; irrelevant to most gardeners and cannabis. (Technically a form of endomycorrhizal fungi.)
4. **Orchid** — partners with orchids only. (Also a form of endomycorrhizal fungi.)

### Endomycorrhizal (arbuscular) — the gardener's type

These fungi are invisible without staining, though their spores are large enough to see with a magnifying glass. They associate with most garden crops, row crops, and cannabis. The fungi don't invade plant cells — instead they are *invaginated*, forming a membrane interface where nutrients transfer between fungus and plant. The most common form is **VAM** (Vesicular Arbuscular Mycorrhiza), usually shortened to **AM** or **arbuscular mycorrhiza**.

Inside the root, the fungus forms **arbuscules** (named for their tree-branch shape) or **hyphal coils** within the **cortical cells** — the cortex being the outer layer of the root, below the epidermis but outside the vascular bundles. These arbuscules and coils are the main sites of nutrient exchange, while the mycelium extends outward into the soil. One key species for cannabis and most garden crops is *Rhizophagus irregularis* (formerly *Glomus intraradices*).

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis uses endomycorrhizal fungi, specifically *Rhizophagus irregularis*. When buying inoculant products, check the label for this species. Apply at transplant — roll roots in the inoculant or sprinkle directly on the root ball. Use lower-phosphorus propagation media in early stages so the plant signals for the mycorrhizal relationship rather than getting "lazy" with easy phosphorus.
>
> The cannabis research is genuinely **mixed**, but two studies are worth knowing. A 2022 Thailand study (Simakram et al.) found that after 60 days, plants inoculated with *Rhizophagus aggregatus* beat both *R. proliferus* and a synthetic-fertilizer control on stem height, diameter, dry weight, branches, and flowers — and showed the highest CBD and THC. A 2021 Agricultural University of Athens study with *R. irregularis* on seedlings saw no change in root biomass, fresh weight, or nitrogen, but significant gains in root length (+5%), stem dry weight (+24.3%), survival rate, and phosphorus content (+12.4%) — at high inoculation rates (40–120 spores/L), far above any label dose. Takeaway: benefits are real but inconsistent, and species, strain, and dose all matter.

## How the Partnership Forms

The plant controls whether the relationship begins. It sends a chemical signal through its [[photosynthesis-and-root-exudates|root exudates]] to attract the fungus. Critically, the plant won't send this signal if it's already getting enough phosphorus or nitrogen — there's no reason to trade carbon for nutrients it already has.

This is why excess fertilization can prevent mycorrhizal colonization. If you're giving the plant everything it needs through synthetic inputs, it has no incentive to form the partnership.

**Inoculation timing matters.** You want the earliest possible colonization:
- **Seeds:** Roll seeds in mycorrhizal inoculant so the fungus is present at germination
- **Transplants:** Apply directly to the root zone at transplant — roll roots in inoculant or sprinkle on the root ball
- **Colonization window:** Allow 10–14 days minimum (up to two months for full colonization) before applying phosphorus-heavy amendments

## What the Fungus Provides

Beyond the well-known phosphorus exchange, mycorrhizal fungi:

- **Extend the root system** — fungal hyphae explore far more soil volume than roots alone can reach
- **Store carbon** — endomycorrhizal fungi are coated with **glomalin**, a glycoprotein responsible for roughly 30–35% of soil carbon. This is why tilling damages carbon storage — it destroys the hyphal network and its glomalin coating
- **Improve soil structure** — glomalin acts as a biological glue that helps form [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|soil aggregates]]
- **Connect plants** — mycorrhizal networks can link plants together, enabling nutrient and signal transfer

## Keeping the Partnership Honest

This relationship has obvious potential for cheating — each organism is looking out for itself, so why does either party hold up its end? A landmark 2011 study (*Reciprocal Rewards Stabilize Cooperation in the Mycorrhizal Symbiosis*, Kiers et al., Netherlands) found a built-in checks-and-balances system: plants can **detect, discriminate, and reward** the best fungal partners with more carbohydrates, while the fungi reciprocate by routing more nutrients to the roots that pay the most carbon. Cooperation is enforced, not assumed — and this is what lets fungus and plant co-evolve as genuine mutualists.

## Why Commercial Products Vary So Much

Not all inoculants are equal — and endomycorrhizal products are inherently harder to make well:

- **Culturing difficulty.** Ectomycorrhizal fungi can be grown *in vitro* in a lab, but endomycorrhizal fungi **require a living plant host**. That makes them far more difficult to produce, which is why commercial endo products carry much **lower spore counts**.
- **Label padding.** It isn't enough that a product *contains* mycorrhizae. Many fertilizer and soil companies add just a token amount to claim it on the label — too little to cause actual root infection in a useful timeframe. Look for the **right type** at a **high enough concentration**.
- **Species and strain matter.** Beyond *Rhizophagus irregularis*, you'll see *Glomus mosseae*, *G. aggregatum*, *G. deserticola*, and others. Critically, the *same species* sourced from different places gives *different strains* with different tolerances for drought, flooding, and temperature. For example, the product DenoMyco isolated a resilient native Israeli strain effective across a wider pH range (per researcher Dr. Yoram Kapolnik of the Volcani Institute). Identical species names on two containers don't guarantee identical results.
- **Concentrated supply.** Few companies actually produce endomycorrhizal fungi commercially; most North American supply traces to a handful of producers, with everyone else repackaging.

## What Destroys Mycorrhizal Networks

- **Tilling/rototilling** — physically severs hyphal networks. The deeper and more aggressive, the worse the damage
- **Excess phosphorus or nitrogen** — prevents the plant from signaling for the relationship
- **Certain fungicides** — not all, but some are incompatible with mycorrhizal fungi
- **Removing and replacing soil** — discards the established network (see [[reusing-soil]])

## Taxonomy in Flux

Until recently, mycorrhizal fungi couldn't be reliably identified to species. Now that RNA and DNA testing is available, proper names are being assigned. Labels on commercial products may use outdated names for the next several years as taxonomy catches up. When in doubt, check current nomenclature online.

## Key Takeaways
- 96% of plants need mycorrhizal fungi — if you're not inoculating, your plants may be missing a critical partner
- The plant controls the relationship through chemical signaling — excess fertilizer shuts it down
- Inoculate as early as possible, directly at the root zone
- Protect existing networks by minimizing soil disturbance and avoiding excess P and N
- Glomalin from mycorrhizal fungi is a major source of soil carbon
