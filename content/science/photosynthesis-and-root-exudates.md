# Photosynthesis and Root Exudates

> **Quick take:** Plants convert sunlight into sugars through photosynthesis, then pump up to 40% of that carbon out through their roots as exudates — the currency that pays for everything the soil food web provides.

Every relationship in the [[soil-food-web|soil food web]] ultimately depends on one thing: the carbon that plants create through photosynthesis and deliberately release into the soil. This is not leakage or waste. Root exudates are a sophisticated investment — the plant's way of hiring, feeding, and managing an underground workforce of microbes that mine nutrients, suppress pathogens, and build soil structure.

## From Sunlight to Soil Currency

Photosynthesis is the process that makes all of this possible. In the leaves, chlorophyll captures light energy and uses it to combine carbon dioxide (CO2) from the air with water (H2O) to produce sugars (primarily glucose, C6H12O6) and oxygen. This is the only significant way that new carbon enters the biological economy of a garden.

What happens next is what matters for soil biology: the plant does not keep all of that carbon for itself. Research estimates that plants push roughly **40% of their photosynthetic carbon** out through their roots and into the surrounding soil. This is an enormous energy investment — nearly half of everything the plant produces goes underground.

Why would a plant give away so much of its hard-won carbon? Because the return on investment is substantial. That carbon purchases nutrient-mining services, disease suppression, and structural soil improvements that the plant cannot accomplish on its own.

## What Root Exudates Contain

Root exudates are not a single substance. They are a complex, dynamic mixture that the plant adjusts based on its needs. The major categories:

### Monosaccharides (Simple Sugars)

Glucose, fructose, and other simple sugars are the "fast cash" of the root zone. They are immediately available to any microbe that encounters them. These feed the general microbial community and attract organisms to the rhizosphere (the narrow zone of soil directly surrounding roots).

### Polysaccharides (Complex Sugars)

Longer-chain sugars like mucilage act more like a "savings account." They break down more slowly, providing sustained energy to microbial communities over time. Polysaccharides also have physical functions — mucilage lubricates the root tip as it pushes through soil and helps maintain moisture around the root surface.

### Amino Acids

Plants release amino acids — the building blocks of proteins — through their roots. These are high-value exudates that provide both carbon and nitrogen to soil microbes. Specific amino acids may serve as chemical signals to recruit particular microbial species.

### Organic Acids

Malic acid, citric acid, oxalic acid, and other organic acids are the "mineral dissolvers" of the exudate toolkit. They directly solubilize nutrients from soil mineral particles, making phosphorus, iron, and other elements available. Organic acids also lower the pH in the immediate root zone (even if bulk soil pH is higher), creating micro-environments where nutrient availability improves. This connects directly to how [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability|soil pH]] affects nutrient access.

## Selective Trading, Not Passive Leaking

One of the most important things to understand about root exudates is that the plant is not passively losing carbon. It is making targeted investments. Research shows that plants change the composition and quantity of their exudates in response to their nutritional status:

- A plant deficient in phosphorus may increase production of organic acids that recruit phosphorus-solubilizing bacteria and fungi.
- A plant under pathogen attack may release compounds that attract biocontrol organisms or trigger defensive responses in the microbial community.
- A plant with adequate nutrient supply may reduce exudation to conserve carbon for growth and reproduction.

[[Tad-hussey]] describes this as a "sophisticated solar-powered barter system." The plant is the only mint in town — the sole source of freshly fixed carbon — and it uses that monopoly position to manage its microbial partners.

## The Rhizosphere Effect

Root exudates create the rhizosphere: a zone extending just a few millimeters from the root surface where microbial populations are 10 to 1,000 times denser than in bulk soil. This concentration effect is why the biology around roots is so much more active than the biology between roots.

The rhizosphere is where the [[soil-food-web|food web]] is most intense. It is where the [[rhizophagy-cycle|rhizophagy cycle]] operates, where mycorrhizal fungi connect to root cells, and where predator-prey nutrient cycling delivers ionic nutrients directly to the root surface.

## Implications for Growers

Understanding root exudates changes how you think about plant nutrition and soil management:

- **Healthy leaves mean healthy roots mean healthy soil.** Anything that reduces photosynthesis — insufficient light, foliar disease, defoliation, environmental stress — reduces the carbon budget available for root exudation. A stressed plant sends less carbon underground, which means less microbial activity, which means fewer nutrients cycled back to the plant. This can create a downward spiral.
- **Carbon is the bottleneck.** In biological growing systems, the limiting factor for soil microbial activity is usually carbon, not the microbes themselves. This is why practices that maximize photosynthesis — adequate light, healthy leaf area, proper [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|watering]], balanced nutrition — indirectly boost the entire underground system.
- **Adding sugar to soil is counterproductive.** Some growers add molasses or sugar to the soil surface to "feed the biology." But exogenous sugar is intercepted within nanometers of the soil surface by whatever organisms happen to be closest — it does not reach the rhizosphere, and it can actually disrupt the natural trading relationships. As [[lee-breeze]] notes, adding sugar undermines the plant's incentive system for symbiotic nitrogen fixation because free-living bacteria get fed without providing anything in return.
- **Root zone disturbance matters.** Transplant shock, root pruning, compaction, and waterlogging all disrupt the exudate-driven economy. Gentle [[transplanting]] practices and maintaining good [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|soil structure]] preserve the microbial community that the plant has invested carbon in building.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis is a fast-growing, high-demand crop that relies heavily on its root zone biology, especially during the stretch phase (early flower) when nutrient uptake peaks. Maintaining maximum photosynthetic capacity — proper light intensity, healthy leaf canopy, controlled VPD — directly supports root exudation and the microbial partnerships it funds. In container systems, root zone management is critical: overwatering reduces oxygen and kills aerobic microbes; underwatering collapses the exudate-driven economy. The [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|air-water relationship]] in the root zone determines whether the carbon investment pays off. Growers who strip lower fan leaves aggressively to "redirect energy" may actually be reducing the plant's total photosynthetic capacity and, by extension, the carbon available for root exudation and microbial partnerships.

## Key Takeaways

- Plants invest roughly 40% of their photosynthetic carbon as root exudates — sugars, amino acids, and organic acids released into the soil
- Exudates are not passive leakage but a targeted investment to recruit and feed beneficial microbes
- The four major exudate types serve different functions: monosaccharides (fast energy), polysaccharides (sustained energy), amino acids (building blocks and signals), organic acids (mineral dissolvers)
- Plants adjust exudate composition based on their needs — more organic acids when phosphorus-deficient, different signals when under pathogen attack
- Anything that reduces photosynthesis reduces the entire underground economy
- Adding exogenous sugar to soil disrupts natural trading relationships and is counterproductive
