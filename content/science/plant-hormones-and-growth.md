# Plant Hormones and Growth

> **Quick take:** Plants do not "reach for the light" — auxin hormones accumulate on the shady side of a stem, making those cells elongate faster, which mechanically bends the plant toward brightness.

When a plant on your windowsill leans toward the glass, it is easy to imagine the plant is actively seeking light. The reality is more elegant and more mechanical. The plant is not making a decision — it is responding to a hormone gradient created by light itself. Understanding this hormone, auxin, unlocks much of what gardeners observe about plant shape, branching, and response to pruning.

[[dawn-marshall]] uses auxin biology as a gateway to teaching gardeners to think about the "why" behind techniques they already practice. Once you understand apical dominance, pruning and training stop being recipes and become logical responses to how the plant is wired.

## Auxins and Phototropism

**Auxin** is a plant hormone (specifically indole-3-acetic acid, or IAA, is the most common natural auxin) produced primarily at the **apical meristem** — the growing tip of each shoot. Auxin promotes cell elongation: cells exposed to higher auxin concentrations grow longer.

**Phototropism** — the bending of plants toward light — works through auxin redistribution:

1. Light strikes one side of the stem
2. Light degrades auxin on the illuminated side
3. Auxin accumulates on the shady side (it is not destroyed there)
4. Cells on the shady side elongate more than cells on the lit side
5. Unequal elongation bends the stem toward the light source

The plant is not "reaching" or "seeking." It is a differential growth response driven by hormone chemistry. The bending is purely mechanical — one side of the stem is literally growing longer than the other.

This mechanism explains a common observation in indoor growing: plants at the edges of a grow light canopy bend inward, toward the center of the light. The inner side of each stem receives more light (degrading auxin), while the outer side receives less (retaining auxin and elongating more). The result is that edge plants curve inward.

## Apical Dominance

Auxin does more than control bending. It also controls branching through a phenomenon called **apical dominance**.

The terminal bud (topmost growing point) of a shoot produces auxin that flows downward through the stem. This downward-flowing auxin **suppresses lateral buds** — the dormant growing points at each node along the stem. As long as the terminal bud is intact and producing auxin, the lateral buds remain inhibited and the plant grows primarily upward as a single dominant shoot.

This is why an unpruned plant tends to grow tall and narrow — the top bud is chemically telling every side bud to stay dormant.

### What Happens When You Remove the Tip

When you cut, pinch, or otherwise remove the apical meristem, you remove the source of suppressive auxin. The lateral buds below the cut are freed from inhibition and begin to grow. Each of these new shoots becomes its own branch with its own apical meristem, each producing auxin that suppresses the buds below it.

This is why:

- **Pinching once** converts a single leader into two or more branches
- **Pinching again** doubles the branch count each time (roughly — biology is messy)
- **Successive pinching** creates exponential branching, transforming a tall, narrow plant into a wide, bushy one

The same principle applies to [[pruning-and-training|point-of-origin pruning]]. Removing a branch at its junction with the main stem (a clean removal) eliminates that branch's meristem without stimulating excessive regrowth. Cutting a branch partway (leaving a stub with nodes) leaves lateral buds that will sprout — which is sometimes desired and sometimes not.

## Nodes and Internodes

Stems are structured as alternating **nodes** (where leaves, buds, and branches attach) and **internodes** (the stretches between nodes). Lateral buds sit at nodes, which is why branching happens at predictable locations after pruning.

Roots, by contrast, do **not** have this node/internode structure. A root can branch from any point where it is cut or damaged. This difference is the biological basis for root scoring at [[transplanting]] — cutting roots anywhere stimulates new branching and fresh [[root-hairs-and-nutrient-uptake|root hair]] growth.

## Seasonal Context

In perennial plants, auxin biology interacts with seasonal physiology. During spring, growth surges using energy reserves stored in the roots over winter. After fruit set (around late May in the Pacific Northwest), reserves are at their lowest — this is when fertilization has the greatest impact, not in early spring when the plant is running on stored energy.

Fall pruning is often cautioned against because it can stimulate new growth that is then damaged by winter cold. [[dawn-marshall]] is blunt about this concern, calling it overstated ("a crock"), though the underlying hormone biology is real — removing apical dominance in fall does release lateral buds. The practical question is whether the resulting growth has time to harden off before freezing temperatures arrive, which depends on climate and timing.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis growers routinely manipulate apical dominance, often without knowing the science behind what they are doing.
>
> **Topping** (cutting the main stem above a node) removes the apical meristem, releasing the lateral buds below. The natural "Christmas tree" shape — one dominant cola with smaller side branches — transforms into a "candelabra" with multiple roughly equal tops. Each topping approximately doubles the number of growing tips.
>
> **Indoor light edge effects** are explained directly by auxin phototropism. Plants at the perimeter of a grow light lean inward because the inner side of each stem receives more light (degrading auxin), while the outer side retains auxin and elongates more. This is not the plant "wanting" more light — it is a mechanical hormone response. Understanding this helps with canopy management and [[pruning-and-training|training]] decisions.
>
> Knowing that auxin flows downward from each growing tip also explains why low-stress training (LST) works: bending a branch horizontal reduces the auxin gradient that suppresses lower buds, encouraging them to grow upward as new tops. You are not fighting the plant — you are redirecting its own hormone signaling.

## Key Takeaways

- Auxin is produced at shoot tips and promotes cell elongation
- Phototropism works by light degrading auxin on the lit side, causing the shady side to grow longer and bend the stem toward light
- Apical dominance means the top bud chemically suppresses lateral buds via downward-flowing auxin
- Removing the top bud (topping, pinching) frees lateral buds to grow, creating branching
- Successive pinching creates exponential branching
- Stems have node/internode structure; roots do not — roots branch from any cut point
- Seasonal timing matters: spring growth uses stored reserves, fertilize after fruit set when reserves are lowest
