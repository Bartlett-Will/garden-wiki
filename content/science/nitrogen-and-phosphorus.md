# Nitrogen and Phosphorus

> **Quick take:** Plants can only absorb nitrogen as nitrate or ammonium and phosphorus as specific phosphate ions — no matter what you apply, biology and chemistry must convert it to these forms first.

Nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) are the two nutrients gardeners think about most, but how they actually enter a plant is less well understood. Whether you apply fish emulsion, synthetic urea, bone meal, compost, or let soil microbes do the work, the plant does not care about the source. It can only absorb specific ionic forms through its [[root-hairs-and-nutrient-uptake|root hairs]], and everything else is just a precursor waiting to be converted.

Understanding these forms — and the biological processes that produce them — is the foundation for making sense of fertilizer labels, soil test results, and the [[soil-food-web]] itself.

## Nitrogen: Two Usable Forms

Plants absorb nitrogen in only two forms:

- **Nitrate (NO3-)** — a negatively charged ion (anion)
- **Ammonium (NH4+)** — a positively charged ion (cation)

That is it. Organic nitrogen (in proteins, amino acids, chitin, urea) must be broken down by soil microbes into ammonium first (a process called **mineralization**), and ammonium can then be further converted to nitrate by nitrifying bacteria (a process called **nitrification**). Both pathways are mediated by the [[soil-food-web]].

The source of nitrogen — whether synthetic fertilizer, compost, fish hydrolysate, or atmospheric fixation — is irrelevant to the plant. By the time nitrogen reaches the [[root-hairs-and-nutrient-uptake|root hair]] exchange sites, it is either nitrate or ammonium. The plant cannot tell the difference between a nitrate ion released by a bacterium decomposing compost and one dissolved from a bag of calcium nitrate.

What does differ between sources is the **speed and pathway** of delivery. Synthetic nitrate is immediately available. Organic nitrogen requires microbial processing time. The [[soil-food-web]] delivers nitrogen "just in time" at the root tip through predator-prey cycling (protozoa and nematodes eating bacteria and excreting plant-available ammonium and nitrate), while synthetic sources flood the system all at once.

## Phosphorus: Two Usable Forms

Plants absorb phosphorus as specific phosphate ions:

- **H2PO4-** (dihydrogen phosphate) — dominant in slightly acidic soils
- **HPO4 2-** (hydrogen phosphate) — dominant in slightly alkaline soils

Like nitrogen, the source does not matter — rock phosphate, bone meal, manure, or synthetic triple superphosphate must all be converted to one of these ionic forms before the plant can use them. [[Soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability|Soil pH]] determines which form predominates and how much phosphorus is available versus locked up in insoluble compounds.

Phosphorus is notoriously immobile in soil. It does not move with water the way nitrate does. This means it must be placed near the root zone to be accessible, and mycorrhizal fungi play a major role in extending the plant's phosphorus-gathering reach far beyond what root hairs alone can cover. The [[soil-food-web]] is not optional for efficient phosphorus nutrition — it is the primary delivery mechanism in most soils.

### Phosphorus Defies the Soil Test

[[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]] calls phosphorus the **least accurate nutrient to manage from a soil test** ([[ep-108-understanding-living-soil-fertility|episode 108]]), precisely because its delivery is so biologically driven. If the soil holds a large mineralizable reservoir — compost, soft rock phosphate, bone meal — microbes mineralize phosphate and hand it to roots efficiently, so soluble phosphorus rarely accumulates. A new bagged mix might show 3–4 ppm on a saturated-paste test, but as soon as it's watered and planted that drops to **0.3–0.5 ppm** — and yet plants show no deficiency and yields stay strong. The numbers that *do* spike soluble P are tells of a specific input: phosphoric acid used to pH-down water, phosphoric-acid-stabilized fish hydrolysate, or high-phosphorus bat guano.

Because the paste number is so unreliable, Bryant weights the **standard (Mehlich-3) test** more heavily for phosphorus and manages it primarily off the **tissue test** — keeping leaf phosphorus in the sufficient range, ideally on the high end — while slowly building the soil reservoir until the standard test reads sufficient. The classic **Mulder's chart** antagonisms (high phosphorus suppressing iron and zinc uptake) appear quickly in crops like corn and soybean but, in his experience, **barely show in cannabis** — even elevated soil and tissue phosphorus rarely produces a clear zinc, iron, or manganese deficiency. In organic systems the more common phosphorus risk is the opposite end: manure-heavy programs pushing total phosphate high enough to suppress [[mycorrhizal-fungi|mycorrhizae]] (a possible calcium-phosphate precipitation issue with very soluble forms, less so with the rock and bone sources organic growers use).

## Symbiotic Nitrogen Fixation

Some plants — notably legumes (beans, peas, clover) — form partnerships with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (primarily *Rhizobium* species) that convert atmospheric nitrogen gas (N2) into ammonium inside specialized root structures called nodules.

This is not free nitrogen. The plant pays for it with carbon — photosynthetic sugars pumped to the bacteria via [[photosynthesis-and-root-exudates|root exudates]]. It is a trade: carbon for nitrogen. The bacteria get reliable energy, the plant gets nitrogen without depending on soil mineralization or external fertilizer.

This sugar trade has an important consequence: **excess available nitrogen in the soil disrupts fixation**. When nitrate or ammonium is already abundant, the plant reduces investment in its bacterial partners — why pay for nitrogen when it is free in the soil solution? The nodules shrink or become inactive. This is why heavy nitrogen fertilization of legume crops actually reduces their nitrogen-fixing capacity, which undermines one of the main reasons for including [[cover-crops-and-crop-rotation|cover crops and legumes]] in a rotation.

## C:N Ratios and Immobilization

The ratio of carbon to nitrogen (C:N ratio) in organic materials determines whether adding them to soil releases or locks up nitrogen.

Soil bacteria have a C:N ratio of roughly 5:1 to 8:1 — they are nitrogen-rich relative to most plant residues. When you add high-carbon materials (wood chips at ~400:1, straw at ~80:1, dried leaves at ~60:1), microbes need nitrogen to digest all that carbon. They pull available nitrogen from the soil to build their bodies, temporarily **immobilizing** it — locking it up in microbial biomass where plants cannot access it.

As [[tad-hussey]] notes, "you might think you have a deficiency when you actually just have a microbial workforce out-competing your plant." The nitrogen is not gone — it is in living microbial tissue — and it will be released later when those microbes die or are eaten by predators. But in the short term, plants can show nitrogen deficiency symptoms even in nitrogen-rich soil.

Materials with a C:N ratio below roughly 20:1 (like fresh grass clippings, manure, or finished compost) tend to release nitrogen. Above 20:1, immobilization dominates initially. This is why mixing high-carbon amendments with a nitrogen source (or composting them first) avoids temporary deficiencies.

Understanding C:N ratios connects directly to [[composting-and-organic-inputs]] — getting the ratio right is what makes composting work, and getting it wrong is what makes raw wood chips cause yellowing in plants.

## The Microbial Loop

The [[soil-food-web]] ties these concepts together. Bacteria and fungi mineralize organic nitrogen into ammonium. Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate. Mycorrhizal fungi extend the reach for phosphorus. And the predator-prey dynamics of the microbial loop (protozoa eating bacteria, nematodes eating fungi) release locked-up N and P back into plant-available forms.

As described in the [[rhizophagy-cycle]], some bacteria cycle directly through root tip cells, leaking nutrients including nitrogen directly into the plant. This is a more intimate delivery system than the bulk soil solution and may be especially important for nitrogen delivery in living soil systems.

## Managing Nitrogen in Heavy-Feeding Crops

[[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]] adds the agronomist's working view of nitrogen ([[ep-072-heavy-metals-cover-crops-and-water-ph|episode 72]]). Nitrogen and potassium are removed from soil in the largest quantities by heavy-yielding crops — leaf tissue targets for nitrogen run **3.5-5% of dry weight** (only calcium reaches that range) — so they deplete first and must be replenished round after round. Compost is his preferred slow-release source for both, applied a little every cycle like deposits in a bank account ([[composting-and-organic-inputs]]).

**The gas tank model.** On a soil test, "available nitrogen" is ammonium plus nitrate. Once organic amendments have fully mineralized, ammonium should sit below 1-2 ppm — so the **nitrate number is the gauge**. But it's a fragile gauge: nitrate is extremely mobile (a single overwatering flushes it) and ephemeral, bouncing constantly. The fluctuation explains why nitrate varies more between beds than any other test value.

**The Goldilocks problem.** Too much nitrogen invites pests, fungal pressure, reduced flavor and aroma, and lower flower density; but the moment a plant runs out, metabolic functions stop — growth and flower development stall. Consistently high-yielding growers run nitrogen slightly *low* and feed soluble nitrogen continuously, letting levels draw down only late in flower (the plant translocates N from older leaves to growing tissue).

**Amino acid nitrogen.** In the leaf, nitrate must be converted into complete proteins via the nitrate reductase pathway (with molybdenum as enzyme cofactor) — a process consuming on the order of **18% of the plant's photosynthetic energy**. Feeding soluble amino acids (commonly derived from soybean meal, or fish aminos) supplies the protein building blocks directly, sparing that energy for growth and root exudation.

> **Evolving view (episode 108).** By the time of [[ep-108-understanding-living-soil-fertility|episode 108]], Bryant had revised his thinking on amino acids. The metabolic-savings story is *theoretically* true, but reviewing the uptake research he no longer believes plants absorb amino acids directly in significant quantity: **microbes feed first**, converting amino acids to ammonium and then nitrate, which the plant takes up and reassembles into amino acids anyway. So he now values amino-acid amendments for their **small particle size and fast availability** (better day-to-day nitrogen control), not a metabolic shortcut. The broader reframing: *all* organic amendments are protein/amino-based and are enzymatically broken down to amino acids — alfalfa meal and soybean meal are "amino" sources too, just at larger particle sizes that take longer to break down. He now thinks more about **particle size** than about whether a label says "amino acids."

**Letting the soil cook.** When a large dose of organic amendments is applied, the microbial bloom that breaks them down consumes a tremendous amount of **oxygen** and parks the nitrogen as **ammonium** before nitrifiers convert it to nitrate — and ammonium is a poor primary nitrogen source for young plants. This is the mechanism behind the old advice to let freshly amended soil "cook" for a few weeks before planting (it overlaps with the thermophilic, exothermic microbial activity that also raises soil temperature and can stress roots). Bryant's preferred way out is to **never deplete the soil so far** that you must dump huge amendment quantities: top-dress or feed nitrogen as the plant needs it, and use **larger soil volumes** (beds over small pots), which need less amendment per unit volume on re-amend and don't overheat or go anaerobic ([[reusing-soil]], [[composting-and-organic-inputs]]).

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Understanding the ionic forms of N and P matters for cannabis fertigation (fertilizer delivered through irrigation water). Whether using organic or synthetic inputs:
>
> - The plant absorbs nitrate and ammonium regardless of source label claims
> - Organic inputs require microbial processing time — they are not immediately available
> - In soilless and hydroponic systems, pH management directly controls which phosphate form is available ([[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]])
> - High-carbon amendments (like raw wood chips in a soil mix) can immobilize nitrogen and cause apparent deficiency — this is microbial competition, not a missing input
> - [[Soil-testing-and-diagnosis|Soil and sap testing]] can distinguish between genuine nitrogen deficiency and immobilization by the microbial community
>
> For living soil cannabis growers, understanding the [[soil-food-web]] nutrient cycling pathway — from organic inputs through microbial mineralization to ionic uptake — is what separates intentional growing from guesswork.
>
> Bryant Mason's working targets for cannabis flowering soil: roughly **75 ppm nitrate** (about 150 lb/acre, with adjusted math for container volumes) going into flower, drawing down to **20-30 ppm** by harvest — never fully deficient, never high enough to hurt flower quality. He pairs this with potassium fed through flower: potassium and calcium are antagonistic in uptake and translocation, so load **calcium in veg, potassium in flower**. Potassium sulfate and potassium silicate are both highly soluble (~420 g/gal vs. gypsum's ~8 g/gal) — easy to drench, but equally easy to leach.

## Key Takeaways

- Plants absorb nitrogen only as nitrate (NO3-) or ammonium (NH4+) — source is irrelevant
- Plants absorb phosphorus only as H2PO4- or HPO4 2- — source is irrelevant
- Symbiotic nitrogen fixation is a carbon-for-nitrogen trade between plants and bacteria
- Excess soil nitrogen suppresses biological fixation — over-fertilizing legumes defeats their purpose
- High C:N ratio materials cause temporary nitrogen immobilization as microbes out-compete plants
- The microbial loop (predator-prey cycling) releases immobilized nutrients back to plant-available forms
- Phosphorus is immobile in soil — mycorrhizal fungi are the primary extended delivery system
- Nitrogen and potassium deplete first in heavy-yielding systems — replenish every round, compost as the slow-release bank
- The nitrate number is the gas tank gauge, but it's mobile and ephemeral — one overwatering flushes it
- Amino acid nitrogen spares the ~18% energy of nitrate conversion *in theory* — but Bryant's later view (ep-108) is that microbes convert most amino acids to nitrate first, so the real benefit is particle size and fast availability
- Load calcium in veg, feed potassium through flower — the two are antagonistic in uptake
- Phosphorus is the least soil-test-predictable nutrient — manage it off tissue tests; 0.3-0.5 ppm soluble P is normal and not a deficiency
- Mulder's-chart P antagonisms (suppressing iron, zinc) show fast in corn/soy but barely in cannabis
- Big amendment dumps consume oxygen and stall nitrogen at ammonium — "let the soil cook," or better, never deplete enough to need a dump
