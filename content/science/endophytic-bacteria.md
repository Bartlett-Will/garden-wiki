# Endophytic Bacteria

> **Quick take:** Endophytes are microbes that live *inside* a plant for part of their lives — and far from being invaders, many of them feed the plant nutrients, manufacture its hormones, and even fix nitrogen straight out of the air.

An **endophyte** is any organism that lives inside plant tissue in a relationship that benefits both partners (an endosymbiont). Most attention goes to endophytic bacteria, but fungi, archaea, viruses, and micro-eukaryotes can all live inside a plant too. As [[jeff-lowenfels]] puts it in his book *Teaming with Bacteria*, a plant is not "existing in a vacuum" — its interior is a populated, working ecosystem.

This is a relatively new frontier. The classic [[soil-food-web]] model — codified from [[dr-elaine-ingham]]'s work and popularized in Lowenfels' 2006 *Teaming with Microbes* — described nutrients being traded in the soil *around* roots. Endophytes add an entirely internal layer to that picture, and much of the detail comes from [[dr-james-white]]'s research at Rutgers.

## How Bacteria Get Inside

The [[rhizophagy-cycle]] is the best-characterized entry route, but it is not the only one. Endophytic bacteria also get in by:

- **Root branching points** — as the root system branches, small cracks open and bacteria slip through.
- **Stomata** — the leaf pores used for gas exchange.
- **Wounds and growth holes** — physical openings in the plant surface.

Once inside, they travel and live in the plant's tissues, often in the spaces between cells.

## What Endophytes Do for the Plant

The reason a plant tolerates — even invites — bacteria that it would normally defend against is that the bacteria pay rent in several currencies:

**Nutrients.** When the plant strips a bacterium's cell wall (see the [[rhizophagy-cycle]]), the wall material is absorbed as plant nutrition. Depending on whether the bacterium is gram-positive or gram-negative, that material includes amino acids, micronutrients, and metals. Six to eight or more nutrients can enter the plant this way.

**Phytohormones.** Endophytic bacteria manufacture real plant hormones — not mimics. They produce auxins, gibberellic acid, ethylene, and several others (six or seven phytohormones in total). These let the bacteria influence plant growth and development directly. Ethylene in particular drives the root-hair growth that is central to the [[rhizophagy-cycle]]. See [[plant-hormones-and-growth]] for what these hormones do.

**Nitrogen fixation.** Some endophytes pull nitrogen straight out of the air and fix it into plant-usable forms — internal nitrogen fixation. This is distinct from the classic *Rhizobium*/legume nodule system, though Lowenfels notes a loose parallel: in both cases a bacterium ends up inside the plant, in a low-oxygen pocket, fixing atmospheric nitrogen. Achieving internal nitrogen fixation in major crops like wheat is, in his words, "the holy grail of agriculture" — and endophytes are a leading route toward it.

## Strain Specificity: A Key Difference from Fungi

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are generalists — a single fungal species can colonize many different plant species (Lowenfels jokes that they "sleep around"). Endophytic bacteria are different. The right bacterium for a strawberry may do nothing for wheat or an apple tree. The pool of bacteria *in the soil* tends to look similar everywhere, but once inside a plant, the endophytic community is strain-specific to the host.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> This strain specificity is striking in cannabis. Studies Lowenfels cites show that while the soil-side pool of potential endophytes is broadly similar, the bacteria that actually establish *inside* a cannabis plant differ from cultivar to cultivar — Girl Scout Cookies hosts a different endophytic community than another strain. Lowenfels calls the endophyte community "the terroir we have been looking for" in cannabis.
>
> Even more remarkable is what may be happening in the **glandular trichomes**. A paper by [[dr-james-white]] proposes that bacteria live inside trichomes and fix nitrogen there. The trichomes provide the required low-oxygen environment by *bombarding the bacteria with cannabinoids* — and the cannabinoids that growers prize are, under this theory, a byproduct of that nitrogen-fixation process. The science is new and still developing, but the implication is that cannabinoid production and the plant's internal microbiome may be linked.

## Seed Transmission: How Endophytes Persist

Endophytes carry over from one plant generation to the next through the **seed**. As a flower forms around the embryo, bacteria get caught up in it — some in the seed coat, some just inside it, some right up against (or within) the embryo itself. When the seed germinates, these bacteria emerge into the soil and seed the rhizophagy and endophytic communities for the new plant.

This has a direct practical warning: **sterilizing seeds strips out beneficial endophytes.** Growers who surface-sterilize cannabis seeds may be removing organisms the plant needs, which Lowenfels suggests could contribute to later problems like powdery mildew. The same logic extends to the paper-towel germination method — the bacteria that jump off the seed onto the towel are worth keeping, so the towel fragment can be planted along with the seedling.

## Why a Plant Allows It

A bacterium is often a plant's enemy, and plants have defenses to keep them out. Endophytes are tolerated because the relationship is mutualistic: the plant gains nutrients, hormones, and sometimes fixed nitrogen, while the bacterium gains a protected place to live and food. The plant also retains control — the same superoxide spray it uses to strip bacterial walls in the [[rhizophagy-cycle]] is a defensive tool, and the plant strengthens its own tissues to survive deploying it.

## Key Takeaways

- Endophytes live inside plant tissue in a mutualistic relationship; bacteria are the best-studied, but fungi, archaea, viruses, and micro-eukaryotes also qualify
- Entry routes include the [[rhizophagy-cycle]], root-branch cracks, stomata, and wounds
- Endophytic bacteria supply nutrients (from stripped cell walls), manufacture real phytohormones (auxins, gibberellic acid, ethylene, and others), and some fix atmospheric nitrogen internally
- Unlike generalist [[mycorrhizal-fungi]], endophytic bacteria are strain-specific to their host plant
- Endophytes pass between generations via the seed — sterilizing seeds removes beneficial organisms
- In cannabis, endophyte communities differ by cultivar, and a leading theory links trichome cannabinoid production to internal nitrogen fixation by trichome-dwelling bacteria
- Internal nitrogen fixation in major crops is considered "the holy grail of agriculture"
