# Water Chemistry and Alkalinity

> **Quick take:** The pH number on your water test matters far less than its alkalinity — a low-alkalinity water at pH 8 is harmless, while a high-alkalinity water at pH 6.9 will slowly push your soil pH up.

Most growers know to check the pH of their irrigation water. Far fewer understand **alkalinity**, which is the number that actually predicts what the water will do to your soil. As [[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]] explains, three related metrics on a water test — pH, alkalinity, and bicarbonates — tell a story together, and reading them correctly takes the mystery (and snake oil) out of water management.

## The Three Metrics

**pH** is, by definition, the hydrogen ion (H+) concentration of the water. Anything that contributes hydrogen ions is acidic and lowers pH; anything that consumes them raises it. That is the entire foundation — any product claim that doesn't reduce to hydrogen ion chemistry deserves skepticism.

**Alkalinity** measures something different: how much acid it would take to lower the pH — in other words, how *resistant* the water is to pH change. Two waters can have identical pH readings but wildly different alkalinity, and they will behave completely differently in your soil.

**Bicarbonates** are the usual cause of high alkalinity. As water moves through soil parent material it picks up carbonates — calcium carbonate or magnesium carbonate, also called free lime — which dissolve into solution and convert to bicarbonate. More bicarbonate means higher alkalinity and usually higher pH.

## Why Alkalinity Beats pH

When water enters soil, the soil buffers its pH. The question is who wins:

- **Low-alkalinity water** is buffered almost instantly. Even at pH 8.0, it reverts to the soil's pH as soon as it hits the root zone. The high pH reading was never a real problem.
- **High-alkalinity water** resists buffering. Even at an innocent-looking pH of 6.9, it will gradually drag the soil pH upward, watering after watering.

So high alkalinity — not high pH alone — is the real indicator that you should adjust your water. This inverts how most growers think about their water test.

## When and How to Acidify

Bryant's rule of thumb: when total alkalinity exceeds about **120 ppm**, start considering acid injection; above 150-160 ppm, definitely consider it. The threshold is somewhat arbitrary — the alternative is running your soil pH a bit lower so the combination lands in range.

The right way to evaluate the combination is a **saturated paste test run with your own irrigation water** rather than distilled water — it shows what your roots actually experience. High-pH, high-alkalinity water applied to a low-pH soil can combine to a perfectly in-range solution ([[soil-testing-and-diagnosis]]). Skip shipping water to the lab if you use RO or most municipal water — they're too clean to matter.

Adding acid dissociates the bicarbonate, lowering both alkalinity and pH. The three common acids trade off differently:

- **Citric acid** — the only one allowed in certified organic production. Weak buffering: bacteria consume the conjugate base, so pH bounces back within a day. More expensive, requires more product, doesn't shift soil pH durably.
- **Phosphoric acid** — [[tad-hussey|Tad's]] go-to and Bryant's favorite for value: powerful, stable, inexpensive, and the phosphorus is itself soluble fertility that cannabis loves. Caveat from topsoil research: high soluble phosphorus suppresses [[mycorrhizal-fungi|mycorrhizal colonization]], since phosphorus delivery is the fungi's core service.
- **Sulfuric acid** — Bryant recommends it (often via a sulfur burner) for orchardists on calcareous, high-pH soils where amending the soil itself is hopeless — you manage the soil *solution* instead. Tad avoids it over reports of harm to soil microorganisms; Bryant notes he hasn't seen strong research support for that concern and treats it as an open question.

Tad has also seen repeated low-dose applications of agricultural elemental sulfur successfully lower soil pH over cycles in facilities battling high pH, with yield improvements — consistent with nutrients becoming easier to access as pH enters the optimal range ([[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]]).

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Indoor cannabis facilities watering daily with high-alkalinity well or municipal water are slowly liming their beds whether they know it or not — drifting soil pH up cycle after cycle until micronutrient lockout appears. Test your source water's alkalinity, not just its pH. If total alkalinity exceeds ~120 ppm, inject acid (phosphoric is the common choice in flower, with the mycorrhizae caveat for living-soil systems) or run your media pH lower to compensate. Send four ounces of your irrigation water with your soil test so the saturated paste reflects reality. And be cautious with RO water for a different reason: ultra-pure water aggressively strips whatever it contacts, including metals from pumps and plumbing — see [[heavy-metals-management]].

## Key Takeaways

- pH is hydrogen ion concentration; alkalinity is resistance to pH change; bicarbonates cause high alkalinity
- Low-alkalinity water buffers to soil pH immediately — its own pH reading barely matters
- High-alkalinity water changes your soil pH over time, even at a near-neutral pH
- Acidify when total alkalinity exceeds ~120 ppm, or run your soil pH lower to compensate
- Run your saturated paste test with your actual irrigation water (unless it's RO/municipal-clean)
- Citric is organic-certified but weak and unstable; phosphoric is powerful and feeds P but can suppress mycorrhizae; sulfuric suits calcareous-soil solution management
- Acid works by dissociating bicarbonate — lowering alkalinity and pH together
