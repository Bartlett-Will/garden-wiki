# Soil pH and Nutrient Availability

> **Quick take:** Soil pH is the master switch that determines which nutrients your plants can actually access — get it wrong and no amount of fertilizer or biology will compensate.

> [!info] Conflicting views: should you correct high-pH irrigation water?
> **Tad Hussey / Jeff Lowenfels** (ep-050) give a flat rule: source water at pH 7.5+ should be acidified (Tad uses citric acid). **Bryant Mason** (ep-072), on [[water-chemistry-and-alkalinity]], counters that water pH itself is nearly irrelevant — what matters is *alkalinity*; low-alkalinity water at pH 8.0 reverts to the soil's pH at the root zone and needs no correction. Alkalinity, not the pH reading, is the real trigger.

Of all the numbers on a soil test, pH is the one that [[lee-breeze]] calls "the overriding factor of nutrient availability." You can have abundant phosphorus, zinc, iron, and manganese in your soil, but if the pH is outside the right range, those nutrients become chemically locked up — present but unavailable. Understanding pH is not optional for serious growers. It is the foundation that everything else builds on.

## What pH Actually Measures

pH is a measure of hydrogen ion (H+) concentration in the soil solution. The scale runs from 0 (extremely acidic) to 14 (extremely alkaline), with 7 being neutral. But here is the critical detail that many growers miss: **the pH scale is logarithmic, not linear.** Each whole number represents a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. A soil at pH 5 is ten times more acidic than one at pH 6, and one hundred times more acidic than one at pH 7.

This means that small changes in pH numbers represent large changes in soil chemistry. Moving from 6.5 to 5.5 is not a minor shift — it is a ten-fold increase in acidity that can fundamentally alter which nutrients are available and which become toxic.

## Optimal Ranges

For most garden crops, the sweet spot is **pH 5.8 to 6.4**, with 6.2 often cited as ideal. In this range, the maximum number of essential nutrients are simultaneously available in the soil solution.

Here is what happens at the extremes:

### Below pH 5: Aluminum Toxicity Territory

In strongly acidic soils, aluminum — which is abundant in most soils but normally locked up in mineral structures — dissolves into the soil solution. Soluble aluminum is toxic to plant roots. It stunts root growth, inhibits water and nutrient uptake, and can kill plants outright. This is one of the most common problems in naturally acidic soils and is the primary reason why liming (adding calcium carbonate) is a standard agricultural practice.

At low pH, manganese and iron can also reach toxic concentrations, while phosphorus, calcium, and magnesium become increasingly unavailable.

### Above pH 7: Lockup Zone

In alkaline soils, calcium dominates the chemistry. It reacts with phosphorus to form insoluble calcium phosphate compounds, effectively locking up P even when soil tests show adequate total phosphorus. Zinc, iron, manganese, and copper also become increasingly unavailable as pH rises above 7.

This is why high-pH soils often show micronutrient deficiencies even when total nutrient levels are adequate. The nutrients are there — they are just chemically inaccessible. [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis|Soil testing]] will show the total amount, but the plant cannot access it.

### The Blueberry Example

Some crops have evolved for acidic conditions and need pH well below the standard range. Blueberries, for instance, thrive at pH 4.5 to 5.5 — a range that would cause serious problems for most vegetables. This is not arbitrary; blueberries have adapted their root chemistry to function in acidic soils where aluminum is soluble and iron is readily available. Trying to grow blueberries at pH 6.5 typically results in iron chlorosis (yellowing leaves) because the iron they need becomes locked up at that pH.

[[dawn-marshall|Don Marshall]] tells this as a cautionary story: he planted a blueberry into alkaline biochar-amended soil running close to pH 7.0, and it barely grew for two years despite the soil being rich in nutrients. The plant was "working really hard just to stay alive" because everything it needed was present but unavailable. The fix is not more fertilizer — it is the right pH. (Don also notes blueberries are a **bog plant**: they want acidity *and* high moisture *and* high oxygen at the roots, which is why they fail in heavy clay even at the correct pH — see [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|the air-water relationship]].)

### Presence Is Not Availability: Reading the Field

The recurring lesson is that **presence is not the same as availability** — and the symptoms can fool you into fertilizing when you shouldn't. Two field cues [[dawn-marshall|Don Marshall]] uses:

- **Winter nitrate "deficiency" that isn't.** A homeowner tests a Pacific Northwest lawn in the rainy winter, sees nitrate reading near zero, and concludes the lawn is starved. But nitrate (NO3-) is an **anion** that does not bind to soil and **leaches away with groundwater** — there is little present in winter no matter how fertile the soil. Applying nitrogen then just washes it into the watershed. The nitrate returns in summer as microbes convert ammonium from organic matter. Test and feed on the plant's schedule, not the calendar's.
- **Moss as a diagnostic, not a shade problem.** Gardeners blame moss on shade and reach for moss-killer, but moss thrives in full sun too (you see it on roofs). Moss indicates **low pH, poor nutrition, compaction, and excess water** — conditions where grass can't outcompete it. Kill the moss without fixing those, and it returns. The moss is the symptom; the soil condition is the cause.

## Buffer pH: The Resistance Factor

Your soil test may include a second pH number called **buffer pH** (sometimes labeled "buffer index" or "lime requirement"). This measures the soil's resistance to pH change — essentially, how much amendment it would take to move the pH.

Buffer pH depends on the soil's cation exchange capacity ([[soil-structure-and-aggregates|CEC]]) and the types of minerals present. A heavy clay soil or one high in organic matter has a high buffering capacity. This means:

- It resists pH change in both directions (an advantage for stability)
- But when pH correction is needed, it requires enormous quantities of amendments

[[Lee-breeze]] describes cases where high-pH prairie soils (pH 8.5+) would need 5 tons of elemental sulfur per acre to shift the pH, and the soil bounces back within a few years. In those situations, it is often more practical to grow pH-tolerant crops or use chelated micronutrient applications than to fight the soil's chemistry. Chelation — wrapping a nutrient ion in an organic molecule to prevent it from being locked up — is an effective short-term solution for high-pH soils, particularly for zinc and iron. See [[nitrogen-and-phosphorus]] for more on nutrient forms and availability.

## The "Organics Buffer" Illusion

One of the most persistent myths in biological growing is that "pH does not matter if your biology is right." [[Lee-breeze]] is direct about this: pH is THE factor, and microbes do not override it.

So why does this myth persist? Because organic systems often naturally buffer into favorable pH ranges. Here is why:

- Organic matter decomposition produces organic acids that gently lower pH in alkaline soils
- Compost and other high-OM amendments have high CEC, which stabilizes pH
- Active microbial communities produce CO2, which dissolves in water to form weak carbonic acid
- Well-managed organic soils tend to accumulate in the 6.0-6.5 range naturally

The result is that many organic growers never encounter pH problems — and conclude that biology is managing pH for them. What is actually happening is that their management practices (adding compost, maintaining organic matter, fostering biology) happen to create conditions that keep pH favorable. The biology is not overriding chemistry; it is incidentally producing chemistry-friendly conditions.

The illusion breaks down when growers encounter truly problematic soils: high-pH calcareous soils, heavily acidified soils, or soilless media where organic matter buffering is minimal. In those situations, pH must be managed directly through amendments (lime for acid soils, sulfur or acidifying fertilizers for alkaline soils).

### pH Drives the Biology, Not the Other Way Around

There's a stronger version of this point, and [[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]] makes it with data ([[ep-072-heavy-metals-cover-crops-and-water-ph|episode 72]]): a 2020 meta-analysis of more than 1,200 experiments examining soil microbial diversity found that **species diversity is explained by pH more than by any other factor**. The causation runs from chemistry to biology — long-term microbial community composition is dictated by physical and chemical conditions, pH first among them. This is the scientific basis for Bryant's management philosophy: optimize pH (and the other physical/chemical conditions), promote rather than suppress biological activity, and let the community composition fall into place. The usual caveat applies: the research base is topsoil, and heavily-amended living soil systems may behave differently ([[soilless-media-vs-topsoil]]).

Note also that your irrigation water can quietly move soil pH: what matters is not the water's pH reading but its **alkalinity** (bicarbonate content). High-alkalinity water resists buffering and limes your soil a little with every watering — see [[water-chemistry-and-alkalinity]].

### How Far Does Microbial Buffering Actually Go?

[[jeff-lowenfels]] and [[tad-hussey]] put numbers and boundaries on this in [[ep-050-jeff-lowenfels-returns|episode 50]]. Tad's estimate: microbial buffering lets growers "get away with it" perhaps 75–80% of the time — but not always, and not against extreme inputs. Jeff's framing: **pH only counts where the roots are**. The rhizosphere is where pH gates nutrient uptake (and affects the bacteria, fungi, and other organisms doing the work); surface readings where no roots live are noise. But a natural system buffering itself is different from you adding an outside substance at pH 8+ — "the plant will take care of itself" was never a license to irrigate with whatever comes out of the tap.

Practical boundaries from that conversation:

- **Source water at 7.5+ should be corrected** (Tad uses citric acid) rather than left to the microbes.
- **Well water fluctuates seasonally** — test it through the year, not once.
- Jeff doesn't test pH routinely in a healthy garden, but when a plant looks wrong, **pH is the first thing he checks** — it's among the easiest variables to rule out or fix.
- Commercial growers should anchor on a baseline soil test (Mehlich 3 plus saturated paste) and **track trends with the same methods every time**, treating no single number as scripture.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> For cannabis in container/potting soil systems, [[tad-hussey]] recommends a target pH of **6.6 to 6.8** — slightly higher than the field crop ideal of 6.2. This is because many common organic amendments in potting soils are alkaline: biochar, calcium-rich amendments (oyster shell flour, calcium carbonate), and some composts. Starting at 6.6-6.8 allows room for the natural acidification that occurs as organic matter decomposes and roots release organic acids, keeping the media in the favorable range throughout the grow cycle rather than dropping too low. Note that standard agricultural soil test calibrations may not apply directly to soilless potting media — the chemistry of peat-based or coir-based mixes behaves differently from mineral field soils, and some labs offer specific soilless media testing protocols.

## Practical pH Management

### Testing

Always test pH before making amendments. A standard [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis|soil test]] provides both pH and buffer pH. For container growers, testing the pH of runoff water or using a soil slurry method can provide useful monitoring between full lab tests.

### Raising pH (Acidic Soils)

- **Calcitic lime** (calcium carbonate): the standard amendment. Slow-acting, lasts 2-3 years.
- **Dolomitic lime**: adds magnesium alongside calcium. Use only if magnesium is also needed — otherwise it can create imbalances.
- **Wood ash**: fast-acting but inconsistent and can over-correct quickly. Use cautiously.

### Lowering pH (Alkaline Soils)

- **Elemental sulfur**: soil bacteria oxidize it to sulfuric acid over weeks to months. Effective but slow.
- **Acidifying fertilizers** (ammonium sulfate, etc.): provide both nutrients and acidification.
- **For container systems**: adjusting irrigation water pH is often more practical than amending the media.

### When Not to Fight pH

In some situations — particularly high-pH soils with enormous buffering capacity — the most practical approach is to work with the pH rather than against it. This means choosing crops adapted to the existing pH, using chelated micronutrient applications where needed, and accepting that the soil's chemistry is a given rather than a variable.

## Key Takeaways

- pH is the single most important factor controlling nutrient availability in soil
- The scale is logarithmic: each whole number is a tenfold change in acidity
- Optimal range for most crops is pH 5.8-6.4; below 5 risks aluminum toxicity, above 7 locks up phosphorus and micronutrients
- Buffer pH tells you how much amendment is needed to change pH — high-CEC soils resist change
- Organic systems often naturally buffer to favorable pH, creating the illusion that "biology handles pH"
- Some soils are more practical to work with than to fight — grow tolerant crops or use chelation
