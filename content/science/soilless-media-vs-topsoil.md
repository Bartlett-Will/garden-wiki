# Soilless Media vs. Topsoil

> **Quick take:** Most soil science was developed in mineral topsoil with clay — and a peat-or-coir container mix has no clay, so the familiar numbers (CEC, organic matter) mean something different and the rules must be reinterpreted.

A surprising amount of grower confusion traces back to one fact: classical agronomy was built on **topsoil** — real mineral soil composed of sand, silt, and clay, homogenized over eons — while most container growing happens in **soilless media** built from peat, coir, bark, compost, and aeration amendments. As [[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]] emphasizes, the science still applies, but the interpretation changes.

## The Structural Differences

**No clay colloid.** Topsoil's nutrient-holding power lives largely on clay particles — the mineral colloid whose negative charges hold cation nutrients. Soilless media has none. Whatever cation exchange capacity it has comes from **organic matter** instead.

**Heterogeneous, coarse particles.** Topsoil has been mixed for eons into relative homogeneity with fine particles. Soilless media is chunky and variable — larger particle sizes, less uniform.

**More pore space, faster drainage.** Container media is engineered for air. It drains quickly and holds nutrients weakly, which is exactly what makes it productive under heavy irrigation — and exactly what makes topsoil rules misleading in it ([[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship]]).

## Reinterpreting the Soil Test

Because of these differences ([[soil-testing-and-diagnosis]]):

- **CEC** on a soilless media test does not mean what it means in a field — there's no clay colloid to balance. Bryant thinks in terms of **solubility and the soil solution** rather than base-cation balancing of the colloid.
- **Organic matter %** reads absurdly high by topsoil standards (the medium *is* organic matter) and must be interpreted on its own scale.
- **Saturated paste extract** — measuring what's dissolved in the root-zone solution — is more meaningful than standard dry-soil extractions, ideally run with your own irrigation water ([[water-chemistry-and-alkalinity]]).
- Even agronomic units wobble: pounds-per-acre conversions were defined for field topsoil and need adjusted math when applied to cubic yards of container mix.

### CEC is a calculation, not a measurement

In [[ep-108-understanding-living-soil-fertility|episode 108]] Bryant makes the mechanics explicit. A lab measures calcium, magnesium, zinc, and so on directly (by ICP), but **CEC is not measured** — it is *calculated* by summing the Mehlich-3-extracted cations (calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium, with small adjustments for other bases and hydrogen). In a real acidic mineral topsoil, where excess unbound ions have leached away and the remaining cations sit on clay's negatively charged "docking sites" (the classic dinner-plate / sponge analogy), that sum is a fair estimate of how many exchange sites exist. In peat-based media there are **no docking sites** — the cations float in compost, minerals, and the matrix — so the "CEC" reading is really just an EC-style tally of the cations present.

This creates a dangerous **positive feedback loop**: traditional agronomy applies *more* nutrients to a higher-CEC soil, so a peat mix that reads high CEC (because it's freshly loaded with cations) invites still more application, and you over-amend. A high-CEC mineral topsoil that is genuinely low in nutrients will instead be acidic, because hydrogen ions occupy the empty sites — a tell that doesn't exist in near-neutral peat media. The cation levels also tend to be *correlated* (gypsum brings calcium + sulfur, compost brings everything at once), which is why high CEC coincides with high phosphate and sulfur — coincidence of co-application, not a mathematical link, since phosphate is an anion and never enters the CEC sum.

### Organic matter: loss on ignition measures the wrong carbon

Organic matter *is* directly measured, by **loss on ignition** — the sample is weighed, heated to ~800°F until its carbon (organic matter is ~58% carbon) oxidizes off, and reweighed. The technique is cheap and accurate enough for topsoil, where it captures recalcitrant long-chain humus — the "magical jet fuel" that resists decomposition and builds diverse microbial habitat. In peat media it captures the **wrong carbon**: peat moss, coir, and immature compost are carbon-based but are not organic matter in the agronomic sense (low-nutrient, fast-cycling, not recalcitrant). Worse, the reading is **highly correlated to bulk density**, so a fluffy new mix shows high OM and high CEC straight out of the bag, then both numbers jump around as the media settles (the classic 2–4 inch sink in the first round). The deciding problem, though, is **actionability**: there is no management action you can take from a 30% vs 40% OM reading on a container test, and the purpose of paying for a test is to decide what to apply. Bryant's verdict: **ignore OM% on soilless media**; even outdoors, treat it as a 5–10 year trend, not a year-to-year number, since a heavy compost load inflates it without building true stable organic matter.

### Exchangeable hydrogen is just pH restated

The percent-exchangeable-hydrogen line on a standard test is **100% correlated to pH** — zero above pH 7, ticking up below it — and carries no independent information. Bryant considers it a source of confusion and has asked labs to drop it.

## Nutrient Mining: No Closed System

Every harvest exports nutrients and organic matter off-site. A field topsoil holds a deep mineral reserve — theoretically hundreds of harvests — but even there, heavy-yielding crops deplete **nitrogen and potassium** fastest. A container holds no such reserve: "there's just no way" the nutrient stock in soilless media sustains round after round of heavy cropping without replenishment ([[nitrogen-and-phosphorus]], [[reusing-soil]]). This is the core argument against "all soils have sufficient minerals, just fix the biology": perhaps debatable in some topsoils, simply false in a pot.

## How Living Soil Beds Age

Reused beds change physically over rounds. Organic matter mineralizes and **bulk density rises**; worms move in and by round 10-20 the bed has substantially converted to worm castings — wonderful biologically ([[vermicomposting]]), but denser, with different water-holding and drainage behavior. The fix: re-add **aggregate/aeration** as you re-amend, or adjust irrigation to match the heavier media ([[reusing-soil]], [[soil-structure-and-aggregates]]).

## What Still Transfers

The three-pillar triangle — physical, chemical, biological, all interrelated, every action touching all three — applies in a container as much as a field ([[soil-food-web]]). pH still gates nutrient availability ([[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]]), though some topsoil findings (like high soluble phosphorus suppressing [[mycorrhizal-fungi|mycorrhizae]], or pH driving microbial diversity) carry an honest asterisk: the research was done in topsoil and may transfer imperfectly to soilless systems.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Most living-soil cannabis is grown in exactly this gap — soilless media managed with topsoil intuitions. Practical translations: get your media tested with a saturated paste using your irrigation water; treat CEC and OM numbers as media-specific, not field benchmarks; budget for nitrogen and potassium replenishment every round (compost as the slow-release bank, plus feeding through flower); and when beds hit double-digit rounds, expect castings-dominated media that needs added aggregate and gentler irrigation. Cultivar differences mean tissue testing per cultivar is the precision layer on top of media testing.

## Key Takeaways

- Classical soil science assumes clay and homogeneity; soilless media has neither
- In containers, nutrient holding comes from organic matter, not mineral colloids — think solubility, not colloid balancing
- Saturated paste testing with your irrigation water beats standard extractions for container media
- Containers are mined systems: nitrogen and potassium deplete first and must be replenished every round
- Aging living-soil beds densify toward worm castings — re-add aggregate or adjust irrigation
- The physical-chemical-biological triangle still rules; specific topsoil research findings transfer with caveats
- CEC on a peat-media test is a calculated sum of cations, not a colloid measurement — chasing it over-amends
- Organic matter (loss on ignition) just weighs peat/coir carbon and tracks bulk density — ignore it in containers
- Exchangeable hydrogen is 100% correlated to pH and adds no information
