# The Soil Food Web

> **Quick take:** The soil food web is the network of organisms — from bacteria to earthworms — that cycle nutrients through the soil, powered almost entirely by carbon that plants pump out of their roots.

If you dig a shovel of healthy soil and could see everything alive in it, you would find a web of relationships as complex as any coral reef. Bacteria and fungi break down organic matter and mine minerals from rock particles. Protozoa and nematodes eat those bacteria and fungi, releasing the locked-up nutrients in plant-available forms. Arthropods and earthworms shred larger debris and mix the soil. All of these organisms depend, directly or indirectly, on one energy source: the carbon that plants create through [[photosynthesis-and-root-exudates|photosynthesis]] and push out through their roots.

The term "soil food web" was popularized by [[dr-elaine-ingham]], who spent decades mapping these relationships and advocating for biological approaches to soil management. The concept is powerful and well-supported by science, but it is also frequently oversimplified or presented in ideological ways that skip over important nuances.

## How Energy Flows Through the Web

The soil food web is not a simple chain (plant feeds microbe feeds predator). It is a network with many overlapping pathways. But the broad pattern is:

1. **Plants fix carbon.** Through photosynthesis, plants convert sunlight and CO2 into sugars and other carbon compounds. Up to 40% of this carbon is pushed out through the roots as [[photosynthesis-and-root-exudates|root exudates]] — the energy currency of the entire underground economy.

2. **Microbes trade services for carbon.** Bacteria and fungi cluster around root tips where exudates are richest (this zone is called the rhizosphere). They use enzymes and organic acids to dissolve minerals from soil particles, making nutrients available in exchange for the plant's carbon. Think of it as a trading post: the plant is the only mint in town, and every microbe needs its currency.

3. **Predators release nutrients.** Bacteria and fungi are, in [[tad-hussey]]'s memorable phrase, "tiny concentrated bags of fertilizer." They lock up nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients in their bodies. When protozoa or bacterial-feeding nematodes eat them, they excrete the excess as ionic forms — ammonium (NH4+), nitrate (NO3-), phosphate — the same chemical forms found in synthetic fertilizers. The difference is that biology delivers these nutrients just in time, directly to the root tip, rather than in a single large dose that can leach or burn.

4. **Larger organisms mix and structure.** Earthworms, arthropods, and other macro-organisms physically move organic matter through the soil profile, create channels for air and water, and produce castings rich in plant-available nutrients. Their tunneling helps build [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|soil aggregates]].

## The Trading Post Metaphor

The relationship between plants and soil microbes is best understood as trade, not charity. The plant does not passively leak carbon — it actively controls what it releases and changes the composition of its exudates based on what it needs. When a plant is short on phosphorus, it can increase exudation of specific organic acids that attract phosphorus-solubilizing bacteria. When it needs nitrogen, it can shift the blend to recruit nitrogen-fixing organisms.

This is not a one-way gift. Microbes that fail to deliver useful services do not get preferential access to the carbon supply. The system is competitive and dynamic — more like a marketplace than a commune.

## Predator-Prey Nutrient Release (The Microbial Loop)

One of the most important mechanisms in the food web is the microbial loop. Here is how it works:

- A bacterium has a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N) of roughly 5:1 to 8:1, meaning its body is very nitrogen-rich relative to carbon.
- A protozoan eats several bacteria. It needs the carbon for energy but has more nitrogen than it can use.
- The protozoan excretes the excess nitrogen as ammonium — a form the plant can absorb immediately.

This loop explains why diverse, biologically active soils often show steady nutrient availability without heavy fertilization. The nutrients are not coming from nowhere; they are being cycled from the bodies of organisms that mined them from soil minerals and organic matter. Understanding this cycle connects directly to [[nitrogen-and-phosphorus|nitrogen and phosphorus nutrition]] and to broader principles of [[composting-and-organic-inputs|organic soil management]].

## Stoichiometry: Why Carbon Additions Can Starve Plants

Because bacteria are so nitrogen-rich (low C:N), adding high-carbon amendments like raw wood chips or straw to soil triggers a microbial population boom. The microbes multiply rapidly using the new carbon source, but they need nitrogen to build their bodies. They pull it from the soil solution, outcompeting the plant. This is called nitrogen immobilization.

You might think you have a nutrient deficiency when you actually just have a microbial workforce temporarily hoarding the available nitrogen. The nutrients are not lost — they will be released later as predators consume the swollen microbial population — but the timing mismatch can hurt crops during critical growth phases.

This is why understanding C:N ratios matters when choosing amendments and why [[composting-and-organic-inputs|composting]] high-carbon materials before applying them is usually wiser than adding them raw.

## What the Food Web Is Not

The soil food web is a useful framework, but some common claims go beyond what the science supports:

- **"You never need to soil test if your biology is right."** Microbes can solubilize and cycle nutrients, but they cannot create elements that are not present in the soil. If your soil is deficient in zinc or boron, no amount of biological activity will fix that. [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis|Soil testing]] remains essential.
- **"pH does not matter if you have good biology."** Organic systems with high biological activity often happen to buffer [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability|pH]] into a favorable range, creating the illusion that biology overrides chemistry. It does not — it just correlates with conditions that keep pH in check.
- **"Just copy nature."** A forest floor is optimized for survival and reproduction of wild species, not for maximizing harvest of a crop. Blindly replicating forest conditions in a garden bed or grow room can create drainage problems, anaerobic pockets, and nutrient imbalances.
- **"Fungal-to-bacterial ratios are the key metric."** F:B ratios change with every watering, every temperature swing, every root exudate shift. A single measurement tells you very little, and the science on what ratio is "ideal" for a given crop is still thin.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Indoor cannabis growers sometimes try to replicate a forest-floor ecosystem inside a grow tent — layering raw compost, wood chips, and cover crops in a 4x4 space. This rarely works well. The confined environment lacks the drainage, airflow, and biological diversity of an outdoor system. The goal for controlled-environment agriculture (CEA) is not to copy nature but to harness biological principles within a managed, data-driven system. Use targeted inoculants (e.g., mycorrhizal fungi like *Rhizophagus irregularis* at transplant — cannabis is strictly endomycorrhizal), [[biocontrols-and-ipm|biocontrol agents]] like *Trichoderma* and *Beauveria bassiana*, and [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis|soil and sap testing]] to confirm that the biology is actually delivering what the plant needs.

## Key Takeaways

- The soil food web is the network of organisms that cycle nutrients through soil, powered by plant-derived carbon
- Plants actively control root exudates to recruit the microbes they need — it is trade, not charity
- Predator-prey interactions (the microbial loop) convert locked-up microbial nutrients into plant-available ionic forms
- High-carbon amendments can temporarily starve plants by triggering microbial nitrogen immobilization
- Biology is powerful but cannot replace soil testing, pH management, or site-appropriate amendments
- "Just copy nature" is not a viable strategy for managed growing environments
