# The Coot Mix

> **Quick take:** Equal thirds sphagnum peat, aeration, and high-quality worm castings, amended with kelp, neem, and basalt rock dust — a deliberately minimal living soil recipe whose real secret ingredient is the quality of your castings.

The most widely shared free soil recipe in organic cannabis growing, [[clackamas-coot|Clackamas Coot]]'s mix is a modified **Cornell mix** — the standardized potting medium Cornell University developed around 1937–38, before perlite even existed. Coot is blunt that he didn't invent it: "All people have to do is look up the Cornell mix. That's all I did and modified it a bit."

## The Recipe

**Base — equal thirds:**
1. **Sphagnum peat moss** — ⅓
2. **Aeration** — ⅓: Coot prefers **pumice or lava rock (scoria)**, cheap from landscape yards. He avoids perlite — from his nursery days he knows it migrates and floats to the top, while pumice stays where it belongs in the strata
3. **Worm castings** — ⅓: high-quality, ideally homemade (see [[vermicomposting]])

**Amendments — deliberately few:**
- **Kelp meal** — the cornerstone bio-accumulator: 83 elements in plant-relevant balance, no accumulated salt (see [[dynamic-accumulators]])
- **Neem meal** (and/or karanja) — insect and disease suppression via secondary metabolites
- **Crab meal** — historically the third amendment, but Coot has largely backed off it: the chitin it provides is available far more cheaply from [[malted-barley-and-enzyme-teas|malted barley]]
- **Basalt rock dust** — "copious amounts"; Pacific Northwest basalt is among the best in the world
- **Malted grains** — his later addition, worked into the soil

## Garbage In, Garbage Out

A third castings draws criticism as excessive — Coot shrugs it off, and his defense reveals the real point: **the recipe is only as good as its castings.** People who substitute bagged "steer manure from Walmart" for homemade castings, get bad results, and blame the recipe "didn't do mine. You don't have my castings."

The corollary: if you can't make quality castings yet, start a worm bin today — a fabric pot, aged manure, aeration material, and all the worms (or cocoons) you can afford. Plan a year ahead. "You want good potting soil, you've got to start with something good."

The recipe's influence runs all the way to commercial blending: [[rob-knechtel|Black Swallow Living Soils]] began with Coot-recipe variations, tweaked batch to batch against soil analysis, before the formulation work that became the Canadian KISS Mix ([[ep-075-formulating-soils|episode 75]]). The same castings caveat applied there too — which is why serious blenders [[formulating-living-soils|formulate around their own compost sources]] rather than copying anyone's recipe verbatim.

## On Peat Moss

Coot defends sphagnum peat against two common attacks:

- **"Peat is unsustainable."** He argues the sustainability fight is largely British Isles politics — in rural Ireland and Scotland peat is still home heating fuel, so gardening demand competes with energy needs. Canadian sphagnum harvest, by contrast, is tightly quota-controlled by the Canadian Sphagnum Peat Moss Association: even the largest harvester (Sun Gro, 54% of the allowed harvest) cannot exceed its allocation. The often-suggested alternatives — leaf mold, forest duff, hemp fiber — are simply not commercially available at scale.
- **"Peat is too acidic."** Mildly acidic, and usefully so: the acidity leaves room to add calcium up to target levels, which coco doesn't allow.

Practical detail: **3.8 cu ft compressed bales** are cut and wrapped at the harvest site, unprocessed. Bagged retail peat (e.g., in branded mixes) has been kiln-dried ("sterilized" — microbes killed), ground, then steam-rewetted so it feels fresh when the bag is opened. Buy bales, not bags. And "composted forest products" or "forest humus" on a label is, in Coot's words, code for sawdust (see [[composting-and-organic-inputs]]).

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> The Coot Mix is the de facto reference recipe for living-soil cannabis. Two failure modes account for most complaints: substituting poor-quality compost or castings for the ⅓ castings fraction, and running organic soil like a hydro system — a five-foot plant cannot thrive in a three-gallon pot. Give the biology adequate volume (see [[reusing-soil]] on pot sizing), skip the bottled "proprietary" programs entirely, and reuse the mix cycle after cycle — it improves with age. Auto-pruning fabric pots (Smart Pots and similar) yield more biomass than rigid nursery pots of equal volume, though not double; avoid tall narrow cylinders, which are designed for tree saplings and tip over.

## Key Takeaways

- Equal thirds: sphagnum peat, pumice/lava rock, quality castings
- Amendments are minimal: kelp, neem/karanja, basalt rock dust, malted grains (crab meal optional now)
- The recipe stands or falls on casting quality — make your own
- Pumice beats perlite because it stays distributed in the soil profile
- Buy peat in 3.8 bales (unprocessed), not kiln-dried bagged peat
- The recipe descends from the 1937–38 Cornell mix — this is old, proven horticulture
