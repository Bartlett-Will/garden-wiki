# Field Observation

> **Quick take:** The best diagnostic tool in your garden is showing up consistently, paying attention, and learning to trust what your eyes, nose, and hands are telling you.

Science gives us lab tests, tissue analysis, and biological assays. But the most experienced growers and consultants — people like [[lee-breeze|Lee Breeze]], who makes weekly field visits across central North Dakota — will tell you that consistent, attentive observation is what ties it all together. Lee calls himself a "plant doctor," and like a doctor, his diagnosis starts with looking at the patient before ordering tests.

## The Morning Wellness Check

Develop a routine. The best time to observe your garden is early morning, before heat stress or midday wilt mask the true condition of your plants.

A simple daily walk-through:

1. **Approach from a distance first** — Stand back and look at the whole planting. Is growth uniform? Are there patches that look different — lighter green, wilted, stunted? Uniformity (or the lack of it) tells you whether a problem is systemic (everywhere) or localized (specific zones).
2. **Move in closer** — Look at individual plants. Check new growth first — this is where nutrient deficiencies and toxicities show up fastest.
3. **Check both sides of leaves** — Pests often feed on the undersides. Fungal diseases frequently start where you don't look.
4. **Touch the soil** — Is it moist? Dry? Crusted? Soil surface condition tells you about [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|water management]] and [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|soil structure]].
5. **Note what changed since yesterday** — The single most valuable observation is change over time. A plant that looked fine yesterday and is wilting today is telling you something different than a plant that has been slowly declining for a week.

This takes five minutes. Done daily, it builds an intuitive understanding of your garden's rhythms that no amount of testing can replace.

[[dawn-marshall|Don Marshall]] frames the same routine as a hospital-style **"well-person check"**: walk the facility first thing, coffee in hand, with *no* task list — just "how's everybody doing?" Doing it when your energy is high and before the day's demands start is what lets you catch problems early and turn them into teaching moments for your team. He pushes the diagnosis one step further than the leaves: **knock suspect plants out of the pot** and read the root mass. Healthy roots are white and *tug back* (they snap or resist); roots that **slime off in your hand** are dead. And when you see a fungus on a declining plant, suspect that it is an **opportunist decomposing already-dead roots**, not the cause of death — which means the real fix is upstream (usually [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|overwatering]]), not a fungicide.

## Visual Indicators

Learn to read what your plants are showing you:

### Color

- **Dark green, uniform** — Healthy, adequate nitrogen
- **Pale green or yellowing (chlorosis)** — Possible nitrogen deficiency, iron deficiency (especially in high-pH soils), or overwatering
- **Yellowing between veins (interveinal chlorosis)** — Magnesium or manganese deficiency, or [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability|pH-induced]] micronutrient lockout
- **Purple or red tints** — Phosphorus deficiency, cold stress, or genetic expression (some cultivars naturally show anthocyanins)
- **Tip burn or leaf edge browning** — Potassium deficiency, salt stress, or calcium transport issues

### Vigor

- **Rapid, balanced growth** — Plant is happy
- **Stunted growth with dark green leaves** — Excess nitrogen pushing vegetative growth in wrong ratio, or root zone problem limiting water/nutrient uptake
- **Leggy, stretched growth** — Light-seeking behavior, insufficient light intensity
- **Uneven growth within the same planting** — Look for microclimates, drainage differences, or soil variability

### Uniformity

Uniformity is one of the most underrated diagnostic tools. If 95% of your plants look great and 5% look terrible, the problem is almost certainly localized — a drainage issue, a compaction spot, a microclimate. If everything looks equally stressed, the problem is systemic — pH, overall fertility, [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|watering]] regime.

## Microclimates and Dead Spots

Every garden has microclimates — zones where conditions differ from the average. Cold pockets where frost lingers, hot spots against south-facing walls, wet areas where drainage collects, dry corners that irrigation misses.

Map these over time:

- **Where does frost hit first and last?** — This defines your effective growing season zone by zone
- **Where does water pool after rain?** — Drainage problems, potential anaerobic zones
- **Where do plants consistently underperform?** — Compaction, shade, root competition from trees, contaminated soil
- **Where do plants thrive beyond expectations?** — Protected microclimate, superior drainage, residual fertility from previous use

Understanding your microclimates lets you place crops where they'll succeed rather than fighting conditions.

## Smell as a Biological Indicator

Your nose is a legitimate diagnostic tool:

- **Earthy, pleasant smell** — Actinomycetes (a group of soil bacteria that produce geosmin, the compound that makes rain smell like rain). This smell indicates active, healthy soil biology.
- **Sour, vinegar-like smell** — Anaerobic conditions. Beneficial aerobic organisms are suffocating, and anaerobic bacteria are producing organic acids. Drainage problem or overwatering.
- **Sulfurous, rotten egg smell** — Severe anaerobic conditions. Hydrogen sulfide production. This is toxic to roots.
- **No smell at all** — Low biological activity. Dead or sterilized soil.

When you dig into your soil for the [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis|shovel test]], always smell it. The biological information is immediate and requires no lab.

## The Qualitative + Quantitative Approach

[[lee-breeze|Lee Breeze's]] "plant doctor" method combines both approaches:

1. **Qualitative first** — Walk the field. Use your eyes, hands, and nose. Form hypotheses about what's happening.
2. **Quantitative to confirm** — Order the soil test, the tissue analysis, the water test. Use data to confirm or reject your field observations.
3. **Calibrate continuously** — Every time you get lab results back, compare them to what you observed in the field. Over seasons, your qualitative observations become more accurate because you've trained your eye against real data.

The goal is not to replace testing with gut feeling, or to replace observation with data. It is to build a feedback loop where each sharpens the other. Experienced growers can often diagnose a problem by sight and confirm it with a test. Beginners need the tests to teach their eyes what to look for.

## Lee Breeze's Plant Doctor Method

Lee visits his client farms weekly during the growing season. Each visit follows a consistent pattern:

- **Visual scan** of the whole field from the road — note uniformity, color, growth stage
- **Walking transects** through the field — checking specific plants, digging roots, inspecting soil
- **Recording observations** alongside lab data from that same period
- **Comparing** this visit to the previous one — what changed?
- **Building a season-long narrative** — one observation means nothing, but the trend over weeks tells the real story

"Details matter" is Lee's refrain. The grower who walks their field daily and notices the first aphid catches a problem when 10 plants are affected. The grower who checks monthly catches it when 10,000 plants are affected.

## Calibrating Gut Feel Against Data

Every experienced grower has stories about "knowing" something was wrong before the test confirmed it. That intuition is not magic — it is pattern recognition built from thousands of observations, each one unconsciously filed away.

To build this faster:

- **Write it down** — Keep a simple garden journal. Date, weather, what you saw, what you did. Review it at the end of the season.
- **Photograph consistently** — Same angle, same time of day, weekly. Time-lapse photos reveal gradual changes your daily eye misses.
- **Pair every observation with a question** — "This plant is yellowing" is an observation. "Is this nitrogen deficiency, overwatering, or pH lockout?" is a question that drives you to investigate and learn.
- **Follow up** — When you make a change based on an observation, check back in a few days. Did it work? This closes the feedback loop.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Indoor cannabis growers have an advantage and a disadvantage for observation. The advantage: you control the environment completely, so changes in plant health almost always trace to something you did (or didn't do). The disadvantage: the controlled environment can mask problems until they're severe — there's no rain to reveal drainage issues, no wind to test stem strength. Adjust your observation routine seasonally, even indoors: watering schedules and plant demands shift between vegetative and flowering stages. Morning checks before lights reach full intensity (or right at lights-on) catch issues like overnight pest activity, condensation problems, or early wilt that disappears once transpiration ramps up.

## Key Takeaways

- Show up every day, even for five minutes — consistency matters more than duration
- Start broad (whole garden scan) then narrow (individual plants, leaves, soil)
- Uniformity is diagnostic: systemic problems look uniform, localized problems create patches
- Your nose is a real tool — healthy soil smells earthy (actinomycetes), sick soil smells sour or sulfurous
- Pair qualitative observation with quantitative testing to build a calibrated feedback loop
- Record your observations — a garden journal is cheap and compounds in value over seasons
- The goal is pattern recognition: the more you observe, the faster you diagnose
