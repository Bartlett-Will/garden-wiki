# Reusing Soil

> **Quick take:** Throwing away used potting soil discards the microbial community, soil structure, and root infrastructure your plant spent an entire cycle building. Replace what was removed, and the soil only gets better with age.

Both Jeff Lowenfels and Tim Wilson are emphatic: discarding soil after a grow cycle is wasteful and counterproductive. Wilson ran no-till indoor bins for **seven years** without replacing the soil, producing consistent yields the entire time. The soil improves with each cycle as biology matures and structure develops.

## The Law of Return

In nature, nothing leaves the system. When a redwood drops its needles, they decompose through the [[soil-food-web]] and the nutrients cycle back to the tree. Nobody fertilizes a forest.

When you harvest a crop, you break this law — you remove plant material (and the nutrients it contains) from the soil. The fix is simple: **put back what you took out.** This can be organic amendments, [[compost-tea]], [[vermicomposting|vermicompost]], or cover crop residues. The mechanism (organic vs. synthetic) matters less than the principle: replace what was removed.

## Why Reused Soil Is Better Than Fresh

1. **Established microbial community** — the bacteria, fungi, and protozoa that the previous plant recruited through its [[photosynthesis-and-root-exudates|root exudates]] are already present and adapted. A new plant of the same species doesn't need to attract them from scratch
2. **Root infrastructure** — when you cut a plant at the base (don't pull it out), the old root channels remain as highways for new roots. New roots follow existing tunnels because it costs less energy than creating their own
3. **Intact [[mycorrhizal-fungi|mycorrhizal]] networks** — established hyphal networks, their glomalin coatings, and the carbon they store are preserved
4. **Mature soil structure** — [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|aggregates]], pore spaces, and drainage patterns are built over time and destroyed by starting over
5. **Nutrient reserves** — mass flow and diffusion concentrate nutrients around old root zones; new roots grow into these nutrient-rich areas

## How to Reamend Between Cycles

1. **Cut the old plant at the base** — leave roots in the soil to decompose and provide structure
2. **Top-dress** with compost, [[vermicomposting|vermicompost]], and dry amendments
3. **Apply [[compost-tea]]** to reinvigorate microbial populations
4. **Test the soil** if possible — see [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis]] to identify specific deficiencies rather than blindly adding everything

For rapid turnaround (24–36 hours between crops), getting amendments into the root zone is the main challenge. Options:
- **Light incorporation** — gently work amendments into the top few inches without pulverizing the soil
- **Bottom-layering** — place a nutrient-rich layer at the bottom of the container when first setting up, which can sustain 3–4 grow cycles
- **Liquid feeds** — [[compost-tea]], fish hydrolysate, or diluted organic nutrients as a bridge while solid amendments break down

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis growers commonly discard soil after each cycle — largely because the synthetic nutrient paradigm (high-EC salt-based feeds) leaves soil too saline to reuse without extensive leaching. In an organic system, this problem doesn't exist. Reuse your soil, top-dress between cycles, and apply compost tea. The biology and structure only improve. Use adequate pot sizes — trying to grow a full-cycle plant organically in a 1–2 gallon container is fighting an uphill battle. Organic growing needs enough soil volume for the biology to function.

## The Tilling Question

The no-till movement has strong scientific backing outdoors — tilling destroys [[mycorrhizal-fungi|mycorrhizal networks]], kills earthworms, degrades soil structure, and causes erosion (the original reason the USDA advocates no-till).

But indoor containers are different:
- No erosion risk
- No earthworms to bisect
- The biology you care about is primarily microbiological, not macro
- You may need to incorporate amendments quickly between crops

Light soil disturbance in containers (turning with a shovel, double-digging a raised bed) is very different from rototilling a field. The rule is **minimum disturbance necessary** — don't pulverize the soil, but don't be afraid to open it up enough to get amendments where they need to be.

## Key Takeaways
- Never throw away organic soil — it's a living system that improves with each cycle
- Cut plants at the base; leave roots as infrastructure for the next crop
- Replace what you removed: the law of return
- Test soil between cycles to amend intelligently
- Light disturbance in containers is acceptable; pulverizing is not
- Adequate pot size matters more in organic systems than synthetic ones
