# Soil Testing and Diagnosis

> **Quick take:** A soil test is your starting point, not your whole answer — pair the lab numbers with what you can see, smell, and feel in the field.

Soil testing is the foundation of informed growing. But a lab report is only one piece of the puzzle. [[lee-breeze|Lee Breeze]], an independent crop consultant working across central North Dakota, describes his approach as combining quantitative data (lab results) with qualitative field observation — what he calls the "plant doctor" method. The goal is to build a mental model of your soil that gets more accurate every season.

## Chemical Analysis: What the Numbers Mean

A standard soil chemical analysis measures nutrient levels in a dried soil sample. Because the sample is dried and processed under controlled conditions, these numbers are stable and repeatable regardless of when you sampled relative to moisture or temperature swings. This makes chemical analysis the most reliable starting point.

Key values to understand:

- **[[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability|pH]]** — The overriding factor of nutrient availability. Optimal range is 5.8-6.4, with 6.2 being ideal for most crops. Below 5.0, aluminum becomes toxic. Above 7.0, calcium starts tying up phosphorus, zinc, and other nutrients. In organic systems, high organic matter naturally buffers pH into a favorable range, which creates the illusion that pH doesn't matter. It does.
- **Buffer pH** — Measures your soil's resistance to pH change. A wide buffer means enormous amendments are needed to shift pH. At 8.5+, you might need 5 tons of sulfur per acre and it will bounce back within a few years. Sometimes it is better to grow pH-tolerant crops than to fight the buffer.
- **[[soil-structure-and-aggregates|CEC]] (Cation Exchange Capacity)** — Your soil's nutrient storage capacity. Clay and high-organic-matter soils naturally have high CEC. This explains why [[soil-food-web|humic acids]] dramatically help sandy soils but do almost nothing in clay or high-OM soils — "like taking aspirin when you don't have a headache."
- **EC / Soluble Salts** — Dissolved salt concentration. High salts create osmotic drought even when water is present, meaning the plant can't access moisture despite wet soil.
- **[[nitrogen-and-phosphorus|Nitrogen and Phosphorus]]** — Plants absorb N only as nitrate (NO3-) and ammonium (NH4+), and P only as H2PO4- and HPO4 2-. The brand or source is irrelevant — the plant sees only these ionic "Lego blocks."
- **Zinc** — Co-enzyme for [[photosynthesis-and-root-exudates|photosynthesis]] and reproduction (flower set, pollination). Easily locked up at high pH. Bonds with phosphate instantly, so avoid mixing them in solution.

### Zinc Application Options

If your test shows low zinc:

- **In-furrow chelated zinc**: ~1 qt/acre at planting for immediate availability
- **Broadcast ZnSO4**: every 3-4 years for background building
- **ZnSO4 in fertigation water**: for drip or irrigated systems

In high-pH soils, chelated forms are essential because free zinc ions get locked up immediately. Chelation wraps the nutrient ion in an organic molecule to prevent lockup, but chelates are expensive and only last one season.

### Choosing a Lab and Reading Ratios

[[steve-solomon]] adds practical lab economics: a standard test costs around $14 at Spectrum Analytic or $25 at Logan Labs (whose report format is friendlier to amateurs). Don't agonize over the choice — different labs' numbers may differ by roughly 10% across the board, but the **relationships between the numbers are what matter**, and the ratios read the same from either lab. Those ratios are the basis of the [[soil-remineralization|base cation balancing]] approach to amendment.

**Leaf tissue analysis** is the complement to soil testing: it shows what actually reached the plant, not just what's in the ground. Solomon proposes it as the decisive test for slow-release amendments like rock dust. (Note for cannabis growers: mailing cannabis leaves across state lines for analysis is not legal in most US jurisdictions.)

### Sampling Variability: Less Scary Than It Looks

A common objection to soil testing is the tiny sample size relative to a whole field or facility. [[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]], who has reviewed thousands of cannabis soil tests, offers a reassuring empirical answer ([[ep-072-heavy-metals-cover-crops-and-water-ph|episode 72]]): sample-to-sample variability — sometimes 30 ppm between samples — **almost never changes the management decision**. The same pattern shows up across samples. His Willamette Valley hazelnut story: a full day of rigorous sampling across 10 zones produced near-identical chemistry in every one. Most growers oversample.

Practical implications:

- **Stick to one lab.** Labs differ enough that targets only make sense within a single lab's methods (Bryant uses Logan Labs exclusively; the industry is "apples to oranges").
- **The exceptions are nitrate and pH** — the values that genuinely fluctuate between beds. Nitrate is extremely mobile; one overwatering flushes it ([[nitrogen-and-phosphorus]]).
- **Good beds vs. bad beds**: sample each separately, but expect similar chemistry — differing cultivar tolerance usually explains why some beds struggle ("canaries in the coal mine") while others shrug off the same imbalance.
- **Tissue test per cultivar.** Get the soil into a target range where most cultivars thrive, then refine soil targets cultivar-by-cultivar using leaf tissue results. The goal is improving levels, not perfection.
- **Send your irrigation water.** Have the saturated paste run with four ounces of your actual irrigation water (skip if RO or clean municipal) — soil and water combine to set the pH and solution your roots actually see ([[water-chemistry-and-alkalinity]]).

[[ep-075-formulating-soils|Episode 75]] reinforces the one-lab rule from the soil blender's side: [[tad-hussey]] can glance at a Logan report (Mehlich 3 plus saturated paste) and spot the likely limiting factor immediately, precisely because he sees the same format on every sample. It doesn't have to be Logan — [[rob-knechtel]] uses A&L Labs in Ontario, after Tad calibrated A&L's numbers against Logan's once — the point is to know what tests your lab runs and stay put. Batch-over-batch tests from a single lab are also the feedback loop that commercial [[formulating-living-soils|soil formulation]] is built on.

## Qualitative Assessment: The Shovel Method

Lab results tell you what's in the soil. Field observation tells you what the soil is actually doing. Lee Breeze recommends a hands-on assessment every time you visit a site:

1. **Dig a spadeful** — Note the resistance. Compacted soil fights back. Healthy soil lifts easily.
2. **Observe color** — Dark color indicates organic matter. Grey or blue tones suggest anaerobic (waterlogged) conditions. Mottling (rust-colored streaks) indicates intermittent drainage problems.
3. **Ribbon test** — Squeeze a moist handful between your thumb and forefinger. A long, smooth ribbon means high clay. A gritty, crumbly feel means sand-dominant. This tells you your soil texture (sand, silt, clay fractions) which determines water holding, drainage, CEC, and workability.
4. **Inspect aggregates** — Break apart a clod gently. Healthy [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|soil aggregates]] look like "chocolate cake" — porous, crumbly, with visible structure. Each aggregate is an ecosystem with different zones: dense interior (low oxygen), middle, and outer edge (high oxygen). Tillage destroys these structures, harming fungi more than bacteria.
5. **Smell it** — Healthy soil has a distinctive earthy smell from actinomycetes (a group of soil bacteria). Sour or sulfurous smells indicate anaerobic conditions. No smell at all may indicate low biological activity.

The power of qualitative assessment is that it reveals things lab tests miss: compaction layers, drainage patterns, biological activity, and structural health. Over time, you calibrate your observations against lab data and build intuition.

## Biological Testing: Proceed with Caution

Biological soil tests (PLFA profiles, CO2 respiration rates) are increasingly marketed to growers, but Lee Breeze offers a frank critique:

1. **Highly sensitive to sampling conditions** — Results swing with moisture, temperature, time of day, and season. Two samples from the same spot taken a week apart can look completely different.
2. **We don't know the targets** — Unlike chemical tests where we know the optimal range for phosphorus or potassium, we don't yet know which organisms to cultivate or what ratios to aim for.
3. **Not actionable** — The management recommendations would be the same regardless of the biological test results: add organic matter, reduce tillage, maintain good drainage.

If you do run biological tests, sample under the same conditions every time — same season, similar moisture levels, similar temperature, same point in your crop cycle. This at least lets you track trends, even if the absolute numbers are hard to interpret.

The best biological indicator remains a healthy crop.

[[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]] lands in the same place from the agronomy side: newer techniques (genome sequencing, PLFA) can *measure* biology, but without established targets they can't yet *manage* it — and the research consistently shows long-term microbial community composition is dictated by physical and chemical conditions anyway, pH above all ([[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]]). He goes further on equipment: the microscope is "the least useful of all the agronomic tools" for the average grower. His shopping order: a pulse meter and a tensiometer first, then a dino-lite scope for pest scouting, a dissolved oxygen meter, or a pH pen — the microscope last. (For the qualitative counterview, [[tim-wilson]] built an entire methodology on microscopy — see [[compost-tea]].)

### Microbial Biomass Meters: The Trend-Tracking Use Case

[[jeff-lowenfels]] offers a counterpoint in [[ep-050-jeff-lowenfels-returns|episode 50]]: biological tests become useful when you stop expecting absolute answers and use them for **trends**. His tool of choice is the **microBIOMETER**, an inexpensive in-field test that reads total microbial biomass on a scale up to ~2000 (a reading of 285 says "add biology"; 1700 says "work on something else"). His use cases:

- **Input efficacy** — test before and after applying a product, tea, or amendment, exactly as you would track NPK, to see whether it actually raised biomass.
- **Plant behavior** — biomass surges around roots during spring exudation and shifts when cannabis enters flower; you can watch it happen.

Its limits, which Jeff acknowledges: it does not identify species, does not (yet) separate bacteria from fungi — a beta version aims to add a bacteria:fungi ratio and pH — and a biomass number alone can't distinguish beneficials from a contaminated soil. Pair it with your nose: high biomass plus a sour smell is still bad soil. It complements, never replaces, chemical analysis and microscopy. Biomass does fluctuate through the day, but not dramatically enough to swamp trend comparisons.

Related options: **Solvita** CO2 respiration tests are backed by good research but expensive; **PLFA** (phospholipid fatty acid) analysis is the lab standard but slow and ~$500 per run. [[tad-hussey]]'s position is more conservative — a biomass number doesn't change his practices, since he's already brewing teas and adding quality compost — which echoes Lee Breeze's "not actionable" critique above. Both views agree on the underlying rule: only collect numbers you'll act on, and collect them consistently.

## Heavy Metals and Input Safety

A listener case from [[ep-050-jeff-lowenfels-returns|episode 50]]: a raised bed filled with bagged potting soil tested at **40 ppm lead**; the native soil next to it tested zero. The lead came in with the purchased soil — and digging into the regulations explained how:

- In Washington, products registered as **fertilizers** require heavy-metal testing, but **potting soils and soil amendments** largely do not. High cadmium, lead, or arsenic in a potting soil would simply never be checked. Radioactivity isn't tested for at all.
- Oregon requires heavy-metal testing only for **bagged** product (threshold ~238–243 ppm lead) — bulk truckload sales are exempt — and that threshold traces to FDA-commissioned consulting work decades old.
- Alaska, where Jeff lives, doesn't test cannabis for pesticides at all. Standards vary state by state.

Why this matters for growers:

- **Lead exposure is cumulative** — 40 ppm here plus another source there "adds up like a backpack." No number is comfortably ignorable.
- **Some crops concentrate metals.** Root crops and cannabis are dynamic accumulators (see [[silicon-in-plants]] for the silicon–arsenic transporter connection). Extraction makes it worse: leaf material processed into oil carries its metals into the concentrate.
- **Watch high-rock-dust inputs.** Heavy remineralization programs can import metals along with the minerals — see [[soil-remineralization]].
- **Test the "good stuff" too.** Excess is its own contamination: manure-heavy programs can push phosphorus to ~250 ppm — against a target nearer 50 ppm or lower — high enough to suppress [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for decades, and over-applied nitrogen shows up as toxicity that a test can confirm before you misdiagnose it as deficiency.

The moral: know where every input comes from, and test — both your inputs and your finished soil.

## Sufficiency First, Then Balance

[[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]] frames the order of operations explicitly ([[ep-108-understanding-living-soil-fertility|episode 108]]): **achieve sufficient nutrient levels first, then work toward balance.** A perfectly balanced soil is useless if there simply isn't enough food in it — a low EC / soluble-salt reading (say 200 ppm) means the plant runs out of fertility quickly no matter how elegant the cation ratios look. So check sufficiency and EC before you ever start fine-tuning ratios.

For the balance step, Bryant manages off the **saturated paste percentages**, not the standard-test base cation saturation ratios (BCSR). The familiar Albrecht-style BCSR targets — roughly **68–80% calcium, 10–15% magnesium, 3–7% potassium** on the Mehlich-3 standard test — he treats as "the tail that wags the dog." Instead he aims for roughly **calcium 50% / magnesium 20% / potassium 20%**, with the remaining ~10% to sodium and others, on the paste test, because that solution balance correlates best with the **tissue test** (which is what ultimately matters). He still keeps the standard-test ratios inside guardrails (potassium near 7%, never above ~12% magnesium, calcium above 60% and ideally 70%+), but he reads sufficiency and balance off the paste, not the standard percentages.

### Cation Excesses and Leaching as a Hard Refresh

Bryant ranks the cations by how worried he is to see them high:

- **Sodium** — the worst. Not an essential nutrient; pure osmotic burden on roots, forcing a less-efficient metabolism (some crops even substitute it for potassium). Aim as low as possible, but if sodium is in your **irrigation water you can never flush below the source level** — flushing with sodium-laden water just resets to the water's concentration.
- **Magnesium** — the trickiest to manage in peat media. Often excessive, gently antagonistic to calcium and potassium, and capable of quietly slowing growth and yield before any visual symptom. Set a minimum-sufficient magnesium and let calcium/potassium float to maintain balance; never apply magnesium above ~12% on the standard test; source only K-Mag (langbeinite) or an Epsom-salt foliar, never dolomite in a peat mix.
- **Sulfur** — a luxury nutrient: it rides along with calcium and potassium inputs (gypsum, compost) and only pressures EC. Harmless otherwise.
- **Calcium, potassium, manganese** — well tolerated high. Bryant runs calcium high, tolerates high potassium (especially with adequate tissue calcium into flower), and accepts slightly high manganese as long as it stays below iron. Genuinely alarming: chloride, and copper (60 ppm copper would worry him).

When something is genuinely in excess (e.g., sodium over ~45 ppm on the paste test, or a heavy-metal or nitrate failure), **leaching is a hard refresh.** Everything on the paste test is mobile and flushes together, so leaching drops *all* the soluble nutrients at once — after which you **retest and re-amend** fully. Method: water to runoff and keep going until the runoff EC falls to ~50% of where it started, or until roughly 6–12 inches of water has run out of the bed/pot (severity dictates how hard you go). Leach **after harvest**, not mid-round — mid-round leaching risks more harm than good in poorly draining media. If you must intervene mid-round (e.g., a commercial grower at 300 ppm nitrate with stalled plants), use multiple slower pulses that pull air back into the profile between events rather than one long soak.

## Calibrating Observation Against Lab Results

The real skill in soil diagnosis is developing a feedback loop:

1. **Get a baseline lab test** — Know your pH, CEC, nutrient levels, and organic matter percentage.
2. **Walk the field with that data** — Do the qualitative assessment. Note where the lab results match or contradict what you see.
3. **Make one change** — Amend, adjust watering, change tillage practice.
4. **Observe and retest** — Did the change show up in plant health? Did the next lab test move in the expected direction?

## On-Farm Experimentation

When testing a new practice or product:

- **Include a control** — Leave one section untreated so you have a comparison.
- **Replicate** — Test in multiple spots to account for natural variation.
- **Change one variable at a time** — If you change the fertilizer and the watering schedule simultaneously, you won't know which caused the result.
- **Track profitability, not just yield** — A practice that increases yield by 10% but costs 20% more is not an improvement.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Standard soil test calibration curves were developed for field agriculture and may not translate directly to soilless media. If you're growing in peat, coir, or other potting mixes, be aware that some lab numbers (especially pH and nutrient levels) need different interpretation. Potting soil pH sweet spot is typically 6.6-6.8 when using alkaline inputs like biochar or high-calcium amendments. For soilless media, a **saturated paste extract** (also called saturated media extract) provides more meaningful results than a standard soil test — it measures what's actually in the root zone solution rather than total exchangeable nutrients. Humic acid additions often show little benefit in high-OM potting soil that already has high CEC.

## Key Takeaways

- pH is the single most important number on your soil test — it controls nutrient availability for everything else
- Chemical analysis is stable and repeatable; biological tests are not yet reliably actionable
- The shovel, your hands, and your nose are legitimate diagnostic tools — use them every visit
- Calibrate your field observations against lab data over multiple seasons to build real soil intuition
- High-CEC soils (clay, high organic matter) need different amendment strategies than low-CEC soils (sand)
- On-farm experiments need controls, replication, and single-variable changes to produce useful information
- Track profit, not just yield
- Stick to one lab — targets only mean something within a single lab's methods
- Sampling variability rarely changes the management decision; nitrate and pH are the values that genuinely swing
- Refine per cultivar with tissue testing, and run saturated paste tests with your own irrigation water
- Sufficiency first, then balance — balanced ratios are meaningless if total fertility (EC) is too low
- Manage cation balance off the saturated paste (~Ca 50 / Mg 20 / K 20), not the standard-test BCSR ratios
- Sodium is the worst cation to have high; magnesium the trickiest in peat; leaching is a hard refresh that flushes everything, so retest and re-amend after
