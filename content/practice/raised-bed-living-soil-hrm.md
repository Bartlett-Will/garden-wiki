# Raised Bed Living Soil for Halifax (HRM)

> **Quick take:** A complete recipe, feeding plan, and sourcing guide for filling a 4×8×18" fabric raised bed with living soil in Halifax Regional Municipality — designed to support heavy-feeding crops through a full season using locally sourced Nova Scotia blueberry compost.

This guide applies principles from [[formulating-living-soils]], [[the-coot-mix]], and [[reusing-soil]] to a specific context: an outdoor fabric raised bed in HRM (Zone 6a, ~1400 mm annual rainfall, granitic bedrock, growing season May through October). Everything you need is on this page.

---

## The Recipe

### Bed Volume

```
4 ft × 8 ft × 1.5 ft = 48 cu ft
48 ÷ 27 cu ft/yard   = 1.78 cubic yards
```

### Base Mix

| Component | % | Volume | Math | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blueberry compost | 55% | 26.4 cu ft | 48 × 0.55 | Biology, organic matter, moisture retention |
| Topsoil / loam | 20% | 9.6 cu ft | 48 × 0.20 | Mineral base, CEC, structure, weight |
| Coarse perlite | 15% | 7.2 cu ft | 48 × 0.15 | Aeration for Halifax's wet springs |
| Worm castings | 5% | 2.4 cu ft | 48 × 0.05 | Premium biology starter |
| Top-off reserve | 5% | 2.4 cu ft | 48 × 0.05 | Extra compost or topsoil after settling |
| **Total** | **100%** | **48.0 cu ft** | | |

### Mineral Amendments

Mixed into the full 48 cu ft before planting.

| Amendment | Rate / cu ft | × 48 | Total | NPK contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glacial rock dust | 1/2 cup | 24 cups | ~15 lbs | Trace Ca, Mg, Fe, Si |
| Gypsum | 1/4 cup | 12 cups | ~6 lbs | Ca + S (pH-neutral) |
| Kelp meal | 1/4 cup | 12 cups | ~4 lbs | K, micronutrients |
| Crustacean meal | 1/4 cup | 12 cups | ~3 lbs | Slow N + Ca + chitin |
| Alfalfa meal | 1/2 cup | 24 cups | ~6 lbs | Slow N + K + triacontanol |

### At Planting

| Item | Amount | When |
|---|---|---|
| [[mycorrhizal-fungi\|Mycorrhizal inoculant]] (MYKE) | 1 box | Dust directly on roots at transplant |
| Straw mulch | 3–4 inch layer | After transplanting |

## Feeding Plan

The base mix provides enough nutrition for 8–10 weeks. Heavy feeders (cannabis in flower, indeterminate tomatoes, brassicas) need mid-season support. This is normal — even the [[the-coot-mix|Coot Mix]] and commercial living soils need supplementing for heavy feeders. "Water-only" is a beginner simplification, not a ceiling (see [[formulating-living-soils]]).

| When | Action | What it provides |
|---|---|---|
| **Week 0** | Mix all 5 amendments into soil | Base nutrition: ~0.54 lbs N up front |
| **Week 6–8** | Top-dress 1–2 cups alfalfa meal per heavy feeder, worked into mulch | Nitrogen boost as plants enter peak demand |
| **Monthly** | [[compost-tea]] drench (vermicompost + molasses, 24–36 hr brew) | Makes existing nutrients plant-available, adds biology |
| **As available** | Top-dress with worm castings from indoor bin | N + P + K + biology directly in root zone |
| **September** | Sow winter rye cover crop | Holds soil structure, adds N when chopped in spring |

**Buy extra alfalfa meal.** The initial mix uses ~6 lbs. Buy a 10–15 lb bag so you have plenty for mid-season top-dressing. It's the cheapest amendment (~$10–15 for a large bag at any feed store).

## Why This Recipe Works

### pH Balance

The blueberry compost sits at **pH 6.5–6.7** (producer-tested). The vegetable target range is **6.0–6.8**. The compost lands squarely in range without correction.

```
Component pH contributions (approximate):
  Blueberry compost (55%):  pH 6.5–6.7
  Topsoil (20%):            pH 5.5–6.5 (typical Nova Scotia, granite bedrock)
  Perlite (15%):            pH ~7.0 (neutral, inert)
  Worm castings (5%):       pH 6.5–7.0

Weighted estimate: ~6.3–6.8
Amendment shifts: rock dust and crustacean meal nudge slightly alkaline (+0.1–0.2)
Gypsum: pH-neutral (no shift)

Expected finished mix: pH 6.3–6.8 ✓
```

**No lime needed.** Adding dolomite lime at standard rates (1/4 cup/cu ft) to a mix already at 6.5+ risks pushing above 7.0, which locks out iron, manganese, and zinc. Gypsum provides calcium without touching pH.

**Test after mixing.** A $15 soil pH meter confirms the balance before planting. If the mix reads below 6.0 (unlikely), add dolomite lime at 1/4 cup per cu ft. If above 7.0, reduce rock dust in the next batch.

### Why Not Peat?

Peat (pH 3.5–4.5) is used in living soil recipes to create pH headroom — the acidity leaves room to pack in alkaline mineral amendments (lime, oyster shell) that carry calcium and magnesium as they buffer pH back up. [[sphagnum-peat-and-potting-soils|Clackamas Coot]] calls this a feature: "the acidity leaves room to add calcium up to target levels."

In this recipe, the blueberry compost already provides pH in range. Adding peat would:

1. **Pull pH down unnecessarily** — requiring lime and oyster shell flour to bring it back up, adding two products to solve a problem peat created
2. **Create a moisture problem** — the blueberry compost producer specifically recommends against adding peat because their compost already holds significant moisture. Peat + this compost in a fabric bed during Halifax's wet springs risks waterlogging, which would require bumping perlite from 15% to 20% to compensate
3. **Add complexity for marginal gain** — lime and oyster shell provide calcium and magnesium, but gypsum already provides calcium and rock dust provides both Ca and Mg. Peat headroom enables *more* of those minerals, not *different* ones

The peat headroom strategy is sound in theory ([[soil-remineralization]]) and essential when starting from a neutral or alkaline base. Here, the compost does the job without it. Simplicity is worth more than marginal mineral loading on a first bed — you can add peat in year 2 if you want to push nutrient density further.

### Nitrogen Budget

Nitrogen is the limiting nutrient. Pure blueberry fruit and leaf waste is carbon-heavy and nitrogen-light compared to manure-based composts. The amendment rates are designed to compensate.

```
Nitrogen from amendments (at mixing):
  Alfalfa meal:     ~6 lbs × 2.5% N  = 0.150 lbs N
  Crustacean meal:  ~3 lbs × 4.0% N  = 0.120 lbs N
  Kelp meal:        ~4 lbs × 1.0% N  = 0.040 lbs N
  Worm castings:   ~15 lbs × 1.5% N  = 0.225 lbs N
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Total up-front N:                   ≈ 0.54 lbs N
```

Demand for a 4×8 bed (32 sq ft):

```
  Moderate feeders (lettuce, herbs, peas):     0.15–0.30 lbs N / season
  Heavy feeders (tomatoes, cannabis, brassicas): 0.60–1.50 lbs N / season
  (based on 4–6 plants)
```

The amendments alone provide **0.54 lbs N** — sufficient for moderate feeders, roughly half of what heavy feeders need. The gap is closed by:

- **Compost mineralization** — blueberry compost continues releasing N as microbes break it down (hard to quantify in year 1, increases with soil maturity)
- **In-situ worm castings** — red wigglers continuously produce N-rich castings in the root zone
- **Compost tea** — monthly drenches make locked-up N plant-available by boosting microbial activity
- **Mid-season alfalfa meal top-dress** — 1–2 cups per plant adds ~0.05–0.10 lbs N per application
- **Indoor worm bin castings** — top-dress as available, each application adds small N increments

This staged approach — base charge plus biological cycling plus targeted top-dressing — is standard living soil practice. The [[formulating-living-soils]] page confirms that even the 19-amendment commercial KISS Mix needs supplemental teas and top-dresses for heavy feeders.

### Phosphorus and Potassium

```
Phosphorus (P):
  Crustacean meal:  ~3 lbs × 3.0% P₂O₅  = 0.09 lbs P₂O₅
  Worm castings:   ~15 lbs × 1.5% P₂O₅  = 0.23 lbs P₂O₅
  Rock dust:        trace
  Blueberry compost: ongoing mineralization
  Total: adequate — P is rarely limiting in compost-heavy mixes

Potassium (K):
  Kelp meal:        ~4 lbs × 2.0% K₂O  = 0.08 lbs K₂O
  Alfalfa meal:     ~6 lbs × 2.0% K₂O  = 0.12 lbs K₂O
  Blueberry compost: moderate K (blueberries carry decent potassium)
  Total: adequate — K is well-supplied between kelp, alfalfa, and the compost
```

The [[nitrogen-and-phosphorus]] page notes that phosphorus is tricky to manage by soil test and is usually adequate in well-composted soils. Potassium is rarely deficient in compost-heavy, kelp-amended mixes.

### Why Blueberry Compost

The blueberry compost is made solely from overripe and underripe blueberries plus the stems and leaves from picking. Nothing else is added. This makes it unusually clean:

- **No weed seeds** — fruit processing waste, not yard trimmings
- **No herbicide carryover** — no hay, manure, or municipal inputs
- **No heavy metal risk** — no mined materials, no sewage sludge, no industrial waste
- **Fungal-dominant biology** — fruit and leaf waste feeds fungi preferentially during composting, producing a fungal-dominant compost. The [[soil-food-web]] pages describe how flowering and fruiting plants (including cannabis) perform best in fungal-dominant soils
- **Sustainable and local** — uses Nova Scotia blueberry processing waste that would otherwise go to landfill

The [[formulating-living-soils]] page states the core principle: "formulate around your compost." The recipe adapts to whatever quality compost you can source locally. This compost's clean profile and near-neutral pH make it an excellent base that requires fewer corrective amendments than most commercial composts.

### Why Each Amendment

| Amendment | Primary role | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| Glacial rock dust | Trace minerals (Ca, Mg, Fe, Si, 60+ elements) | Slow-release mineral diversity the compost and topsoil can't provide alone. See [[soil-remineralization]] |
| Gypsum | Calcium + sulfur | Provides Ca without raising pH — important since the mix is already at 6.5+. Sulfur supports protein synthesis |
| Kelp meal | Potassium + micronutrients + cytokinins | The cornerstone bio-accumulator: 83 elements in plant-relevant balance. See [[kelp-and-seaweed]] |
| Crustacean meal | Slow N + Ca + chitin | Chitin triggers plant chitinase production — a systemic pest defense response. Slow N release over 6–8 weeks |
| Alfalfa meal (doubled) | Slow N + K + triacontanol | Doubled to 1/2 cup/cu ft to compensate for the compost's low nitrogen. Triacontanol is a natural plant growth stimulant. Cheap and locally available at feed stores |

## Fabric Bed Considerations

Rigid beds and in-ground gardens benefit from ground contact with native soil biology and natural drainage. Fabric beds change the equation:

- **Closed system.** Biology must come entirely from your inputs — the bed has no ground contact.
- **Air pruning.** Fabric walls breathe, air-pruning roots at the edges and pushing root mass toward the center. The entire soil column must be plantable, not filler.
- **Faster drying in summer.** Airflow through the walls accelerates evaporation. The blueberry compost's moisture retention and straw mulch compensate.
- **No buried wood.** Decomposing logs rob nitrogen from a finite pool with no subsoil to draw from, and as they shrink the fabric walls sag inward. Cedar is especially harmful — its thujaplicins are antifungal and suppress the [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and [[soil-food-web|soil biology]] the recipe depends on.
- **Freeze exposure.** In winter, a fabric bed freezes from all six sides simultaneously. See Overwintering below.

## Adding Worms to a Fabric Bed

Red wigglers (*Eisenia fetida*) thrive in fabric beds. The fabric walls keep them contained — unlike open-bottom rigid beds where they escape into the ground.

- **Red wigglers, not earthworms.** Wigglers stay in the top 6–8 inches where the organic matter is. Earthworms are burrowers that hit the fabric floor and stress out.
- **Start with half a pound** (~250 worms). They double every 60–90 days. By mid-summer you'll have a healthy population.
- **Wait 1–2 weeks after mixing.** Fresh mineral amendments (especially gypsum) can be harsh. Let the soil buffer first.
- **Don't top-feed with kitchen scraps.** The worms will feed on decomposing straw mulch, dead root material, compost, and cover crop residue — a bed that's 55% compost with straw mulch is plenty of food. Kitchen scraps on an outdoor bed attract fruit flies, rodents, raccoons, and skunks. Run a **separate indoor worm bin** for kitchen scraps and top-dress the bed with those castings instead. See [[vermicomposting]].
- **Mulch is non-negotiable.** Worms need the surface layer cool and moist. Fabric walls let heat in from the sides, so thick mulch (3–4 inches of straw) matters even more than in a rigid bed.

## Overwintering in Halifax

A fabric bed in Zone 6a freezes solid in winter. The [[soil-food-web|soil biology]] is tougher than you'd expect — but it needs help.

### What Survives Freezing

| Organism | Survives? | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Bacteria | Yes | Spore formation and dormancy |
| [[mycorrhizal-fungi\|Mycorrhizal fungi]] | Yes | Spores and colonized root tips overwinter |
| Trichoderma | Yes | Cold-hardy spore production |
| Protozoa and nematodes | Yes | Encystment (protective shells) |
| Red wiggler worms | **No** | Die below −4°C |

The real threat is not sustained cold but **repeated freeze-thaw cycles** — a sunny February day thaws the bed, then it refreezes overnight. Each cycle ruptures cell walls and destroys [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|soil aggregates]]. A fabric bed exposed on all six sides is especially vulnerable.

### Winter Protection

**Insulate the walls (most important):**
- Stack straw bales around the outside perimeter. They become next year's mulch.
- Alternative: wrap with old blankets, carpet underlay, or rigid foam board held with bungee cords.

**Heavy mulch on top:**
- 6–8 inches of straw or shredded leaves after the ground freezes (late November).
- Don't mulch too early — let the bed cool gradually.

**Plant a cover crop in September:**
- Winter rye germinates fast, grows until hard freeze, and its root mass holds soil structure. Chop and drop in spring for free nitrogen. See [[cover-crops-and-crop-rotation]].

**Let snow accumulate** — snow is excellent insulation (~R-1 per inch). Don't brush it off.

**Harvest the worms in October.** Move them to an indoor bin, or accept the loss and re-buy in spring (~$25–40 from Kijiji Halifax). Castings and cocoons left behind still have value.

**Spring restart:** a [[compost-tea]] drench when soil hits ~10°C (late April in HRM) jumpstarts the microbial population. By June you're back to full biological activity.

## Halifax Seasonal Timeline

| When | Action |
|---|---|
| **Late April** | Mix soil in the bed. Let sit 1–2 weeks to buffer. |
| **Early May** | Add worms. Apply MYKE at transplant holes. Plant cool-season crops. Compost tea drench. |
| **Late May** | Plant warm-season crops after last frost (~May 20–25). Mulch 3–4 inches of straw. |
| **Week 6–8** | Top-dress heavy feeders with 1–2 cups alfalfa meal per plant. |
| **Monthly** | Compost tea drench. Top-dress with indoor worm bin castings as available. |
| **September** | Sow winter rye cover crop between remaining plants. |
| **October** | Harvest worms to indoor bin. Final harvest. Chop plant material, leave in bed. |
| **Late November** | Heavy mulch (6–8 in). Stack straw bales around exterior walls. |
| **Late April (Year 2)** | Remove insulation. Top-dress with compost and worm castings. Compost tea drench. Repeat. |

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> This recipe works for outdoor cannabis in HRM (Zone 6a, ~140–150 frost-free days). Autoflowering varieties (see [[autoflowering-cannabis]]) are the safest bet for the short season — 70–90 days seed to harvest fits between late May and early October. Photoperiod strains risk October frost before finishing flower. The blueberry compost at pH 6.5–6.7 lands right in the cannabis sweet spot (6.0–6.5). The mid-season alfalfa meal top-dress is particularly important for cannabis in flower — the nitrogen demand spike during weeks 3–5 of flowering is when deficiency shows first. The compost's clean profile (no heavy metals, no herbicide carryover) is a compliance advantage for regulated production. For mycorrhizal inoculant, confirm the product contains *Rhizophagus irregularis* (endomycorrhizal) — cannabis does not associate with ectomycorrhizal species (see [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]).

## Key Takeaways

- Blueberry compost (pure fruit + leaf waste, pH 6.5–6.7) provides clean biology and organic matter at near-ideal pH — no lime or peat needed
- Five amendments provide the base nutrition charge; nitrogen is the limiting nutrient and is addressed with doubled alfalfa meal + mid-season top-dressing
- The feeding plan (base charge → week 6–8 top-dress → monthly compost tea → worm castings) is designed to carry heavy feeders through a full Halifax season
- Gypsum provides calcium without raising pH above 7.0; rock dust provides Ca, Mg, and 60+ trace minerals slowly
- Fabric beds are closed systems — all biology comes from inputs, not the ground
- Red wigglers process mulch and compost in-situ; run a separate indoor bin for kitchen scraps to avoid attracting pests
- Harvest worms in October — they cannot survive a Halifax winter in a fabric bed
- Insulate walls with straw bales and top with heavy mulch to slow freeze-thaw cycling
- Year 2 costs drop to $20–40 as the biology matures and stockpiled compost, worm castings, and cover crops provide free inputs
