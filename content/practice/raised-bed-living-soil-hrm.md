# Raised Bed Living Soil for Halifax (HRM)

> **Quick take:** A complete recipe and sourcing guide for filling a fabric raised bed with living soil in the Halifax Regional Municipality — adapted to Nova Scotia's acidic soils, maritime climate, and locally available inputs.

This guide applies the principles from [[formulating-living-soils]], [[the-coot-mix]], and [[reusing-soil]] to a specific context: an outdoor fabric raised bed in HRM (Zone 6a, ~1400 mm annual rainfall, granitic bedrock, short growing season from May through October). It is a standalone document — everything you need is on this page.

## Why Fabric Beds Need a Different Approach

Rigid beds and in-ground gardens can use a hugelkultur base (buried logs for moisture and fungal food) and benefit from ground contact with native soil biology. Fabric beds change the equation:

- **No ground contact.** Biology must come entirely from your inputs — the bed is a closed system.
- **Air pruning.** Fabric walls breathe, which air-prunes roots at the edges and pushes root mass toward the center. The entire soil column must be plantable, not filler.
- **Faster drying.** Airflow through the walls accelerates evaporation — moisture retention matters more than drainage.
- **Freeze exposure.** In winter, a fabric bed freezes from all six sides simultaneously, unlike an in-ground bed insulated by the earth below.
- **No buried wood.** Decomposing logs rob nitrogen from a finite pool with no subsoil to draw from, and as they shrink the fabric walls sag inward.

## The Recipe

Sized for a **4 ft x 8 ft x 18 in** fabric raised bed.

### Volume

```
4 ft x 8 ft x 1.5 ft = 48 cu ft
48 / 27 cu ft per yard = 1.78 cubic yards
```

### Base Mix

This recipe uses **blueberry compost** as the primary base — a locally available, slightly acidic compost made from blueberry processing waste. It replaces both the peat moss and the standard compost in a traditional mix, providing organic matter, moisture retention, acidity, and biology in one input.

| Component | % | Volume | Math | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blueberry compost | 55% | 26.4 cu ft | 48 x 0.55 | Organic matter, moisture retention, acidity, biology |
| Topsoil / loam | 20% | 9.6 cu ft | 48 x 0.20 | Mineral base, CEC, structure, weight |
| Coarse perlite | 15% | 7.2 cu ft | 48 x 0.15 | Aeration — Halifax spring and fall rains demand it |
| Worm castings | 5% | 2.4 cu ft | 48 x 0.05 | Premium biology starter (worms will produce more in-situ) |
| Top-off reserve | 5% | 2.4 cu ft | 48 x 0.05 | Extra blueberry compost or topsoil after settling |
| **Total** | **100%** | **48.0 cu ft** | | |

**Why these ratios:**

- **Blueberry compost at 55%** — does double duty as both the compost and the peat fraction. The producer describes it as "slightly acidic," but the actual pH will vary by batch — ask the supplier for lab results or test a sample yourself before mixing. Its organic matter holds moisture through dry July spells. The producer recommends against adding peat moss alongside it.
- **Topsoil at 20%** — provides mineral content and [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|CEC]] that compost alone lacks, adds weight to stabilize the fabric in wind, and contributes mineral-associated microbes.
- **Perlite at 15%** — aeration, not drainage. The fabric handles drainage; perlite keeps oxygen in the root zone during Halifax's prolonged wet periods. Pumice is superior but expensive to ship to Atlantic Canada.
- **Worm castings at 5%** — starter dose of premium [[soil-food-web|microbial diversity]]. Kept low because live worms added to the bed will manufacture castings in-situ continuously. See [[vermicomposting]].

### Mineral Amendments

Mixed into the full 48 cu ft before planting. Rates are per cubic foot, multiplied by 48.

| Amendment | Rate / cu ft | x 48 | Total | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glacial rock dust | 1/2 cup | 24 cups | ~15 lbs | Slow-release trace minerals (Ca, Mg, Fe, Si) |
| Kelp meal | 1/4 cup | 12 cups | ~4 lbs | Potassium, micronutrients, growth hormones. See [[kelp-and-seaweed]] |
| Crustacean meal | 1/4 cup | 12 cups | ~3 lbs | Chitin (triggers plant pest defense), slow N + Ca |
| Gypsum | 1/4 cup | 12 cups | ~6 lbs | Calcium + sulfur without raising pH |
| Dolomite lime | 1/4 cup | 12 cups | ~6 lbs | pH buffer, adds Ca + Mg |
| Alfalfa meal | 1/4 cup | 12 cups | ~3 lbs | Slow N, triacontanol growth stimulant |

**On calcium and pH buffering:** dolomite lime buffers acidity and provides calcium + magnesium. Gypsum provides calcium + sulfur without moving pH. Two sources at standard rates is sufficient for the first season. If pH testing after a full growing season shows the bed drifting acidic, add oyster shell flour (ultra-slow calcium carbonate, breaks down over 1-2 years) as a long-term buffer at that point — but don't add it blind.

**Test pH after mixing and before planting.** The blueberry compost pH will vary by batch — the liming rate above is a starting point, not a prescription. Target 6.0-6.8 for vegetables. A $15 soil pH meter pays for itself immediately. If the compost turns out to be very acidic (below pH 5.0), increase lime to 1/3 cup per cu ft.

### At Planting

| Item | Amount | When |
|---|---|---|
| [[mycorrhizal-fungi\|Mycorrhizal inoculant]] (MYKE) | 1 box | Dust directly on roots at transplant |
| Straw mulch | 3-4 inch layer | After transplanting — non-negotiable for worm survival |

## Adding Worms to a Fabric Bed

Red wigglers (*Eisenia fetida*) thrive in fabric beds. The fabric walls keep them contained — unlike open-bottom rigid beds where they escape into the ground.

- **Species matters.** Red wigglers, not earthworms. Wigglers stay in the top 6-8 inches where the organic matter is. Earthworms are burrowers that hit the fabric floor and stress out.
- **Start with half a pound** (~250 worms). They double every 60-90 days in good conditions. By mid-summer you'll have a healthy population.
- **Wait 1-2 weeks after mixing** before adding worms. Fresh mineral amendments (especially lime and gypsum) can be harsh. Let the soil buffer first.
- **Feed from the top.** Bury kitchen scraps under the straw mulch — banana peels, coffee grounds, melon rinds. The worms process it into [[vermicomposting|castings]] right in the root zone, which is why the initial castings fraction is only 5%.
- **Mulch is non-negotiable.** Worms need the surface layer cool and moist. Fabric walls let heat in from the sides, so thick mulch (3-4 inches of straw) matters even more than in a rigid bed.

## Overwintering in Halifax

A fabric bed in Zone 6a freezes solid in winter. The biology is tougher than you'd expect — but it needs help.

### What Survives Freezing

| Organism | Survives? | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Bacteria | Yes | Spore formation and dormancy |
| [[mycorrhizal-fungi\|Mycorrhizal fungi]] | Yes | Spores and colonized root tips overwinter |
| Trichoderma | Yes | Cold-hardy spore production |
| Protozoa and nematodes | Yes | Encystment (protective shells) |
| Red wiggler worms | **No** | Die below -4C |

The real threat is not sustained cold but **repeated freeze-thaw cycles** — a sunny February day thaws the bed, then it refreezes overnight. Each cycle ruptures cell walls and destroys [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|soil aggregates]]. A fabric bed exposed on all six sides is especially vulnerable to this cycling.

### Winter Protection

**Insulate the walls (most important):**
- Stack straw bales around the outside perimeter. They become next year's mulch.
- Alternative: wrap the outside with old blankets, carpet underlay, or rigid foam board held with bungee cords.

**Heavy mulch on top:**
- 6-8 inches of straw or shredded leaves after the ground freezes (late November in Halifax).
- Don't mulch too early — let the bed cool gradually.

**Plant a cover crop in September:**
- Winter rye germinates fast, grows until hard freeze, and the root mass holds soil structure through winter. The dead top growth adds insulation. Chop and drop in spring for free nitrogen and organic matter. See [[cover-crops-and-crop-rotation]].

**Let snow accumulate** — snow is excellent insulation (~R-1 per inch). Don't brush it off.

**Harvest the worms in October.** Red wigglers die in frozen soil. Move them to an indoor bin for winter, or accept the loss and re-buy in spring (~$25-40 from Kijiji Halifax). The castings and cocoons they leave behind still have value.

**Spring restart:** a [[compost-tea]] drench when soil temperatures hit ~10C (typically late April in HRM) jumpstarts the microbial population. By June you're back to full biological activity.

- **DIY bin** — any opaque plastic storage bin ($8-12 at Walmart or Dollarama on Sackville Dr) with holes drilled in the lid.

- 5-gallon bucket ($4-5) from Home Depot or Canadian Tire

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> This recipe works for outdoor cannabis in HRM (Zone 6a, ~140-150 frost-free days). Autoflowering varieties (see [[autoflowering-cannabis]]) are the safest bet for the short season — 70-90 days seed to harvest fits comfortably between late May and early October. Photoperiod strains risk running into October frost before finishing flower. The blueberry compost acidity is actually favorable for cannabis, which prefers pH 6.0-6.5 — lighter liming than the vegetable target may be appropriate. For mycorrhizal inoculant, confirm the product contains *Rhizophagus irregularis* (endomycorrhizal) — cannabis does not associate with ectomycorrhizal species (see [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]).

## Halifax Seasonal Timeline

| When | Action |
|---|---|
| **Late April** | Mix soil in the bed. Let it sit 1-2 weeks to buffer. |
| **Early May** | Add worms. Apply MYKE to transplant holes. Plant cool-season crops (lettuce, peas, brassicas). Drench with compost tea. |
| **Late May** | Plant warm-season crops after last frost (~May 20-25 in HRM). Mulch with 3-4 inches of straw. |
| **June-August** | Feed worms with kitchen scraps under mulch. Brew compost tea monthly. |
| **September** | Sow winter rye cover crop between remaining plants. |
| **October** | Harvest worms to indoor bin. Final harvest. Chop remaining plant material and leave in bed. |
| **Late November** | Heavy mulch (6-8 in). Stack straw bales around exterior walls. |
| **Late April (Year 2)** | Remove winter insulation. Top-dress with worm castings and compost. Compost tea drench. Repeat. |

## Key Takeaways

- Fabric beds are closed systems — all biology must come from your inputs, not the ground
- Blueberry compost replaces both peat and standard compost in a single locally-sourced input
- Ask the supplier for pH lab results or test a sample — liming rates depend on actual acidity
- Test pH after mixing and before planting — target 6.0-6.8 for vegetables; add oyster shell flour only if pH drifts low after a season
- Add red wigglers after 1-2 weeks of buffering; they manufacture castings in the root zone all season
- Harvest worms in October — they cannot survive a Halifax winter in a fabric bed
- Insulate walls with straw bales and top with heavy mulch to slow freeze-thaw cycling
- Year 2 costs drop to $20-40 as the soil biology matures and the worm bin produces free castings
