# Bottomless Pots

> **Quick take:** Cut the bottom off a pot, grow a plant in it until the roots hold the soil, then set it directly on top of a living soil bed — no hole, no transplant shock, and roots that simply grow downward into the bed.

The bottomless-pot technique is a transplanting method developed by [[steve-cantwell|Steve Cantwell]] at Green Life Productions. It replaces the traditional dig-a-hole transplant with a "place, don't plant" workflow: a plant is started in a pot whose bottom has been removed, allowed to develop a root ball that retains the soil, and then set on top of an established [[reusing-soil|living soil]] bed. The roots continue down into the bed without interruption.

It is primarily a commercial-scale, high-ceiling technique, but the underlying principle — minimize root disturbance at transplant — applies broadly. See [[transplanting]] for the general science of why disturbance sets plants back.

## The Origin

Cantwell discovered it by accident. A seedling he forgot to transplant sat on a mother bed in a small pot, stressed and yellowing, then a week later had re-greened and blown up healthy — and was rooted into the bed beneath it. The parallel to the old rockwool-cube-on-slab method ("little cube on the big cube") suggested a way to eliminate the labor and mess of digging holes for hundreds of plants.

## How It Works

1. **Remove the pot bottom.** Cut the bottom off a plastic pot, or use a purpose-made bottomless fabric pot (Cantwell had Grassroots produce one). Early versions sat on a saucer during the start phase.
2. **Establish a root ball.** Grow the clone or seedling in the bottomless pot until roots develop enough to hold the soil column together at the base.
3. **Place, don't plant.** Clear any [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|mulch]] or living cover off a patch of bed so the pot sits on bare soil with a clean access point, then set the pot directly on top. No hole is dug.
4. **Let roots grow down.** Roots continue downward into the bed. The plant transitions seamlessly from pot to bed.

## What It Delivers

- **Labor savings.** Placing a room of plants dropped from a day and a half to a half day at GLP — the original and primary goal.
- **Minimal soil disturbance.** No holes, no mess, and consistent with no-till / living-soil philosophy. Contrast with the older practice of digging in half-gallon pots and harvesting root balls, which removed the worms, bacteria, and fungi nesting in the rhizosphere and set the biology "two steps back."
- **Faster, healthier growth — the surprise benefit.** Reduced transplant shock; plants take off faster and went into flower bigger and healthier, driving a substantial yield increase over time. On a fixed schedule it does not save calendar time, but it grows a much larger plant in the same window.

## Practical Tips

- **Clear the contact point.** Setting a pot on top of mulch or living cover causes rotting and decomposition in the root zone — expose bare soil first.
- **Water the pots directly at first.** For roughly the first two to two-and-a-half weeks, water the pots directly a couple of times a week. Capillary action from the bed isn't enough to wick water up into the pot until roots reach down. Once roots are in the bed, you can ignore the pots and water the bed.
- **Scale and ceiling height matter.** Best suited to large commercial grows with high-bay ceilings planting many plants per month. For home gardeners with limited ceiling height, the added height of a surface-set pot may be a drawback.
- **Try one bed or one pot first** before converting a whole operation.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> This technique was developed specifically for commercial living-soil cannabis. Leaving the bed undisturbed preserves the [[soil-food-web|soil biology]] between cycles, which pairs with Cantwell's practice of leaving old root balls in the bed (rotated via a grid-planting pattern) to feed worms and microbes. Note that regulators may be unfamiliar with the method — Nevada initially resisted bottomless pots before accepting them, which is one more reason Cantwell documents everything with SOPs.

## Key Takeaways

- A bottomless pot lets a rooted plant transition into a living bed with no hole and no root disruption
- Originally a labor-saving move (1.5 days → 0.5 day to place a room), it also reduced transplant shock and raised yields
- Expose bare soil at the contact point; setting pots on mulch rots the root zone
- Water pots directly for the first ~2 weeks until roots reach the bed, then water the bed
- Best for high-ceiling commercial grows; less suited to height-constrained home gardens
