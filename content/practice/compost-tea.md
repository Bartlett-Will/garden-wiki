# Compost Tea

> **Quick take:** Aerated compost tea extracts and multiplies beneficial microorganisms from compost, creating a living liquid you apply to soil or foliage to boost the microbial population in your garden.

Compost tea is a water-based extract of compost (or vermicompost) that uses aeration and agitation to strip microorganisms off compost particles and multiply them with a food source. The result contains the "fertilizer bags" (bacteria, fungi) and "fertilizer spreaders" (protozoa, nematodes) of the [[soil-food-web]] in liquid form, ready to apply.

## The Theory

Compost already contains everything the soil food web needs — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and the nutrients locked inside their bodies. Compost tea concentrates and multiplies these organisms so you can apply them precisely where needed.

The process works like this:
1. **Extraction** — agitation strips bacteria and fungi off compost particles (they stick via bacterial slime, the same biofilm you brush off your teeth each morning)
2. **Multiplication** — a food source (typically molasses) feeds the extracted microbes, causing rapid population growth
3. **Nutrient cycling activation** — as bacteria multiply, protozoa emerge from dormant cysts and begin feeding on them, releasing plant-available nutrients as waste

When applied as a drench or foliar spray, these microbes colonize the [[soil-food-web|rhizosphere]] and leaf surfaces, enhancing nutrient cycling and disease suppression.

## Brewing Basics

### What You Need
- **Compost or [[vermicomposting|vermicompost]]** — the microbial source. Quality matters enormously — a bag sitting on a shelf for six months at a hardware store won't match fresh worm castings from an active bin
- **Aeration** — air stones, air lifts, or venturi systems to maintain dissolved oxygen. Air lifts provide up to 10x the dissolved oxygen capacity of air stones alone
- **Food source** — unsulfured molasses is the simplest and most effective. It feeds both bacteria and fungi equally well
- **Water** — dechlorinated

### Timing Windows

The timing of your brew determines what you get — this is more important than the recipe:

| Hours | What's Happening | Best For |
|-------|-----------------|----------|
| 0–12 | Bacteria multiplying rapidly | Bacterial-dominant application |
| 12–24 | Fungi growing; protozoa starting to emerge from cysts | Balanced application |
| 24–36 | **Protozoa at peak** — active nutrient cycling occurring | Maximum nutrient cycling |
| 36–48 | Still usable but declining | Apply soon |
| 48+ | **Ciliates take over** — the tea degrades into "ciliate soup" | Discard and start over |

**36 hours is the sweet spot** if you don't have a microscope. This is when protozoa populations peak and active nutrient cycling is at its highest.

### Simple Recipe

Tim Wilson's testing showed that complex recipes don't necessarily produce better results than the basics:

- Good quality vermicompost or compost
- Unsulfured molasses
- Clean, aerated water

That's it. Adding rock phosphate, kelp meal, fish hydrolysate, and other amendments to the brew doesn't measurably improve the microbial population compared to simple molasses and good compost. If you want to add complexity, organic alfalfa meal pairs well with the base recipe.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> For cannabis, use soil from a previous cannabis grow as your compost tea base when possible. In theory, that soil contains exudates that attracted the specific microbes the cannabis plant wanted — so you're giving the new plant back the very microorganisms it would have recruited on its own. Apply as a soil drench throughout the growing cycle.

## Common Myths

### "Molasses grows E. coli"
This originated from two lab studies (USDA and Canadian) that *inoculated* compost tea with E. coli, then demonstrated it grew. Of course it did — if you put E. coli into aerated, food-rich water, it multiplies. This doesn't mean compost tea naturally produces E. coli. The resulting NOP guidance against molasses in compost tea was, in Tim Wilson's view, a knee-jerk reaction.

### "Good foam means good tea"
No consistent relationship exists between foam and microbial quality. Microscope examination shows well-foamed teas can be poor and flat teas can be excellent.

### "Brew for 72+ hours for maximum benefit"
Beyond 48 hours, ciliates (large protozoa associated with anaerobic conditions) take over. Perpetual brewers selling tea to customers face a quality consistency problem — right after adding fresh compost and food, the tea needs 24 hours to cycle back to being useful.

### "You can store compost tea in the fridge"
Microbes go dormant or die under refrigeration and don't come back when warmed. Even with continuous aeration, stored tea degrades within 3 days. **Brew fresh, use immediately.**

## Compost Extract — The Low-Effort Alternative

Not ready to invest in a brewer? Put compost in cheesecloth, knead it in water for 15–20 minutes. You won't get the multiplied populations of a proper brew, but you'll extract some of the beneficial microbes. Tim Wilson also documents a simple stir method on his Microbe Organics website that he's verified under the microscope.

## Using a Microscope

A microscope transforms compost tea from guesswork to science. You can:
- Verify bacterial and fungal diversity
- Confirm protozoa are present and active
- Time your application to the nutrient cycling peak
- Evaluate compost quality before brewing
- Compare recipes and methods with actual data

Sending samples to a lab is problematic — the microbial community changes during shipping, so results reflect transit time, not brew quality.

## Key Takeaways
- Quality compost in → quality tea out. Your own worm castings beat store-bought compost
- 36 hours is the target brew time for peak nutrient cycling
- Simple recipes (compost + molasses) work as well as complex ones
- Never brew beyond 48 hours — ciliates take over
- Use immediately; storage doesn't work
- Indigenous microbes from your own soil and compost are ideal
- A microscope is the best tool for evaluating tea quality
