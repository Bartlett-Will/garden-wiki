# Single Piece Flow and Efficiency

> **Quick take:** Completing all steps on one item before moving to the next (single piece flow) is consistently twice as fast as doing one step on everything first (batch-and-queue) — and this principle transforms garden and farm productivity.

Most people default to batch processing: harvest everything, then wash everything, then pack everything. It feels efficient because each step looks organized. But lean research — and [[ben-hartman]]'s farm experience — shows that single piece flow is reliably **2x faster** than batch-and-queue for nearly every task.

## Single Piece Flow vs. Batch-and-Queue

### Batch-and-Queue (the default)

1. Harvest all 50 heads of lettuce.
2. Carry them all to the wash station.
3. Wash all 50.
4. Carry them all to the packing area.
5. Pack all 50.

This seems logical, but at each transition you handle every item again. [[ben-hartman]] found his products were touched **7 times** between field and sale.

### Single Piece Flow

1. Harvest one head of lettuce.
2. Wash it immediately (bring wash station to the field or work adjacent).
3. Pack it.
4. Set it down — finished.
5. Move to the next.

The rule: **"Add as much value as possible to the product in your hand before you set it down."**

With single piece flow, Ben reduced product touches from 7 to 3-4 and **nearly doubled profits** — not from growing more, but from eliminating handling waste.

### Why it's faster

- **No batching overhead.** You don't lose time organizing, staging, and re-orienting between batch steps.
- **Immediate feedback.** If something's wrong (a damaged head, a quality issue), you catch it on the first unit instead of discovering it after processing 50.
- **Less physical handling.** Each item moves through its full journey once instead of being picked up, set down, picked up, set down.
- **Smaller workspace needed.** You don't need staging areas for 50 items between each step.

## Spaghetti Diagrams for Layout Optimization

A spaghetti diagram maps every physical movement you make during a work session. The technique:

1. Get a simple floor plan of your garden, farm, or grow room (hand-drawn is fine).
2. For 45-60 minutes, draw a line tracing every path you walk.
3. Look at the result. The tangled "noodles" reveal wasted motion.

Then systematically **shorten, straighten, or eliminate** each noodle:

- Can you move the tool storage closer to where the work happens? (See [[the-5s-system|5S — Set in Order]].)
- Can you rearrange beds so the harvest path is linear instead of zigzag?
- Can you bring the processing station to the field instead of carrying everything to a fixed location?

Spaghetti diagrams make invisible waste visible. Motion waste is the most common type of Muda on farms, and most growers are shocked by how much unnecessary walking they do.

## Deep Work and Flow States

Productivity research (Cal Newport's *Deep Work*) aligns with lean principles on a neurological level:

- **60-90 minute uninterrupted blocks** are the sweet spot for maximum productivity. This matches the "Shine" cycle in [[the-5s-system|5S]]: work for 60-90 minutes, then restore to zero.
- **The average office worker is interrupted every 15 minutes.** Gardens are better, but phone notifications, jumping between tasks, and unfocused wandering still fragment attention.
- **Multitasking changes neural pathways** — and not for the better. Each context switch has a recovery cost. Lean's emphasis on completing one task (or one item in single piece flow) before moving to the next is neurologically sound.

Protect your work blocks. Turn off your phone. Finish one bed before starting another.

## Customer Value Analysis

Lean asks three questions about every product, crop, or task:

1. **What does the customer want?** (Not what you think they should want.)
2. **When do they want it?**
3. **How much will they pay?**

For commercial growers, this means getting orders before buying seeds — mapping every crop to a buyer. For home gardeners, the "customer" is your household: grow what you'll actually eat, not what looked interesting in the seed catalog.

### Owner Value Distortion

One of the most common traps: the owner's fascination overrides what the customer actually needs. [[tad-hussey]] shares his own example — building a permaculture nursery that nobody wanted. The energy, space, and time were real costs, but the demand wasn't there.

The lean check: if you removed this crop or task, would anyone besides you notice?

## Annual 5% Expense Reduction

A simple, compound discipline:

1. Print your full expense ledger at year-end.
2. Identify and cut 5% of costs — not by buying cheap, but by questioning whether each expense adds value.
3. Repeat annually.

Over 10 years, this compounds to roughly a **50% reduction** in expenses. The key is doing it systematically each year, not in one dramatic slash.

## The Five Whys: Root Cause Analysis

When something goes wrong, ask "why" five times to reach the root cause instead of fixing symptoms:

1. **Why** did the lettuce bolt? → It got too hot.
2. **Why** did it get too hot? → The shade cloth wasn't deployed.
3. **Why** wasn't the shade cloth deployed? → Nobody checked the forecast.
4. **Why** doesn't anyone check the forecast? → There's no morning checklist.
5. **Why** is there no morning checklist? → We never formalized the observation routine.

Root cause: missing a standardized [[field-observation|morning observation routine]], not "the weather was hot."

[[ben-hartman]] tells the story of a greenhouse built on a roof — the Five Whys traced a cascade of problems back to a single design decision that seemed minor at the time.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Single piece flow and lean efficiency techniques have direct applications in cannabis operations:
>
> - **Harvest processing** is the highest-value application. Instead of cutting all plants, then trimming all plants, then drying all plants — process each plant completely through cut, trim, and hang before moving to the next. This reduces handling damage to trichomes and catches quality issues immediately.
> - **Trimming workflow** benefits enormously from single piece flow. Trim one branch completely (removing fan leaves, shaping, inspecting for mold or pests) before setting it down and starting the next. Batch trimming leads to uneven quality and missed defects.
> - **Spaghetti diagrams in grow rooms** often reveal that growers walk past the same plants 4-5 times per session for different tasks (watering, inspecting, defoliating, adjusting). A single-piece-flow approach — completing all tasks for one plant or one section before moving on — cuts total time significantly.
> - **Customer-driven strain selection** applies the value analysis: grow what sells (or what you actually consume), not what has the most exotic name. Owner value distortion is rampant in cannabis, where growers chase rare genetics that underperform reliable cultivars.
> - **Five Whys for crop failures:** When a harvest disappoints, resist the urge to blame one factor. Trace the chain of causation back through environment, nutrition, genetics, and workflow.

## Key Takeaways

- Single piece flow is consistently 2x faster than batch-and-queue — complete all steps on one item before moving to the next
- "Add as much value as possible to the product in your hand before you set it down"
- Spaghetti diagrams (45-60 min of tracing your movement) make invisible motion waste visible — then shorten, straighten, or eliminate each path
- Protect 60-90 minute deep work blocks — multitasking and interruptions have real neurological costs
- Every crop or task should connect to a customer need, not just the owner's interest
- Cut 5% of expenses annually — it compounds to 50% reduction over a decade
- Use the Five Whys to reach root causes instead of treating symptoms
