# Composting and Organic Inputs

> **Quick take:** Compost is powerful but carries real contamination risks — know your source, test your inputs, and understand that composting is a biological reset, not a guarantee of purity.

Compost and organic amendments are foundational to building healthy [[soil-food-web|soil biology]]. But "organic" and "natural" do not mean "safe." As both [[tad-hussey|Tad Hussey]] and [[lee-breeze|Lee Breeze]] emphasize across multiple episodes, untested compost is the single highest contamination risk in most growing operations. The goal is not to avoid compost — it is to use it intelligently, with eyes open about what can go wrong.

## Contamination Risks

Compost can carry a surprisingly long list of contaminants. Understanding these risks helps you make informed sourcing decisions.

### Heavy Metals

Municipal compost and biosolids (treated sewage sludge) can contain lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury, and other heavy metals. These metals do not break down during composting — they concentrate. Heavy metals accumulate in soil over repeated applications and can be taken up by plants, entering the food chain. Sources include industrial waste, painted materials, treated lumber, and urban runoff.

### PFAS (Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances)

"Forever chemicals" that persist through composting, resist biological breakdown, and accumulate in soil and plant tissue. PFAS enter compost through biosolids, food packaging, and contaminated water sources. This is an emerging and poorly regulated area — many compost facilities do not test for PFAS.

### Pathogens

Raw manure contains human-transmissible pathogens including E. coli, Salmonella, and Listeria. Proper composting (sustained temperatures above 131F/55C for defined periods) kills these organisms. This is what [[lee-breeze|Lee Breeze]] calls the "biological reset" — composting transforms manure from a pathogen risk into a relatively safe soil amendment. The key word is "proper" — undercooked compost still carries risk.

### Microplastics

Increasingly found in municipal compost, especially from programs that accept compostable packaging (which often isn't fully compostable). Microplastics persist in soil indefinitely and their long-term effects on soil biology and plant health are still being studied.

### Fungus Gnats and Pest Organisms

Poorly composted material can introduce fungus gnats, root aphids, and other pest organisms directly into your growing environment. This is especially problematic for indoor growers where pest pressure is managed through exclusion.

### Antibiotic Residues

Manure from livestock treated with antibiotics can carry residues that suppress beneficial soil bacteria. The composting process reduces but may not eliminate all antibiotic compounds.

## Composting as Biological Reset

The composting process, when done correctly, is a powerful biological transformation. Sustained thermophilic temperatures (131F/55C+) kill human pathogens, weed seeds, and many pest organisms. The microbial community shifts dramatically through the process, breaking down complex organic compounds and stabilizing nutrients.

For manure specifically, composting is essential — not optional. Raw manure applied to growing areas carries unacceptable pathogen risk for any crop consumed by humans. The biological reset of proper composting eliminates these pathogens while preserving the nutrient value and organic matter benefits.

However, composting does not eliminate:
- Heavy metals (these concentrate)
- PFAS (biologically stable)
- Microplastics (physically persistent)
- Some persistent herbicides (aminopyralid, clopyralid, picloram)

This is why source matters more than process for these contaminants.

## Aerated Compost Tea

Aerated compost tea (ACT) is a liquid extract produced by steeping compost in aerated water, typically for 24-48 hours. The purpose is to multiply beneficial microorganisms from the compost and apply them as a soil or foliar inoculant.

Key principles:

- **Use as inoculant, not fertilizer** — Compost tea introduces biology, not significant nutrients. If you're relying on it for fertility, you're using the wrong tool.
- **Quality depends on the compost** — The tea can only contain organisms present in the starting compost. Contaminated compost makes contaminated tea.
- **Minimize raw compost volume** — By extracting biology into tea, you get the microbial benefits while limiting the physical compost volume (and its associated contamination risks) that you add to your system.

ACT is one tool in the biological toolkit, alongside targeted [[biocontrols-and-ipm|biocontrol]] inoculants. It complements, not replaces, products like lab-grown high-CFU strains (e.g., Microbial Mass, Mammoth P) that deliver specific organisms at guaranteed concentrations.

## When to Minimize Raw Compost Volume

There are situations where reducing the total amount of compost in your system is the smart move:

- **Indoor/container growing** — You control the entire environment. Every input is magnified. Less volume means less risk of introducing contaminants or pests.
- **Unknown source material** — If you can't verify what went into the compost, use less of it and supplement biology through targeted inoculants and compost tea instead.
- **High-value or regulated crops** — When contamination has legal or economic consequences, minimizing uncontrolled inputs is basic risk management.
- **Already high-OM soil** — If your soil or media already has high organic matter and CEC, additional compost adds diminishing returns for fertility while maintaining full contamination risk.

The pattern: use compost strategically for what it does best (introducing diverse biology, building organic matter in depleted soil) while managing the risks through source verification, testing, and volume control.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> For cannabis growers, compost contamination is not just a health concern — it's a compliance issue. Certificate of Analysis (COA) testing for heavy metals, pesticides, and microbial contaminants is required in most regulated markets. Using untested compost inputs is, as Tad puts it, "playing Russian roulette" with your COA results. A failed test means destroyed product and lost revenue. Source every input from tested, certified suppliers. If you make your own compost, test it before use. The stakes are too high for assumptions. PFAS contamination is an emerging concern that most state testing programs haven't caught up with yet — staying ahead of regulation by testing now is smart business.

## Key Takeaways

- Compost is the highest contamination risk in most organic growing operations — know your source
- Composting kills pathogens (the "biological reset") but does not eliminate heavy metals, PFAS, or microplastics
- Raw manure must be composted before use — this is a food safety requirement, not a preference
- Use aerated compost tea as a biological inoculant, not a fertilizer
- Minimize raw compost volume in indoor and container systems where you control the environment
- When in doubt, test your compost before applying it to your growing area
- Targeted microbial inoculants (lab-grown, high-CFU) complement compost tea for biological management
