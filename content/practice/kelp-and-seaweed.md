# Kelp and Seaweed Inputs

> **Quick take:** Kelp meal in the soil is the foundation; seaweed extract is a convenient soluble supplement that has lost most of what makes kelp special — know which one you're buying and why.

Kelp is [[clackamas-coot|Clackamas Coot]]'s flagship example of a complete, honest input in a market full of repackaged commodities. Humans have hauled seaweed out of the ocean for agriculture for centuries — especially island cultures like Ireland, where farming under resource constraints made seaweed central to soil fertility and livestock feed.

## Kelp Meal vs. Seaweed Extract — Not the Same Thing

An international treaty signed in the late 1950s (renewed since) legally defines what may be called **kelp** versus **seaweed extract**. The distinction matters:

- **Kelp meal** is the dried, milled plant. Everything is still in it — trace minerals, vitamins, plant hormones, carbohydrates, and saponins.
- **Seaweed extract** is produced chemically. Under USDA NOP rules, seaweed extracts must be made with **sodium or potassium hydroxide** — strongly alkaline chemistry. The vitamins and plant hormones don't survive. Coot's analogy: buying a car and getting only the frame and the motor.

That doesn't make extracts useless. They are balanced, **100% soluble** fertilizers, trivially easy to apply, and — as [[tad-hussey|Tad Hussey]] points out — they're what's actually inside many bottled nutrient products, which makes straight extract powder an honest, cheap way to **transition growers off bottles**. Look for cold-water-processed *Ascophyllum nodosum* (North Atlantic kelp); Acadian Sea Plants is the benchmark, and several house brands are the identical product.

One caution from Coot: he won't use seaweed extract in [[compost-tea]]. Given the hydroxide processing, he doesn't trust every grain to be free of alkaline traces, and the whole point of a tea is growing microbial colonies. For teas, use the meal — see [[botanical-teas]].

## Why Kelp Meal Earns Its Place

- It's a **bio-nutrient accumulator** — a broad, balanced spectrum of elements in plant-available form (see [[dynamic-accumulators]])
- At roughly $1.50/lb ($99–100 per 50 lb bag) it's cheap next to bottled products costing $90 per cup
- It's the same material sold to far bigger markets: most kelp goes to **livestock feed and biomedical uses**, not gardening. Show dogs, racehorses, and dairy herds eat kelp; holistic pet stores sell the identical meal powdered into capsules at $36/lb

Coot feeds kelp to his bulldog daily; Tad adds a quarter cup per 20 pounds of chicken feed, which measurably improves egg nutrition.

## The Research Base

The deep literature is the life's work of **Dr. T.L. Senn of Clemson University**, who spent 55 years studying kelp in agriculture and horticulture. His self-published book is sold by AcresUSA for about $15 (Amazon has listed it at $70), and his research repository is accessible through university library systems. Also worth reading: *Food Power from the Sea*. Avoid judging kelp by cheap eBay "kelp" — if it doesn't smell like the ocean, it isn't the real thing, and bad product produces the online myth that "kelp ruined my plants."

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Kelp meal is a core amendment in [[the-coot-mix]] and most living-soil cannabis recipes — typically incorporated up front and refreshed when [[reusing-soil|reamending]]. For growers moving away from bottled nutrient lines, cold-processed seaweed extract powder is the pragmatic bridge: it's soluble like the bottles they're used to, it's actually what many bottles contain, and it costs a fraction as much.

## Key Takeaways

- Kelp meal = whole plant; seaweed extract = hydroxide-processed derivative without the hormones and vitamins
- Use kelp meal in soil as the foundation; use extract only as a supplemental soluble fertilizer
- Don't put seaweed extract in compost tea — alkaline residue risk; use the meal instead
- Cold-water-processed *Ascophyllum nodosum* is the quality standard
- Most kelp goes to livestock and biomedical markets; gardeners are a rounding error
- T.L. Senn's 55 years of Clemson research is the authoritative source — buy the book from AcresUSA
