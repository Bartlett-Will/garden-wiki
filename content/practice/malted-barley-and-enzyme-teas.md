# Malted Barley and Enzyme Teas

> **Quick take:** Malted (sprouted) barley from a home-brew store is a cheap, enzyme-rich soil amendment — the enzymes act as catalysts that deconstruct organic matter, feed fungi, accelerate worm bins, and unlock the chitin already in your soil.

[[clackamas-coot|Clackamas Coot]] introduced malted grains to cannabis horticulture by way of an unlikely path: bread baking. Artisan "lean doughs" use **diastatic malt** — barley that has been sprouted and dried without killing its enzymes — to boost yeast activity. Coot realized the same enzyme package could work in soil, and the practice spread from there. (Non-diastatic products like barley extract have had their enzymes destroyed by heat — they still contain sugars, but not the catalytic activity that makes malt valuable.)

## What the Enzymes Do

Biologists describe enzymes as **catalysts**: they don't feed plants directly, they accelerate the deconstruction of organic material into forms microbes and plants can use. Malted barley contains roughly eight enzymes. Brewers only care about one — amylase, which converts starch to sugar — but the others matter more in soil:

- **Amylase** — breaks carbohydrates into simple sugars (the same enzyme in your saliva)
- **Protease** — breaks down proteins (in bread, too much protease destroys gluten structure; in soil, that's not a concern)
- **Urease** — acts on urea
- **Phosphatase** — acts on phosphorus compounds, of perennial interest to growers worried about phosphorus availability
- **Chitinase** — the standout (see below)

Coot interviewed more than 20 brewmasters and malting-house founders; none had heard of chitinase, phosphatase, or urease in their own product. Brewing literature won't teach you this application.

### The Chitinase Connection

Chitin — the polysaccharide that forms insect eggshells and exoskeletons and fungal cell walls — does little in soil by itself. As soil bacteria work to deconstruct chitin, **chitinase** is produced, and the chitinase delivers the benefits: deconstructing insect eggs and supporting the plant's defense signaling. The chitinase–salicylic acid pathway is one of several routes into **systemic acquired resistance (SAR)** — the plant immune priming described in [[plant-stress-responses]]. (This is also why Coot uses quality aloe vera — salicylic acid is a key component of several defense pathways.)

Practical consequence: Coot largely dropped crab meal from [[the-coot-mix|his soil mix]] — malted barley supplies the chitin/chitinase activity at a fraction of the cost.

## Observed Results

With a 33-year-old genetic control (the same plant grown from mother cuts), Coot reports — and many growers have independently echoed:

- **Faster flowering finish** — roughly 15% (some report 15–20%)
- **Increased resin set** — "off the chart"
- **Greater branch girth and tensile strength** — less support needed under heavy flowers
- **Accelerated rooting** — about ½ teaspoon sprinkled on top after transplanting rooted cuttings, watered in
- **Explosive fungal growth** — in a public-garden trial where whole (unground) malted barley was top-dressed with castings, mycelium visibly exploded around each intact grain within two weeks. Malted grain is an outstanding fungal food
- **Worm bin acceleration** — see [[vermicomposting]]: Doug at Northwest Red Worms saw the best worm reproduction rates of his career in a windrow amended with nothing but malted barley

## How to Buy and Use It

1. **Buy at a home-brew store.** Any malted grain works — barley, rye, oats, wheat, spelt. Buy on price; distressed sacks are fine. **Rye** is slightly higher in phosphorus if that's your focus.
2. **Skip six-row barley.** American six-row has more amylase than two-row but no more of the enzymes that matter for soil, and costs more. Two-row is fine.
3. **Grind it.** Most brew stores grind for a small fee. Finer grind = more surface area = faster microbial breakdown. Use fine grind for top-dressing (water it in); coarser is fine when mixing soil or feeding compost and worm bins.
4. **Dose modestly.** A light sprinkle (e.g., ½ tsp for a fresh transplant) goes a long way, though outdoor growers with 200-gallon pots have used pounds per week without harm. Don't mix it with harsh additions like ammonia and then blame the barley.
5. **Do not substitute spent brewery grain.** Fermentation has already transformed it. It's fine in compost, but it is not the same input and "you can really get into trouble" using it as if it were.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Malted barley earned its reputation in cannabis: faster flowering finishes (~15%), heavier resin set, and stronger branches that need less trellising late in flower. Top-dress finely ground malt and water it in, or add it generously to worm bins and soil mixes. It also replaces crab meal as the chitin source in living soil recipes — relevant for growers watching input costs. As always, evaluate changes against a known genetic baseline; clone-run consistency is what made these effects credible in the first place.

## Key Takeaways

- Malting just means sprouting — the grain is germinated, which activates its enzyme package
- Diastatic = enzymes alive; non-diastatic (extracts) = enzymes destroyed
- Enzymes are catalysts: they accelerate decomposition, they aren't fertilizer
- Chitinase deconstructs insect eggs and feeds into SAR defense pathways
- Any malted grain from a brew store works; buy on price; grind fine for top-dressing
- Malted barley is a powerful fungal food and worm-bin accelerant
- Never swap in spent brewery grain and expect the same results
