# Vermicomposting

> **Quick take:** Worms convert organic waste into biologically rich castings that outperform commercial compost — especially when the worms are "neglected" and forced to thoroughly process their food rather than constantly fed fresh material.

Vermicompost (worm castings) consistently shows superior microbial diversity and biomass compared to thermophilic compost or commercial bagged products. It's the gold standard input for [[compost-tea|compost tea brewing]] and a cornerstone amendment for living soil systems.

## Why Worm Castings Are Superior

Tim Wilson's microscope work consistently shows that vermicompost produces better microbial results than other compost sources. The key appears to be how thoroughly the material has been processed — worms don't just break down organic matter, they inoculate it with their gut microbiome, creating a uniquely diverse microbial community.

The quality difference between homemade and commercial castings can be dramatic. A bag of compost sitting on a store shelf for months will have far less biological activity than fresh castings from an active worm bin.

## The "Neglect" Method

Tim Wilson's most successful vermicomposting approach runs counter to conventional wisdom about keeping worms well-fed:

1. **Set up the bin** with a rich initial mix (see below)
2. **Stop adding fresh food** after the initial setup
3. **Force the worms to redigest** what's already there — they process every scrap instead of always reaching for new, easy food
4. **Leave it for a long time** — months, not weeks

The result is a gummy, dark material that looks quite different from the granular castings sold in bags. Wilson believes the forced redigestion produces superior microbial diversity because the worms must extract nutrition from increasingly processed material, driving deeper biological transformation.

## Initial Mix Components

Tim Wilson's recipe for the initial worm bedding:
- **Horse manure** — semi-dried, collected from the field
- **Peat moss** — high CEC, good moisture retention
- **Wheat bran** — cheap bulk food source (from livestock supply stores)
- **Alfalfa meal** — no more than 5% of the mix (excess nitrates kill worms)
- **Kelp meal** — as much as budget allows for trace minerals
- **Sawdust and straw** — occasional additions for carbon balance

For kitchen-scale systems, the same principles apply with household scraps substituted for farm inputs, but the neglect period still matters.

## Accelerants: Neem and Malted Barley

[[clackamas-coot|Clackamas Coot]]'s trials (ep-003) found two amendments that dramatically boost worm bins:

- **Neem meal** added to the bedding produced remarkable increases in both worm reproduction rates and worm length and girth
- **[[malted-barley-and-enzyme-teas|Malted barley]]** worked even more dramatically: Doug at Northwest Red Worms added nothing but malted barley to an aged-manure windrow and saw the highest worm reproduction rates of his career. The grain's enzymes act as catalysts that deconstruct the material, multiplying the microbes the worms actually feed on

Coot's own setup: 100-gallon fabric pots on pallets (bottom aeration), three-year-old black leaf mold layered lasagna-style with kelp meal, malted barley, neem, and karanja. Because vermicomposting is a **cold process**, the secondary metabolites in neem and karanja survive into the finished castings — preserving their insect- and disease-suppression properties, which thermophilic composting would burn off.

## Bin Fundamentals: Water Is the Enemy, Mass Is the Answer

Coot is blunt about the most common failure mode (ep-007): a Rubbermaid tote with holes drilled in it, fed kitchen scraps, won't produce vermicompost. Recycling scraps is virtuous — but it isn't vermicomposting.

- **Hydration level is the enemy.** Excess water — the inevitable result of scrap-heavy totes — ruins the process.
- **You need mass**: a real volume of thermophilic compost or quality manure as the bin's body. Source manure carefully — organic dairy or well-kept horse manure (animals fed kelp and minerals pass that quality along), not bagged steer manure.
- **The neem claim is microscope-verified.** When KIS Organics ran 200-gallon bins amended with neem meal and basalt per Coot's recommendation, [[tad-hussey|Tad Hussey]]'s microscopy showed the neem cake *increased* beneficial bacteria and fungi — debunking the worry that neem inhibits bin biology. See [[neem-and-karanja]].
- **Feed it your tea muck.** Spent meal from [[botanical-teas]] still holds half its nutrients — the worms recover what the water didn't extract.

Even a 20-gallon pot works as a starter bin. If you'd rather buy than build, find a local producer — worm farms serving anglers can often be talked into making castings, and fresh local material beats anything bagged.

## Worm Selection and Foodstock

- **Red wigglers** work the top of the bedding; **European nightcrawlers** stay low and finish material thoroughly — good in static bins, the wrong choice for vertical flow-through systems
- **Cocoons** are the budget route: ~3.8 worms per cocoon on average, hatchlings reach sexual maturity in 42 days, then produce ~3 cocoons per week. Under ideal conditions (Edwards, *Vermiculture Technology*), a pound of worms could in theory become a thousand pounds in a year
- **Aged manure** makes excellent foodstock. If the animals were given vermicides, age the manure 4–6 weeks — the vermicides deconstruct and are no longer a problem
- Even mediocre bagged compost comes out good after a pass through the worms — the vermiculture cycle is a value-add on whatever you start with

## Casting Quality

Castings leave the worm's digestive tract coated in a **calcium carbonate slime**, which buffers them — raw, unamended castings from a well-run windrow test around pH 6.8. Combined with their microbial diversity, this is why quality castings are the linchpin of soil recipes like [[the-coot-mix]]: "you don't have my castings" is Coot's standard answer to people who substitute bagged products and blame the recipe.

## Harvesting

Wilson's method for separating worms from castings:
1. Fill plastic mesh transplant trays with the worms' favorite food
2. Cover with wet newspaper
3. Lay trays across the top of the worm pile
4. Worms crawl up into the trays over a day or two
5. Remove trays, transfer worms to a new bin
6. Harvest the castings

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis growers who reuse soil (see [[reusing-soil]]) can maintain a worm bin fed with cannabis stems and leaf trim. The resulting castings contain microbes already adapted to decomposing cannabis plant material, which may provide a head start when those castings are used as a top-dress or [[compost-tea]] input for the next grow cycle.

## Using Vermicompost

- **Soil amendment** — mix into potting soil or top-dress existing beds
- **[[Compost tea]]** — the ideal microbial source for brewing. Vermicompost + molasses produces excellent microbial diversity with no additional ingredients needed
- **Seed starting** — small amounts in seed-starting mix provide gentle biological activity
- **Living soil maintenance** — top-dress between crop cycles to replenish biology

## Key Takeaways
- Homemade worm castings consistently outperform commercial products under the microscope
- The "neglect" approach — letting worms thoroughly process a rich initial mix — produces superior results
- Keep alfalfa meal below 5% to avoid nitrate toxicity to worms
- Neem meal and malted barley dramatically boost worm reproduction and casting quality
- Cold processing preserves neem/karanja secondary metabolites that hot composting destroys
- Red wigglers work the top of the bedding; European nightcrawlers finish material down low
- Quality castings come out near-neutral (≈pH 6.8), coated in calcium carbonate
- Vermicompost is the best possible input for compost tea brewing
- A diverse initial mix (multiple mineral sources, varied organic matter) produces more diverse biology
- Overhydration is the top failure mode — bins need mass (compost or quality manure), not kitchen scraps in a tote
- Spent botanical tea muck is ideal worm food — nothing is wasted
