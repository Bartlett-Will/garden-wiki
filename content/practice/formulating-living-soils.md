# Formulating Living Soils

> **Quick take:** A good soil mix isn't a recipe you copy — it's a process: pick quality inputs you can actually get, balance everything around your compost, then iterate through lab tests and plant trials until the numbers and the plants agree.

Google "soil recipe" and you'll find thousands of them, mostly slight variations on the same themes. What the recipes don't tell you is why they worked for their author — and why they may not work for you. When [[tad-hussey]] and [[rob-knechtel]] collaborated on the Canadian "KISS Mix" (a two-to-three-year project, after Tad's own US mix took five or six years to nail down), the value wasn't the final ingredient list. It was the process, and that process is what this page captures.

## The Three Pillars

When formulating a soil, evaluate it along three dimensions:

1. **Physical properties** — How well does it drain? What is its [[soil-structure-and-aggregates|CEC]] and ability to hold nutrients? What is its water saturation capacity? Structure deserves the number one position on the list: get it wrong and nothing else matters.
2. **Biological properties** — What microbiology is in the mix? The [[soil-food-web|microbes]] are what cycle nutrients into plant-available forms, so you want diverse, biologically rich sources. Tad evaluated Rob's compost samples both under the microscope and through lab tests.
3. **Chemical properties** — The mineral content of every input, established by lab analysis, so the totals can be balanced.

Only after you understand all three for your candidate inputs can you decide what else the mix needs.

## Formulate Around Your Compost

For potting and living soils, **start with your compost and worm castings** — they are what you're formulating around. The compost determines how much of every other input goes in, because it arrives carrying its own (highly variable) nutrients and biology. Source the highest quality you can get and afford (see [[composting-and-organic-inputs]]).

This is also why copying internet recipes disappoints: the author balanced their recipe around *their* compost sources, and you don't have them. [[clackamas-coot|Clackamas Coot]] says the same about [[the-coot-mix|his own mix]] — you're not growing in Coot's mix if you're not using his castings.

Diversity helps. The KISS Mix base uses three compost sources — a fish compost, a leaf compost, and worm castings — later joined by a horse compost from a producer who had studied under [[dr-elaine-ingham|Elaine Ingham]]. Between the peat and the composts, that's where the biological life of the soil comes from.

## The Base Mix

The common starting ratio is a third peat, a third compost, a third aeration — and plenty of people grow well with it. Tad's preference:

- **~20% compost** (split across diverse sources)
- **~33–34% aeration**
- **The balance sphagnum peat** — for water retention paired with good aeration

**Choosing aeration is a sourcing decision as much as a horticultural one.** Black Swallow's first mixes used rice hulls — reasonable in theory, with a silica bonus — but unless perfectly blended, rice hulls channel water and leave dry pockets in the mix. Perlite won on performance, price, and availability. Pumice, excellent where it's local (the Pacific Northwest), is a "luxury amendment" in Ontario that must come in by the truckload from BC or the States; the freight costs more than the value it adds.

Peat is naturally hydrophobic, so a well-amended mix takes patience to rehydrate: small doses of water with time between them. Black Swallow adds **yucca** as a surfactant to help (see [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship]]).

## Sourcing and Vetting Inputs

The unglamorous foundation of a good soil:

- **Buy from producers, not distributors,** wherever possible. Rob traveled to facilities in multiple provinces to weigh one producer against another.
- **Lab-test inputs** — especially for **heavy metals**. Anything mined (rock dusts above all) and any compost is a potential source, and regulated crops must pass heavy-metal testing (see [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis]]).
- **Judge compost on its biology, not just its nutrient analysis.** Many suppliers see compost as broken-down organic matter or an NPK number; for living soil, the biological contribution matters as much or more.
- **Let geography shape the recipe.** Ontario's proximity to the Canadian Shield makes interesting rock dusts cheap and abundant there — so the KISS Mix runs heavier on rock dust than its US counterpart. Sustainability and freight belong in the formula alongside agronomy.

## The Testing Loop

Formulation is iterative, and it costs money:

1. **Mix** a batch to the candidate recipe
2. **Lab test** the finished soil
3. **Tweak** the recipe based on the numbers
4. **Repeat** until the balance looks right — it took "quite a few tests" even with two experienced formulators starting close to the goal
5. **Plant trials** — only once the numbers are right do plants enter the picture

Two rules make the loop work. First, **stay with the same lab** — results are not comparable across labs, so consistency is what lets you correlate batch to batch. Logan Labs (Mehlich 3 extraction plus saturated paste) is the de facto standard in the US; Rob uses A&L Labs in Ontario, and Tad calibrated A&L results against Logan before trusting them. Second, know what tests your lab runs — the methodology matters more than the brand. For larger producers, **sap and tissue testing** extend the loop into the growing cycle, letting you adjust fertility mid-crop.

You will not nail it on the first mix. Nobody does.

## Complexity, With Balance

The finished KISS Mix contains **19 amendments**, more than most recipes dare — and Rob found the complexity enhances performance. But this is the opposite of the "kitchen sink" approach from the early days of soil recipes, where more was assumed better. The difference is balance: every amendment is there because the lab numbers, the compost analysis, and the plant trials justified it. Throw everything at a soil without that discipline and you get an unbalanced mess you can't diagnose.

A related discipline for growers: when trying new inputs, change one variable at a time and trial on a portion of your plants, not everything at once (see [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis]] on on-farm experimentation). Otherwise you can't tell which input helped, which hurt — and if a crop fails a heavy-metal test, tracing the culprit through a dozen "secret sauces" is nearly impossible. The money is usually better spent on tools you'll use every grow: a PAR meter, a moisture meter, a microscope.

## Seed Starts and Young Plants

Seedlings and rooted clones need very little nutrition, and a hot mix can actively slow them down. The standard advice: dilute the full-strength mix 50/50 with plain peat for seed starting, and step up to full strength once roots are established (see [[transplanting]]).

## "Water Only" in Context

Living soils are often marketed as "water only," and the term deserves unpacking. Taken literally it implies the soil holds the ideal balance of every nutrient from sprout to harvest, independent of the genetics you're growing — a huge demand no mix can fully meet. What it really means: a beginner can get a very decent harvest from a quality living soil by doing nothing but watering well. The term originated with Tad's founding idea for KISS — that a medical patient could buy a bag of soil, water it, and succeed.

Experienced growers move beyond it: targeted teas and top-dressings at the right growth stages, tuned to the cultivar, will outperform water alone. Both things are true.

## Vegan and Veganic Mixes

Some growers want mixes free of animal products — no blood, bone, or feather meal. It's formulatable, with caveats: most vegan inputs are low in nitrogen and phosphorus, so hitting targets costs more. The category lines are also murky (insect frass and worm castings pass for some vegans, not others). The counterargument Rob and Tad both raise: slaughterhouse and fishery byproducts are waste streams that become landfill if not returned to soil, so a sustainability-focused line (avoiding industrial food chain products specifically) may be more coherent than a strict vegan one.

## Prilling

A production note: prilling — binding powdered amendments with lignin into consistent granules — greatly cuts dust and makes blended nutrient products safer and easier to handle. No agronomic change, purely a handling improvement.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> "Cannabis" is a classification covering enormous genetic variety, and cultivars pull nutrients at very different rates — growers call them heavy versus light feeders. No mix is perfect for all of them: start with a well-formulated premium soil, then tweak per cultivar, and expect different cultivars to leave the soil in different states at reamendment time (see [[reusing-soil]]).
>
> Heavy-metal vetting of inputs is a compliance issue, not just a health one — flower must pass heavy-metal testing in regulated markets, and rock dusts and compost are the most likely import routes (see [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis]]).
>
> The most common failure mode Rob sees daily: growers from hydroponic backgrounds who keep "one foot in each pool," carrying bottle-nutrient habits and assumptions into living soil. The two systems need different mental models — in living soil, the biology does the feeding, and the grower's main jobs are moisture management and observation. Genetics, finally, play one of the biggest roles in yield and cannabinoid content — a premium soil cannot rescue mediocre genetics.

## Key Takeaways

- Evaluate any mix on three pillars: physical structure, biology, and mineral chemistry — structure first
- Formulate around your compost; it sets the levels for everything else, which is why copied recipes disappoint
- Base ratio: third/third/third works; Tad prefers ~20% compost, ~33% aeration, balance peat
- Source from producers, vet by visiting, and lab-test inputs — especially mined inputs and compost for heavy metals
- Iterate: mix → lab test → tweak → repeat → plant trials; use the same lab every time
- Complexity helps only when balanced — 19 disciplined amendments beat 12 random products
- Dilute mixes 50/50 with peat for seed starts; young plants don't need full-strength nutrition
- "Water only" is a beginner-friendly simplification, not a ceiling
- Change one variable at a time; spend spare money on tools, not more inputs
