# Soil Remineralization

> **Quick take:** Test your soil, compare the numbers to research-based targets, and add the specific minerals that are missing — instead of hoping compost alone covers it.

> [!info] Conflicting views: do base-cation saturation ratios (BCSR) matter?
> **Steve Solomon** (ep-006) builds his amendment program on Albrecht-style base-cation *ratios* read from a standard soil test. **Bryant Mason** (ep-072/108), on [[soilless-media-vs-topsoil]], argues those standard-test ratios are misleading in clay-free peat media — there are no colloid "docking sites," so chasing BCSR over-amends; he balances off saturated-paste percentages instead. The two largely reconcile by substrate: Solomon's targets suit mineral field soil, Bryant's critique targets low-CEC containers.

Soil remineralization is the practice of restoring a full spectrum of minerals — not just N-P-K, but calcium, magnesium, potassium, and trace elements like zinc, copper, manganese, and boron — to levels that produce healthy plants *and* nutrient-dense food (see [[nutrient-density]] for why this matters). The approach popularized by [[steve-solomon]] in *The Intelligent Gardener* is built on a simple loop: soil test → compare to targets → amend → retest.

## Start with Targets, Not Tests

Solomon's first advice: the soil test is just a tool. Decide what you're trying to accomplish first.

The intellectual foundation comes from William Albrecht, who worked out the rough relationships between calcium, magnesium, potassium, and phosphorus that let a plant produce abundant protein and full health — such that livestock eating the crop also thrived. Later farm advisors built on Albrecht's work into a fertilization philosophy called **base cation balancing**: bring nutrient levels and ratios to the point that optimizes the health of whatever eats the crop, while also producing high yields and stress-resistant plants. Maintaining soil organic matter is part of the system — it only works on healthy, biologically active land.

### Sufficiency vs. the Ethical Approach

Two competing fertilization philosophies dominate:

- **Sufficiency (economically rational):** add nutrients only up to the point where more no longer pays for itself in yield. Conventional soil test recommendations are built this way — they target *no visible deficiency*, not optimal composition.
- **Base cation balancing (ethical):** target the levels and ratios that maximize the nutritional quality of the food, even when the extra copper or zinc does nothing for yield. Ten times the sufficiency level of a trace element may change the crop's phytonutrient content without changing how it looks.

A grower feeding their own family — or selling a premium crop — has every reason to choose the second philosophy. The inputs cost more; for a garden or a high-value crop, the cost is trivial.

## The Soil Test

Modern testing is cheap and good. Solomon uses **Spectrum Analytic** (~$14 US for a standard test on 60 g of soil); **Logan Labs** (~$25) presents results in a more amateur-friendly format. Don't agonize over lab choice:

- Different labs may produce numbers that differ by ~10% across the board
- What matters is the **relationship between the numbers** — the ratios — and those read the same from either lab

See [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis]] for sampling technique and how to read the rest of the report.

## Rock Dust: The Skeptic's Math

Rock dust is the iconic remineralization amendment, but Solomon — despite remineralization being his life's work — is skeptical of it as a trace element source, based on direct experience with Cascades basalt dust:

- A ton of basalt dust at 3 ppm zinc contains about **3 grams** of zinc, locked in slowly weathering mineral
- A target application of soluble zinc is on the order of **7 pounds per acre**, immediately available on exchange sites
- To deliver meaningful trace elements from rock dust alone, you'd need something like 15–40 tons per acre

The standard counterargument — made by [[tad-hussey]] in the episode — is that rock dust feeds the [[soil-food-web]]: microbes weather the minerals and cycle nutrients into plant-available forms over successive seasons (the same mechanism as the silicate-mineralizing bacteria in [[silicon-in-plants]]). Solomon accepts this happens but wants quantitative proof: his proposed test is **leaf tissue analysis** — grow with rock dust, send leaves to a lab, and see whether copper, zinc, manganese, and boron actually reached the plant at the desired levels.

Practical reading: use rock dust as a long-term, biology-mediated mineral reservoir (the Singatoga Valley is the natural proof of concept), but don't count on it to fix a measured trace element deficiency this season. For that, use soluble or chelated forms.

## Trace Element Sources and Organic Certification

- **Sulfates** (zinc sulfate, copper sulfate, manganese sulfate) are the cheap, effective standard — but under organic programs (NOP in the US, and similarly in Australia) they're only permitted when a soil test documents the deficiency
- **Chelated trace elements** (e.g., Biomin line, often OMRI-listed) are an alternative organic growers may be more comfortable with; users report needing only about half the rate for the same result
- **Kelp meal** supplies a broad trace element spectrum at low concentrations
- Availability varies by region — Solomon notes that at the end of Tasmania's supply chain, manganese sulfate is sometimes the only practical manganese source

## Containers: CEC Changes Everything

Solomon's framework for container growing turns on **buffering capacity** ([[soil-structure-and-aggregates|CEC]]):

- A medium with *no* exchange capacity fed by soluble nutrients "is hydroponics, even if it looks like soil" — even with living compost in it, if nutrition arrives in the water, it's hydroponics
- A medium with CEC above ~20 can hold mineral amendments on exchange sites, where they wait for the plant to draw them off — stable, field-like nutrition
- High-CEC media can therefore be amended and managed like field soil: mix in as much of the amendment as possible without making the mix "too hot," then top-dress more as the plant grows
- Test the media at the end of each cycle and re-amend to bring it back into balance — the *Intelligent Gardener* loop applied to pots (see [[reusing-soil]])

## Expect a Multi-Year Arc

Remineralization is not a single application:

- Topsoil responds first; subsoil takes years as minerals leach downward
- Deep-feeding crops (carrots, parsnips, beets, chicories) are your subsoil gauge — they improve last
- Flavor improvements compound year over year ([[nutrient-density]])
- In living systems, microbial weathering of mineral amendments improves availability over successive cycles

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Use *Intelligent Gardener* targets for everything **except potassium — run roughly twice** the book's recommendation. Cannabis/hemp is a fiber-building, vegetatively vigorous crop that draws potassium hard and also uses unusually large amounts of manganese. This 2× potassium finding comes independently from Solomon's hemp consulting in Tasmania and KIS Organics' container trials, against the traditional advice of running P equal to K.
>
> Match nitrogen to growth stage: vegetative cannabis is a high-nitrogen crop (nitrogen builds chlorophyll), but in flower the plant builds low-chlorophyll bud mass and needs perhaps a third to a quarter as much nitrogen — while still needing full calcium and trace element levels.
>
> Container specifics from the KIS Organics system: ~100 lbs of mineral amendments per yard of soil, thermally composted for two weeks before planting; a 7-gallon pot is roughly the minimum to run water-only through a full cycle (top-dress a nutrient pack at the end of weeks 1 and 3 of flower in smaller pots); larger beds and re-amended living soil produce healthier crops and avoid the dump-and-replace cycle that leaches salt-loaded media into groundwater.

## Key Takeaways

- Define targets first; the soil test is just the measuring tool
- Base cation balancing targets food quality; sufficiency targets only yield — choose deliberately
- Lab numbers vary ~10% between labs, but the ratios — which are what matter — agree
- Rock dust supplies trace elements too slowly to fix a current deficiency; treat it as a long-term microbial reservoir and verify with tissue tests
- Sulfates fix deficiencies fast but face organic-certification limits; chelates and kelp are the workarounds
- Growing media with CEC > ~20 can be amended and managed like field soil; media without buffering is hydroponics regardless of appearance
- Remineralization compounds over years as nutrition leaches into the subsoil
- For cannabis: 2× potassium, generous manganese, stage-matched nitrogen
