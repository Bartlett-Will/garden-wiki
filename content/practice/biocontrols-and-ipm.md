# Biocontrols and IPM

> **Quick take:** Targeted biological controls — specific fungi and bacteria that attack pests and pathogens — outperform broad-spectrum approaches because they protect the beneficial organisms your plants depend on.

Integrated pest management (IPM) is about using the right tool at the right scale. In the context of [[soil-food-web|living soil]] systems, that means favoring biological controls that target specific problems over broad-spectrum treatments that wipe out beneficial organisms alongside pests. As [[tad-hussey|Tad Hussey]] explains, the rhizosphere is a complex ecosystem — indiscriminate treatments do collateral damage that can take months to repair.

## Trichoderma: The Predatory Fungus

Trichoderma species are among the most versatile biocontrol agents available to home gardeners. These fungi work through multiple mechanisms:

- **Direct parasitism** — Trichoderma physically attacks and consumes pathogenic fungi. It wraps around the target organism, penetrates its cell walls, and feeds on its contents.
- **Siderophore production** — Trichoderma produces siderophores, specialized molecules that bind iron and make it unavailable to competing (often pathogenic) organisms. This is essentially chemical warfare at the microbial scale — starving pathogens of a nutrient they need.
- **Competition** — By colonizing root surfaces rapidly, Trichoderma occupies the ecological niche that pathogenic fungi would otherwise fill.

Trichoderma is available as a commercial inoculant and can be applied at transplant or incorporated into growing media. It establishes a protective biological layer around roots and persists through the growing cycle.

## Entomopathogenic Fungi: Beauveria and Metarhizium

Entomopathogenic fungi are fungi that infect and kill insects. The two most important genera for gardeners are:

### Beauveria bassiana

A naturally occurring soil fungus that infects a wide range of insect pests. Spores contact the insect's body, germinate, penetrate the exoskeleton, and colonize the insect from within. Commercially available as sprays and granular products. Effective against aphids, whiteflies, thrips, and many other soft-bodied pests.

### Metarhizium (formerly Metarhizium anisopliae)

Similar mode of action to Beauveria but with different host specificity. Metarhizium species are particularly effective against soil-dwelling pests including fungus gnat larvae and root aphids. These fungi persist in soil and can provide ongoing pest suppression across multiple growing cycles.

Both Beauveria and Metarhizium are targeted — they infect insects through direct contact but do not harm plants, earthworms, or most beneficial soil organisms. This precision is what makes them superior to chemical pesticides in a living soil context.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi: The Root Partnership

[[mycorrhizal-fungi|Mycorrhizal fungi]] form symbiotic associations with plant roots, extending the root system's effective reach by orders of magnitude. The fungal hyphae access water and nutrients (especially phosphorus) from soil volumes the roots alone cannot reach, trading these resources for plant-produced carbon.

For practical application:

- **Inoculate at transplant** — Apply mycorrhizal inoculant directly to roots or the transplant hole. The fungi need root contact to establish the partnership.
- **Use Rhizophagus irregularis** (formerly Glomus intraradices) — This is the most broadly compatible and well-researched species for horticultural crops.
- **Protect the partnership** — High phosphorus fertilization can suppress mycorrhizal colonization (the plant doesn't need the fungus if P is already abundant). Fungicides and soil fumigants kill mycorrhizae.

Mycorrhizal fungi provide indirect pest and disease resistance by improving overall plant health and nutrient status. A well-nourished plant mounts stronger [[plant-stress-responses|stress responses]].

## Neem: the Botanical Arm of IPM

[[neem-and-karanja|Neem]] complements the fungal biocontrols with a botanical mode of action: it disrupts insect feeding and reproduction rather than poisoning on contact, and it suppresses pathogenic fungi and nematodes while *enhancing* beneficial fungal colonies, including mycorrhizae. [[clackamas-coot|Clackamas Coot]]'s protocol (ep-007): blend neem and karanja oils 2:1, emulsify with soap nut tea, spray before dark, and commit to **four applications 72 hours apart** to break the full egg–larva–adult cycle — stopping early is the classic failure. For fungus gnat larvae, a neem meal drench (2 cups aerated in 5 gallons for 24 hours) works from the soil side. See [[neem-and-karanja]] for sourcing and the full method, and [[botanical-teas]] for the tea technique.

## Bicarbonate Fungicides for Powdery Mildew

For outdoor powdery mildew, the cheapest effective fungicide is in the baking aisle. Retail products are mostly **potassium bicarbonate** plus a surfactant, sold at $30 per half pound — but per Steve Diver's published comparison, plain **sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) is 80% as effective**, and a 15-pound bag costs under $10. The nursery sector has used it since the 1920s because it works.

- **Rate:** 1 tablespoon per gallon of water; dissolves instantly; spray and repeat after rain
- **Freshness matters:** bicarbonates lose potency — replace the bag each season
- **Outdoors only:** indoors, manage humidity and airflow instead
- **PNW reality check:** organically powdery-mildew-free squash in a wet maritime climate is close to impossible; variety selection helps more than spraying

## Why Broad-Spectrum Biofumigants Are Problematic

Mustard meal and other brassica-derived biofumigants release isothiocyanates — compounds that are toxic to a broad range of soil organisms. While they are marketed as "natural" alternatives to chemical fumigants, they are fundamentally indiscriminate:

- They kill pathogenic fungi — but also Trichoderma, mycorrhizae, and other beneficial fungi
- They suppress pest nematodes — but also beneficial nematodes that cycle nutrients
- They can disrupt the [[rhizophagy-cycle|rhizophagy cycle]] by killing the Bacillus and Pseudomonas bacteria that plants depend on for nutrient cycling

The damage from biofumigation can take multiple growing cycles to repair, as beneficial microbial communities must recolonize from scratch. In most situations, targeted biocontrols (Trichoderma for fungal pathogens, Beauveria/Metarhizium for insect pests) achieve the same goals without the collateral damage.

The principle: **targeted over indiscriminate**. Identify the specific problem, then apply the specific biological solution.

## Building an IPM Program

A practical biocontrol-based IPM program layers multiple targeted approaches:

1. **Prevention** — Healthy [[soil-food-web|soil biology]], adequate [[silicon-supplementation|silicon nutrition]], and proper [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|watering]] create plants that resist pest and disease pressure naturally.
2. **Biological foundation** — Trichoderma and mycorrhizae at transplant establish a protective biological baseline.
3. **Monitoring** — Regular [[field-observation|observation]] catches problems early when biological controls are most effective.
4. **Targeted response** — When specific pests appear, deploy the appropriate entomopathogenic fungus or beneficial insect.
5. **Soil testing** — Use [[soil-testing-and-diagnosis|soil and tissue analysis]] to ensure nutrient deficiencies aren't weakening plant defenses. Microbes are not alchemists — they cannot create minerals from nothing.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis is **endomycorrhizal only** — it associates with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) like Rhizophagus irregularis, not ectomycorrhizal species. Products containing only ectomycorrhizal species (common in forestry blends) will not colonize cannabis roots. Check the species list on any mycorrhizal product before purchasing. Cannabis growers in regulated markets benefit significantly from biocontrol-based IPM because it reduces or eliminates the need for pesticides that could fail COA testing. [[silicon-supplementation|Silicon supplementation]] pairs well with biocontrols — silicon enables the plant's own immune responses while biocontrols handle the external pest and pathogen pressure.

## Key Takeaways

- Trichoderma is a triple-threat biocontrol: it parasitizes pathogens, produces iron-stealing siderophores, and competes for root-surface territory
- Beauveria bassiana and Metarhizium kill insects through direct contact without harming plants or beneficial soil organisms
- Apply mycorrhizal inoculant (Rhizophagus irregularis) at transplant for the best colonization
- Avoid mustard meal biofumigants — they kill beneficial organisms alongside targets, and the damage takes cycles to repair
- Always prefer targeted biological controls over indiscriminate broad-spectrum treatments
- Healthy soil biology is the foundation of pest resistance — biocontrols supplement it, not replace it
