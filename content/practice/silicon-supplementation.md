# Silicon Supplementation

> **Quick take:** Silicon is the second most abundant element in soil but most of it is locked up — supplementing at 30-60 ppm through root drench gives your plants the raw material to defend themselves against pests, disease, and drought.

Silicon is not officially classified as an essential plant nutrient, but a growing body of research — including 17+ years of work by [[dr-wendy-zellner|Dr. Wendy Zellner]] — shows it plays a critical role in [[silicon-in-plants|plant stress responses]]. The key insight: silicon doesn't fight diseases or pests directly. It enables the plant's own immune and stress responses to operate at full genetic potential. Think of it as giving your plant the building materials it needs to defend itself.

## Target Rates

For soilless and hydroponic systems, aim for **30-60 ppm Si** in your nutrient solution. This range is well-supported by research and provides consistent benefits without risk of toxicity — plants self-regulate silicon uptake, so phytotoxicity essentially doesn't occur at these rates.

For soil-based systems, supplementation is more nuanced because native soil minerals contribute variable amounts of silicon. [[soil-food-web|Silicate-mineralizing bacteria]] (including Bacillus species) in the rhizosphere actively weather rock minerals and release plant-available silicic acid. Living soil systems get better at silicon release over successive cycles as these microbial communities mature.

## Comparing Products: Read the Label Carefully

The supplement market is full of confusing silicon labels. The only form plants can absorb is **silicic acid** (Si(OH)4), also called orthosilicic acid or monosilicic acid — these are all the same compound. Products marketed as "monosilicic acid" at premium prices are not superior to potassium silicate or calcium silicate at the same silicon concentration.

When comparing products, convert everything to **% elemental Si**:

| Label compound | Conversion | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Si (elemental) | 1x | 10% Si = 10% Si |
| SiO2 (silicon dioxide) | Divide by ~2.14 | 21% SiO2 = ~10% Si |
| Si(OH)4 (silicic acid) | Divide by ~3.42 | 34% Si(OH)4 = ~10% Si |

SiO2 labeling inflates the apparent concentration by roughly 2x. Si(OH)4 labeling inflates it by roughly 3.4x. At equal elemental Si concentrations, all sources produce the same plant response.

## Drench vs. Foliar

Silicon moves through the plant via the [[silicon-in-plants|xylem]] (the transpiration stream) only — it cannot move through the phloem and cannot redistribute once deposited in a leaf.

This has practical consequences:

- **Root drench (preferred)** — Silicon absorbed through roots distributes systemically throughout the plant via transpiration. This is the most effective delivery method.
- **Foliar spray** — Silicon stays where it lands. It will protect the sprayed leaf surface but will not move to new growth. You must re-spray every time new leaves emerge.

For comprehensive protection, root drench is the clear winner. Foliar can supplement in acute situations (heavy pest pressure on existing leaves) but cannot replace systemic delivery.

## Choosing a Silicate Type

The three common silicate salts are functionally equivalent at recommended rates:

- **Potassium silicate (K2SiO3)** — Most common in horticulture. Adds a small amount of potassium.
- **Calcium silicate (CaSiO3)** — Common in agriculture (often sold as wollastonite). Adds calcium and raises pH slightly.
- **Sodium silicate (Na2SiO3)** — Sometimes avoided out of sodium concerns, but at recommended silicon concentrations, the sodium contribution is negligible.

Choose based on what else your system needs. If you want a bit of extra potassium, use potassium silicate. If you want calcium and pH buffering, use calcium silicate.

## Slow-Release Sources for Soil Systems

For growers building living soil, several amendments release silicon over time:

- **Wollastonite** — A calcium silicate mineral. Releases roughly 4-6% available Si. One of the better rock-mineral sources.
- **Rice hulls** — Silicon content varies dramatically depending on how the rice was grown. Rice hulls are only high in silicon if the rice crop itself was fertilized with silicon. Always test for arsenic, as rice accumulates it.
- **Rice hull biochar** (pyrolyzed at ~700C) — Releases silicon at similar rates to raw rice hulls, with the added benefits of biochar (carbon stability, water retention, microbial habitat).
- **Diatomaceous earth (DE)** — Releases only about 0.2% Si, much less than wollastonite. Not an efficient silicon source despite its reputation.
- **Perlite** — Releases silicon into solution. Systems using perlite as a growing medium may need less supplementation than growers assume.
- **Rock dusts** — Broad-spectrum mineral amendments. Silicon release varies widely by source rock. Incorporate into living soil and let microbial communities weather them over multiple cycles.

## Living Soil Over Successive Cycles

One of the most interesting findings from silicon research is that living soil systems improve their silicon availability naturally over time. Silicate-mineralizing bacteria in the [[rhizophagy-cycle|rhizosphere]] weather mineral particles and release plant-available silicic acid. Each growing cycle builds the microbial community's capacity to do this work.

Practical approach for living soil growers:

1. Incorporate rock dusts (wollastonite, basalt) into your initial mix
2. Reuse your media cycle after cycle
3. Supplement with soluble silicate in early cycles while the microbial community establishes
4. Monitor plant health — you may be able to reduce soluble supplementation as cycles progress

## Evaluating Unproven Products

The silicon supplement market includes many products with bold claims and limited evidence. [[dr-wendy-zellner|Dr. Zellner's]] advice: ask vendors for **tissue analysis data** proving that their product actually increases silicon concentration in plant tissue. Before-and-after photos are not evidence of silicon uptake — they could reflect any number of variables. A tissue test showing elevated Si in treated vs. untreated plants is the minimum standard of proof.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis is a **silicon hyperaccumulator**, with tissue concentrations around 2% Si — higher than calcium, potassium, or nitrogen. This makes silicon supplementation particularly important. Because cannabis takes up silicon continuously throughout its entire life cycle, **apply silicon from transplant through harvest without interruption**. The heavy silicon demand also means cannabis is especially vulnerable to heavy metal uptake when silicon-deficient: silicon and arsenic are chemically similar (adjacent on the periodic table), and silicon-starved plants activate extra transport mechanisms that accidentally absorb arsenic. Keeping silicon levels adequate is both a quality and a safety issue. Cannabis is endomycorrhizal, and [[root-hairs-and-nutrient-uptake|mycorrhizal fungi]] may assist with silicon access in organic systems.

## Key Takeaways

- Target 30-60 ppm Si in soilless/hydro; living soil systems build availability naturally over cycles
- Convert all product labels to % elemental Si before comparing — SiO2 and Si(OH)4 labeling inflates numbers
- "Monosilicic acid" products are the same compound as silicic acid at a premium price
- Root drench is far more effective than foliar because silicon moves only through xylem and cannot redistribute
- Potassium, calcium, and sodium silicates are all equivalent at the same Si concentration
- Wollastonite (4-6% Si release) outperforms DE (0.2%) as a slow-release source
- Demand tissue analysis data from vendors, not just photos
