# Scaling and Facility Design

> **Quick take:** Grow into your operation in stages, trial everything at small scale before you commit, and you'll make your expensive mistakes cheaply — on a four-light test room, not a four-greenhouse facility.

The instinct when expanding a garden or grow is to design the whole thing at once and build it big. [[adam-arnold|Adam Arnold]], who built one of the largest living-soil cannabis greenhouse facilities in the US, argues for the opposite: **build out slowly and let each stage teach you.** His central claim is striking — if someone had handed him $8 million in 2018 and said "build the whole facility today," he would have made *more* mistakes, not fewer. Constraint forced learning.

## Build out slowly

Adam built his facility incrementally — a budget greenhouse first to "tide us over," then progressively more sophisticated houses (Nexus, then next-gen) as lessons accumulated. He deliberately left the footprint blank enough to expand into while learning from earlier mistakes. The recurring lesson: **every time you increase scale, you create entirely new problems you could not have foreseen.** A wider 4,200 sq ft greenhouse, for example, made it harder to move dehumidified air to the far side without blocking precious winter sun — a problem that simply did not exist at smaller scale.

This connects to the [[lean-farming|lean farming]] idea of evolving systems through observed reality rather than upfront theory, and to [[single-piece-flow-and-efficiency|the cost-benefit mindset]] of investing where the math actually pays.

The warning case: growers who go big too fast and end up with hundreds of thousands — or millions — of dollars of obsolete equipment because they committed to a method (e.g. full hydroponics) before discovering it wasn't right for them, then had to switch.

## The R&D facility ("the beater car")

Adam keeps a separate, small, fully-indoor living-soil facility as a research-and-development space — what he calls his "beater car." **Nothing is deployed at the main farm until it has been trialed there first:**

- New LED lights tested in dedicated four-light rooms (he has seven) before any large purchase
- New genetics — all pheno-hunting and seed-making happens at R&D; clones never went straight into the production farm
- New beds, soil amendments, and microbial products trialed at small scale
- Being fully indoor, it gives cleaner control by removing seasonal variation, so results are more reliable

This is not advanced science — it's an experimental room — but the payoff is enormous. At scale, mistakes and conversions are extremely expensive, and you often can't even get accurate readings amid all the other variables. Small-scale trialing gives clean feedback and lets you give useful feedback to product companies too.

## The R&D space is also your quarantine space

A trialing facility doubles as a **quarantine space** — somewhere new genetics and incoming material live before they can introduce pests or disease to the main crop. Adam recommends every facility have one, and **offsite is even better**. The cautionary tale: while he was out of town, the team root-drenched an entire greenhouse with insecticidal soap to fight aphids — a brand-new treatment applied at full scale with no small-section trial first. That kind of "the cure is worse than the disease" disaster is exactly what a trial-first culture and quarantine space prevent (and what then gets written into the [[#sops-evolve-with-the-operation|SOPs]]).

## Use the sun

A founding decision was to build *greenhouses*, not sealed indoor rooms. Sun-assisted growing slashes electricity costs and, Adam argues, improves quality (terpene profiles). For 90% of summer his lights are off entirely; even through a Montana winter, lights and dehumidifiers throw off enough heat that heaters run only occasionally. A brutal-winter month produced a surprisingly small natural-gas bill — evidence that the right infrastructure (good light-dep curtains, equipment heat capture) pays back continuously. Indoor rooms offer easier control and stability but at a permanently higher power bill.

## Team and SOPs

Scaling a grow is ultimately a people problem. Adam's notes:

- **Hire "blank slates."** People without years of cannabis experience often bring fewer bad habits and more humility; the best team members can change their minds when shown new information. Horticulture or botany backgrounds without cannabis baggage are ideal.
- **Run on an operating system.** Collective Elevation adopted EOS (Entrepreneurial Operating System), with an **accountability chart** that lists every seat's duties and asks whether each person *gets it, wants it, and has the capacity* for that seat. A person who doesn't becomes a "sacred cow."
- **State the vision.** The whole team should know the one-, three-, and five-year goals and the core values (sustainability and accountability among them) — hire and fire against those values.

### SOPs evolve with the operation

Standard Operating Procedures are not write-once documents. They differ drastically by season (a December greenhouse SOP is useless in July) and must be updated every time a new problem appears. The discipline: when something goes wrong, fix it *and* write the fix into the SOP so it survives a key person being sick or absent. Over time the team accumulates a "quiver" of staged responses to every recurring problem.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> For cannabis specifically: a quarantine/R&D space is the single best defense against importing pests and pathogens with new genetics, and the only sane way to evaluate new lights, nutrients, or microbial products before betting a harvest on them. Even a home grower with one tent benefits from trialing a new amendment on a few plants before treating the whole room — the "trial small, then scale" principle holds at every size. And choosing sun-grown living soil over sealed rooms is both a quality and a sustainability decision.

## Key Takeaways

- Build out in stages — each increment teaches lessons that prevent bigger, costlier mistakes
- Every jump in scale creates new, unforeseeable problems; small-scale experience is what lets you absorb them
- Keep a small R&D space to trial every light, genetic, amendment, and product before deploying at scale
- That same space serves as a quarantine for incoming genetics and material — offsite is better
- Never apply an untrialed treatment at full scale (the "cure worse than the disease" trap)
- Use the sun: greenhouses cut power costs and can improve quality versus sealed indoor rooms
- Hire teachable people, run on a clear accountability system, and treat SOPs as living, season-specific documents
