# Neem and Karanja

> **Quick take:** Neem is a whole-plant pest and disease management tool — hundreds of active compounds that disrupt insect feeding and reproduction — and its pressed meal doubles as a soil amendment that feeds beneficial biology.

Neem comes from a tree (a close relative of mahogany, originally from Burma) that India has treated as a "village pharmacy" for thousands of years — the Tamil word for neem literally means that, and the tree appears in Indian art going back millennia. [[clackamas-coot|Clackamas Coot]] is one of the strongest advocates for neem in living soil systems, and his central point is that neem is not a poison: it works by disrupting insect feeding and reproduction, not by killing on contact.

## What Neem Actually Is

Neem is **wild-harvested, not a crop**. All parts of the tree are used (roots, bark, leaves, kernels), but for gardeners the kernel is what matters:

- **Neem oil** — pressed from the kernel. Used emulsified in water as a foliar spray.
- **Neem meal (cake)** — what remains after pressing. Still retains roughly 7–9% of the oil, which is why the meal works as a soil amendment and tea ingredient. The rest of the world calls it "cake"; the US calls pressed seed products "meals."

Because neem is wild-harvested and processed in many districts, **quality varies enormously by source**. Coot describes opening a bag of cheap imported neem meal adulterated with wood chips. Find a supplier with a track record and stay loyal.

## How It Works — the Whole Carrot, Not Carotene

Azadirachtin gets all the attention because it's the easiest compound to extract — which is why isolated "azadirachtin" products dominate shelves. But neem contains **368 identified active compounds, of which only about 110 have been catalogued and researched** (roughly 22 are relevant to gardening). The suite includes compounds that:

- Act as antifeedants — insects that ingest it can't keep food down
- Prevent females from laying eggs (neem is used as livestock birth control)
- Suppress pathogenic nematodes and pathogenic fungi while **enhancing beneficial fungal colonies** — research Coot cites shows enhanced mycorrhizal colony development in neem-amended soil

Coot's analogy: being told to eat oranges for vitamin C doesn't mean a vitamin C tablet does the same job. "It's not about carotene — it's about the carrot." Use the whole input, not the isolated active.

Two more points that surprise people: neem trees **fix nitrogen** (which is why they thrive in poor soils across many climates), and neem is safe enough that it appears in toothpaste, soap, and skin treatments throughout India.

## Soil First: the Indian Tradition

In India neem goes **in the soil as meal** — the spray-focused approach is an American habit. Coot adds neem meal to his [[vermicomposting|worm bins]] and his raised beds. Podcast host [[tad-hussey|Tad Hussey]] verified with microscope work (after large worm bins amended with neem and basalt per Coot's recommendation) that neem cake **increased** beneficial bacteria and fungi rather than inhibiting them — a common worry, debunked.

For fungus gnats: aerate or stir **2 cups of neem meal in 5 gallons of water for 24 hours** and use as a soil drench to kill larvae. See [[botanical-teas]] for the broader method.

## Foliar Protocol: Neem + Karanja

For spraying, Coot blends neem oil with karanja oil (from a related Indian tree) at roughly **2 parts neem to 1 part karanja** — about 66/33, based on an Indian study showing the combination elevates both insect and disease suppression. Two teaspoons neem plus one teaspoon karanja makes the standard tablespoon-per-application rate.

The discipline that makes it work:

1. **Emulsify properly** in warm water — neem oil doesn't mix into water on its own. Soap nut tea is an ideal natural emulsifier: soap nuts contain ~250,000 ppm (25%) saponins, and the spray comes out foamy and coats and sticks to leaves.
2. **Spray right before sundown or lights-out**, so the application sits on the leaves for hours.
3. **Four applications, 72 hours apart — no shortcuts.** The goal is breaking the complete egg–larva–adult cycle. Stopping after two applications because "everything looks dead" is the classic failure mode.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis forums are full of claims that neem "gets into the plant and ruins the taste." Coot's verdict: stoner science. Neem is not systemic in that sense and is safe enough to ingest (vile-tasting, but safe). The legitimate cannabis-specific caution is timing — like any oil-based foliar, avoid spraying flowers; use neem meal in soil and complete foliar IPM during veg. For growers in regulated markets, neem meal in soil plus [[biocontrols-and-ipm|targeted biocontrols]] manages pests without products that fail residue testing.

## Key Takeaways

- Neem meal quality varies wildly by source — buy from a proven supplier
- Azadirachtin is one of 368 active compounds; whole neem beats isolated extracts
- Neem disrupts feeding and reproduction; it is not a contact poison
- Blend 2:1 neem-to-karanja for elevated insect and disease suppression
- Foliar: emulsify in warm water (soap nuts help), spray before dark, 4 applications 72 hours apart
- Neem meal in soil and worm bins increases beneficial bacteria and fungi — verified by microscopy
- Neem meal tea (2 cups/5 gal, 24 h) drench controls fungus gnat larvae
