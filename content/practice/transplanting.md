# Transplanting

> **Quick take:** Transplanting is about managing the [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|air-water relationship]] at each stage of root development — move plants up in progressive steps, score the roots aggressively, and never skip straight from a small container to a large one.

> [!info] Conflicting views: start in the final container, or pot up progressively?
> **Don Marshall** (ep-041/071) warns to *never* jump a small plant into a large container — it can't dry the soil volume, inviting overwatering/anaerobic stress. **Jeff Lowenfels** (ep-050), on [[autoflowering-cannabis]], advises starting autoflowers in their *final* container because the short cycle leaves no room for transplant recovery. The exception hinges on right-sized pots and careful watering.

Moving a plant from one container to another sounds simple, but it's one of the most consequential things you do as a grower. Done well, it sets the plant up for rapid root colonization and healthy growth. Done poorly, it creates waterlogged zones, root circling, and months of stalled progress.

## Why Progressive Potting

The instinct is to save time: put the small cutting in the big pot and let it grow into it. But as [[dawn-marshall]] explains, this creates a serious problem:

1. A small root system can only access a small volume of soil.
2. The rest of the large container stays saturated — roots can't draw water from it.
3. That saturated mass becomes anaerobic (no oxygen), which kills [[root-hairs-and-nutrient-uptake|root hairs]] and blocks nutrient uptake.
4. The plant stalls, showing symptoms identical to [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|drought stress]] — because it effectively *is* drought stress.

**Progressive potting** keeps the soil volume proportional to the root mass at each stage:

- **Stage 1:** Cutting or seedling into a small pot (e.g., 4-inch or 1-quart).
- **Stage 2:** Once roots fill that container, move to an intermediate size (e.g., 1-gallon).
- **Stage 3:** Once roots fill the intermediate, move to the final container.

At each stage, the roots can access and dry out the entire soil volume, maintaining the wet-dry cycle that roots need for healthy respiration and growth.

### How to know it's time

- **Roots visible at drainage holes** — the root system has filled the container.
- **Rapid drying** — the pot goes from wet to dry in 24 hours or less.
- **Root-bound inspection** — knock the plant out and look. If roots are circling the outside of the root ball, it's past time.

## Root Scoring Technique

When you pull a root-bound plant from its pot, the roots are circling the outside of the root ball in the shape of the container. If you transplant without addressing this, those roots continue circling — they don't magically redirect outward into the new soil.

**Root scoring** breaks this pattern and triggers new branching:

1. **Four vertical cuts** down the sides of the root ball, evenly spaced (like quartering an orange). Cut about 1/4 to 1/2 inch deep, depending on root ball size.
2. **One cut across the bottom**, especially if roots have matted at the base.
3. **Don't be gentle.** This is the key point [[dawn-marshall]] emphasizes. Gardeners tend to be too timid with root scoring. You're not hurting the plant — you're forcing it to branch.

### Why scoring works

Unlike stems, which have a node/internode structure and can only branch from nodes, **roots branch from any point where they're cut**. There's no node structure in roots. Wherever you sever a root, new lateral roots emerge from the cut end. This is the same biology behind [[pruning-and-training|pruning]] responses in stems, but without the node constraint.

A scored root ball quickly sends new roots outward into the fresh soil of the new container, rather than continuing to circle within its old footprint.

## Root Girdling

Scoring isn't only about redirecting roots outward — it prevents **root girdling**, a slow strangulation that develops when circling roots are left intact through successive repots.

Here is the mechanism [[dawn-marshall|Don Marshall]] describes:

1. Roots increase in **girth** over time, thickening from a **lateral meristem** (a layer of dividing tissue around the root's circumference), the same way a stem thickens.
2. When two roots cross and both keep thickening, they press against each other.
3. That pressure crushes the **vascular tissue** — the xylem and phloem that carry water up and sugars down — inside the pinched roots.
4. Water and nutrient flow through those roots is choked off, starving the growth they were supplying.

The analogy is a stake tie left on a stem: as the trunk grows in girth against an un-loosened tie, the tie girdles the stem and cuts off flow. A circling root does the same thing to its neighbors from the inside of the root ball.

This is why scoring matters at **every** stage of repotting, not just the final one. Cutting circling roots before they thicken and cross gives each root room to expand into new soil instead of strangling the ones beside it. A root ball that looks impressively "full" — roots wrapped densely around each other — is often already girdling itself, not a sign of health.

## Media Boundary Effects

When transplanting into a larger container, the interface between the old root ball's media and the new surrounding media matters:

- **Hydrophobic barriers** can form if the old root ball's media dries out faster (or slower) than the surrounding soil. Water follows the path of least resistance and may channel around the old root ball rather than through it.
- **Different media textures** create boundaries that roots are reluctant to cross. A peat-based nursery mix inside a perlite-heavy final mix creates an abrupt transition.

### How to minimize boundary effects

- **Rough up the root ball** during scoring — don't leave a smooth, compressed surface.
- **Moisten the root ball** before transplanting so it's not a dry sponge sitting in wet soil.
- **Use compatible media** between stages when possible. If your final container uses a significantly different mix, the transition through an intermediate pot helps acclimate the root system.

## Backfilling with Mixed Native Soil

When transplanting into the ground (not containers), the same boundary-effect logic applies, but with an additional consideration:

- **Don't fill the planting hole with pure compost or amendment.** This creates what [[dawn-marshall]] calls "a pot inside the ground" — the roots grow happily in the rich pocket but never colonize the native soil beyond it.
- **Mix native soil with your amendment** at roughly a 50/50 ratio for backfill. This creates a gradient that encourages roots to transition into the native soil.
- **Match the final conditions** the roots will need to grow in. If your native soil is heavy clay, the plant needs to learn to live in it — a pure-compost pocket just delays the reckoning.

## Root Inspection Before Transplanting

Every transplant is an opportunity to assess root health. Before placing the plant in its new container:

- **Color:** White or cream-colored roots = healthy and actively growing. Brown or dark roots = dead or dying.
- **Texture:** Firm roots = healthy. Mushy roots = waterlogged and decaying.
- **Smell:** Healthy roots have a mild, earthy smell. Sour or rotten smell = anaerobic decomposition, indicating the plant has been overwatered.
- **Distribution:** Roots should be spreading throughout the root ball, not just circling the edges or matted at the bottom.
- **Root hairs:** Carry a magnifier. Visible [[root-hairs-and-nutrient-uptake|root hairs]] (fine, fuzzy extensions near root tips) indicate active absorption capacity.

If the roots look unhealthy, address the underlying cause (usually [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|overwatering]]) before or during transplanting. Putting sick roots into a bigger pot of wet soil won't help — it will make things worse.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Transplanting is a critical control point in cannabis cultivation:
>
> - **Don't be too gentle with scoring.** Cannabis growers tend to baby their plants, but timid root scoring leaves circling roots intact. Make firm, deliberate cuts — the plant recovers quickly and the new root branching is worth the momentary stress.
> - **Standard cannabis progression:** Clone or seedling into a small container (solo cup or 4-inch pot), then to 1-gallon, then to the final container (3-gallon, 5-gallon, or larger depending on grow style). Each step should happen when roots have filled the current container.
> - **Timing with veg/flower transition:** Ideally, the final transplant happens at least 1-2 weeks before flipping to flower. This gives the root system time to colonize the new soil volume before the plant redirects energy to bud production.
> - **Fabric pots** reduce root circling (air pruning at the edges) but progressive potting still matters — a clone in a 5-gallon fabric pot still can't dry out that volume fast enough.
> - **Root inspection at each transplant** is your best diagnostic window. If roots are brown and sparse, review your [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship|watering practices]] before the problem follows the plant into its new container.

## Key Takeaways

- Never jump from a small container to a large one — progressive potting keeps soil volume matched to root mass
- Root scoring requires four vertical cuts plus one across the bottom, and you should be more aggressive than feels comfortable
- Roots branch from any cut point (unlike stems, which only branch from nodes) — scoring forces outward colonization
- Score at every repot to prevent root girdling — circling roots thicken and strangle each other's vascular tissue, like an un-loosened stake tie on a stem
- Media boundaries between old root ball and new soil can block water movement — rough up surfaces, keep root balls moist, and use compatible media
- When planting in the ground, mix native soil into the backfill to avoid creating "a pot inside the ground"
- Every transplant is a diagnostic opportunity — inspect root color, texture, smell, and distribution before potting up
