# The 5S System

> **Quick take:** 5S is a five-step workspace organization method — Sort, Set in Order, Shine, Standardize, Sustain — that eliminates wasted time searching for tools, tripping over clutter, and re-learning where things go.

If you've ever spent ten minutes looking for your pruners, walked to the shed three times in an hour, or abandoned a task because your workspace was too chaotic to think — 5S is for you. It's the most immediately rewarding piece of [[lean-farming]], and most growers see results within a single weekend.

The five S's come from Japanese manufacturing terms. Each builds on the one before it.

## 1. Seiri — Sort

**The question:** Did this tool, supply, or piece of equipment add value last season?

Walk through your garden, shed, greenhouse, or grow room and touch everything. If it didn't contribute to a harvest or a meaningful task in the last growing season, remove it. Be ruthless. That broken hose reel, the half-bag of amendment you bought on impulse, the third rake — they're not just clutter. They're visual noise that slows your decision-making and obscures what matters.

Sorting is not organizing. You're not rearranging — you're *subtracting*.

## 2. Seiton — Set in Order

**The principle:** Tools belong at their point of use, not in a centralized shed.

This is the most counterintuitive step for gardeners. The instinct is to build one nice tool shed and keep everything there. But every trip to the shed is motion waste. Instead:

- **Distribute tools to where they're used.** If you prune in three different areas, consider three pairs of pruners. For any tool under $50, duplicates pay for themselves in saved time almost immediately.
- **Mount tools at eye level** on hooks, magnets, or pegboard at each work station.
- **The rule:** tools are either in their designated place or in your hands. Never on the ground, never on a bench, never "somewhere around here."
- **Use multi-function tools** where possible to reduce the total count.

Think of it like a kitchen: you don't walk to the garage for a spatula. Your garden work zones deserve the same logic.

## 3. Seiso — Shine

**The method:** Clean in focused 60-90 minute sessions, then restore everything to "zero."

"Zero" means the baseline state — everything clean, put away, and ready for the next session. After every 60-90 minute work block, stop and restore to zero before moving on. This aligns with the neuroscience of [[single-piece-flow-and-efficiency|deep work]]: your brain works best in focused blocks of this length, and a clean reset between blocks maintains that focus.

**Photo-based standards** make this concrete and repeatable:

1. Set up each work area the way it should look at "zero."
2. Photograph it.
3. Print and hang the photo at the workstation.

Now "clean" isn't a vague aspiration — it's a specific, visible target. Anyone (including future-you after a long week) can look at the photo and restore the space to match.

## 4. Seiketsu — Standardize

**The habit:** Repeat the same sorting, ordering, and cleaning sequence every time.

Standardization means you don't reinvent your cleanup routine each session. The process is the same whether it's March or September, whether you're energized or exhausted. The photo standards from Step 3 anchor this — they define what "done" looks like so you don't have to think about it.

This is where 5S shifts from a one-time cleanup to a permanent operating system.

## 5. Shitsuke — Sustain

**The accountability:** Third-party audits with numerical ratings.

Sustain is the hardest step because it fights entropy. [[ben-hartman]] recommends having someone outside your operation — a neighbor, a fellow gardener, a farming friend — walk through periodically and rate your 5S compliance on a numerical scale. Self-assessment drifts. External eyes catch the slow creep of clutter and shortcuts.

Schedule these audits. Quarterly is a reasonable starting cadence for a home garden; monthly for a commercial operation.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> The 5S system is especially impactful in grow rooms, where:
>
> - **Confined spaces amplify disorganization.** A cluttered 4x8 tent is functionally unusable. Sorting and distributing tools (hang scissors, pH pens, and spray bottles at each station) eliminates the constant in-and-out that disrupts canopy work.
> - **Cleanliness is pest prevention.** "Shine" isn't just about aesthetics — dead plant material on the floor is habitat for fungus gnats, and disorganized spaces hide early signs of mites or mildew. A clean grow room is an extension of [[biocontrols-and-ipm|IPM]].
> - **Psychological benefits are real.** Growers who implement 5S consistently report that walking into an organized, clean room changes their mental state. They notice problems faster because the signal-to-noise ratio is higher. When everything is in order, a single drooping leaf or an unusual spot stands out immediately.
> - **Photo standards for each growth stage** — vegetative, early flower, late flower, harvest — give you a visual checklist that catches drift before it becomes a problem.

## Key Takeaways

- Sort first by *removing*, not rearranging — if it didn't add value last season, it goes
- Distribute tools to points of use instead of centralizing in one shed — duplicate anything under $50
- Clean in 60-90 minute focused blocks, then restore to "zero" before the next session
- Photograph your "zero" state and hang the photos at each workstation for a concrete, repeatable standard
- Sustain through third-party audits — self-assessment always drifts over time
- 5S is not a one-time cleanup; it's a permanent operating system that compounds in value
