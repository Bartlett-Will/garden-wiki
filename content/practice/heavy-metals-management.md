# Heavy Metals Management

> **Quick take:** Heavy metals enter your garden mostly through inputs — and if you're failing for arsenic, the first thing to check is your kelp meal.

Low levels of heavy metals are a fact of life in organic growing: organic amendments are natural materials, and natural materials carry trace arsenic, lead, cadmium, and friends. The goal is not zero — that's nearly impossible with organic inputs — but *management*: knowing which inputs carry which metals, not stacking high-metal inputs, and reacting proportionately. As [[bryant-mason|Bryant Mason]] puts it: don't freak out about anything unless you're actually failing.

## Where the Metals Come From

Bryant sourced heavy metal test data (arsenic, cadmium, mercury, lead, nickel, barium) across many brands of common inputs — soft rock phosphate, kelp meal, poultry products, alfalfa, karanja, soybean, neem, blood meal, and more. The pattern: certain product *categories* are consistently high in certain metals, and within a category, brands vary enormously.

Headline findings:

- **Arsenic: check your kelp meal first.** Some kelp meals are very low, some very high. This is not a reason to avoid kelp ([[kelp-and-seaweed]]) — it's a reason to use tested brands and moderate quantities. Often less is more.
- Other arsenic sources: some **alfalfa** products, some **rock dusts**, and a couple of brands of **gypsum**.
- Composts vary in metal content like everything else — one more reason quality evaluation matters ([[composting-and-organic-inputs]]).

## Water and Facility Sources

Most growers' source water is not a major metal contributor unless the well is contaminated. But **RO water deserves specific caution**: stripped of all dissolved minerals, ultra-pure water aggressively bonds with whatever it touches. A reservoir pump or facility plumbing (especially older or lead-containing plumbing) can leach metals into RO water that ordinary mineralized water would leave alone ([[water-chemistry-and-alkalinity]]).

## Genetics: The Cultivar Wildcard

Cultivar differences in metal uptake are large. Two genetics in the same room, in the same soil, can return significantly different heavy metal test results — some cultivars "pull up heavy metals like crazy and other ones couldn't seem to care less" ([[tad-hussey]]). Hemp and cannabis are, unfortunately, excellent bioaccumulators — good enough that hemp is used for phytoremediation, which is exactly what you don't want in a crop headed for compliance testing.

## Can You Crop Metals Out?

Asked whether a cover crop could pull arsenic out of a failed soil, Bryant had no specific species to offer — grasses are general bioaccumulators (he has cropped out excess potassium with grasses), but the best-known metal accumulator is cannabis itself. Phytoremediation of a failed bed remains an open question rather than an established practice ([[cover-crops-and-crop-rotation]]).

## Mitigation Levers

- **Source tested inputs** — within every category, low-metal brands exist. Ask suppliers for heavy metal data.
- **Reduce quantities** — if you're near limits and using large amounts of a suspect category (e.g. kelp), cut the rate before abandoning the input.
- **Don't stack risk** — review the *whole* input program; metals accumulate across inputs and across repeated applications.
- **Mind silicon nutrition** — silicon-deficient hyperaccumulators upregulate the shared silicon/arsenic transporters and pull in *more* arsenic. Adequate silicon supplementation measurably reduces heavy metal uptake ([[silicon-in-plants]], [[silicon-supplementation]]).
- **Test before you panic** — react to actual failures, not hypothetical ones.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Regulated cannabis markets test flower for heavy metals — typically arsenic, cadmium, lead, and mercury — and failures mean destroyed product. The practical checklist: (1) if failing for arsenic, audit kelp meal first, then alfalfa, rock dust, and gypsum; (2) keep silicon supplementation continuous, since cannabis is a silicon hyperaccumulator whose arsenic uptake rises when silicon-starved; (3) track cultivar-level test results — your "dirty" genetics may need a cleaner input program or a different bed than your indifferent ones; (4) if running RO, inspect what the water touches between membrane and plant. Extraction concentrates metals along with cannabinoids, so concentrate producers have even less headroom.

## Key Takeaways

- Organic inputs always carry trace metals — manage levels, don't chase zero
- Product categories are consistently high in particular metals; brands within a category vary widely
- Arsenic failures: kelp meal first, then alfalfa, rock dusts, gypsum
- RO water can strip metals from pumps and plumbing; mineralized water mostly doesn't
- Cultivar genetics strongly affect uptake; cannabis and hemp are potent bioaccumulators
- Silicon deficiency increases arsenic uptake in hyperaccumulators like cannabis
- No established cover crop reliably remediates metals from a failed soil
- Only escalate when you're actually failing tests
