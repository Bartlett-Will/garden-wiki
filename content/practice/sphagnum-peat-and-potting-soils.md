# Sphagnum Peat and Potting Soils

> **Quick take:** A compressed bale of Canadian sphagnum peat is cut frozen from the bog and shipped untouched — alive. The peat in bagged potting soil has been kiln-baked and steam-injected — dead. Buy the bale and build your own.

The difference between good and bad growing media usually comes down to processing and marketing, not ingredients. [[clackamas-coot|Clackamas Coot]] walks through how the peat and potting soil industries actually work — knowledge that explains why serious soil builders start from raw bales (see [[the-coot-mix]]).

## How Sphagnum Peat Is Harvested

Canadian sphagnum peat moss is managed by the **Canadian Sphagnum Peat Moss Association** — a partnership of private harvesters and the national government that sets the total harvest. Only about **eight or nine companies** hold harvest rights. One of them, **SunGro Horticulture** (Sunshine mixes, Black Gold), is bigger than all its competitors combined — about **54% of the total harvest** in a recent year.

The path matters more than the brand:

- **Compressed 3.8 cu ft bales** are cut from the bog **while frozen**, wrapped, and shipped. No kiln, no steam — "as untouched as you can get at our level," microbes included. A bale fluffs up to roughly **5–6 cubic feet** when broken up for mixing.
- **Peat destined for bagged mixes** is "sterilized" in a kiln on a conveyor, chopped to a uniform cosmetic size, then **injected with steam** to rehydrate it for bagging. Whatever biology it had is gone twice over.

If no nursery supply near you stocks bales: Ace Hardware's ship-to-store program delivers them with no shipping fee.

## How Commercial Potting Soil Is Made

None of the big-box brands mix their own soil — it's subcontracted to the same few large players. A modern auger system mixes **an entire truckload in eight minutes**, then bags and stacks it. Multiply by thousands of Home Depot and Lowe's stores, each stocking pallets of a dozen brand names, and the economics become clear: potting soil is a **loss leader**. The store cheerfully replaces the bag that killed your plant — "they sell you the problem, and they've got it all set up how they're going to fix it" with amendments and bottled fixes.

### Decoding the label

- **"Composted forest materials" / "aged bark fines" / "forest humus"** — timber industry waste, the residue left after cellulose extraction, selling for about **$5 a yard (≈18¢ per cubic foot)**. Stacking it behind the barn for six months isn't composting.
- **Bark** is naturally **allelopathic** — trees evolved it to inhibit microbial growth, which is exactly why bark mulch suppresses weeds and exactly why you don't want it in living soil.
- **"Natural"** has no statutory or regulatory meaning in US food labeling — and even less in pet food and garden products. "Made from organic and natural materials" on a redesigned bag means nothing.
- **"Topsoil"** is industry code for *anything*. Coot won't put bagged topsoil on ornamentals.
- Recycled nursery waste shows up in retail bags too — surprise roots in your "premium" soil traced back to the parent company's lily fields.

## Building Instead of Buying

The episode's prescription is the standing theme of this wiki: source a frozen-cut peat bale, quality aeration, and real [[vermicomposting|vermicompost]], then amend with kelp, neem, and minerals — see [[the-coot-mix]] for the recipe. Coot's own raised beds get a spring top-dress of **kelp meal, neem meal, and basalt, covered with about an inch of vermicompost** — and essentially nothing else all season.

For vermicompost specifically, find a local producer — worm farms serving anglers often produce castings, and quality local material beats anything bagged. (Coot talked a local worm-bin maker, Northwest Red Worms, into producing vermicompost; within a couple of years California market gardeners were renting U-Hauls to buy it out — 500 yards of castings and 3,000 pounds of worms in a year.)

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Cannabis growers are the most aggressively marketed-to segment in horticulture — "grow store" soils carry the same games as big-box bags at triple the price, plus proprietary mystery blends. If a label says proprietary, composted forest products, or natural, walk away. A 3.8 cu ft peat bale plus aeration plus quality castings makes more and better media for less money, and you know every input — which matters when the crop is going into your body and through a testing lab.

## Key Takeaways

- Compressed peat bales are cut frozen and shipped untouched — biologically alive; bagged-mix peat is kiln-dried and steam-injected — dead
- A 3.8 cu ft bale fluffs to roughly 5–6 cubic feet of mixing material
- ~8–9 licensed harvesters; SunGro alone takes ~54% of the Canadian harvest
- Retail potting soil is a loss leader mixed by subcontractors at a truckload per eight minutes
- "Composted forest materials" is 18¢/cu ft timber waste; bark is allelopathic and doesn't belong in living soil
- "Natural," "topsoil," and "free-range" carry no regulatory meaning — read ingredient lists, not slogans
- Spring maintenance for established beds: kelp + neem + basalt, topped with an inch of vermicompost
