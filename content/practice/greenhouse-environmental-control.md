# Greenhouse Environmental Control

> **Quick take:** Controlling a greenhouse means staging the cheapest tool for each job — vents before fans, fans before evaporative cooling, soil biology before CO₂ burners — and using deep living soil to buffer the swings you can't fully tame.

A greenhouse trades the total control of an indoor room for the free energy of the sun, and that trade defines the whole problem: you are constantly cooling, heating, humidifying, and circulating air against a moving outdoor target. [[adam-arnold|Adam Arnold]] runs one of the largest living-soil cannabis greenhouse facilities in the US near Bozeman, Montana, where the climate swings roughly 150°F across the year — from about 48°F below zero in winter to 97°F summer highs. His systems are a master class in environmental control because the climate gives no margin for error.

A key theme throughout: **deep living-soil beds buffer environmental stress** to a degree that surprises growers used to hydroponics. Plants in big living-soil beds tolerate poor VPD, high heat, and low humidity far better than salt-based systems, because the root zone stays cool and biologically active. Adam reports simple hoop houses with living soil regularly matching his fully-automated greenhouses. See [[soil-food-web]] and [[reusing-soil]] for why.

## Staging: the cheapest tool for the job

The core control philosophy is a **staging ladder** — the system reaches for the least expensive, least disruptive tool first and only escalates when that tool is exhausted. In Adam's greenhouses the cooling sequence runs roughly:

1. **Roof vents / condensate fan** — a small upper fan stirs air gently from above (it sits above the airflow fans) so one side of the house doesn't go cold while the other stays hot.
2. **Gable exhaust fan** — staged on at a set temperature (e.g. 83°F in winter); a drawbridge-style ramp guides incoming air *up* into the canopy rather than dumping cold air on the nearest plants.
3. **Large exhaust fans** — disabled entirely in winter; used in shoulder seasons and summer.
4. **Wet wall (pad pump)** — evaporative cooling, the last and strongest stage.

Each stage has **lockouts** so tools don't fight each other: an exhaust fan and a heater can never run simultaneously; if a gable fan kicks on, the roof vent closes (otherwise the fan short-circuits, pulling air straight from the vent instead of across the room). The heater is set to kick on at, say, 76–77°F and exhaust not until 83–84°F, creating a gentle yo-yo where ideally the heater runs and the exhaust never does. Heaters at the same time as exhaust = wasted fuel, and a well-trained team develops the "sixth sense" to notice that conflict within minutes.

For the coldest, darkest days when light-deprivation curtains are closed and the house runs as a de-facto indoor room on lights alone, the controller (a Wadsworth system) has a custom **"burp" feature**: the curtains crack open 5% at 83–84°F to release a little heat into the upper canopy, then close again.

## Wet walls (evaporative cooling)

A **wet wall** is essentially a car radiator or swamp cooler for a greenhouse: a corrugated cardboard wall kept wet by a pump, with air drawn across it. Evaporation cools the incoming air and raises its humidity (helpfully nudging [[#vpd-vapor-pressure-deficit|VPD]] in the right direction).

Wet walls work best where it is **hot and dry** — the bigger the temperature-humidity gap, the more cooling you get. Montana is near-ideal: Adam can pull a 30°F-plus differential, taking a 108°F exterior down toward the 60s inside. They are far less effective in humid climates (Seattle, Florida). In winter the wet walls are sealed up with double-wall plastic, and a breezeway on the exhaust side captures warm air rather than dumping it straight outside.

## Airflow: VAF vs. HAF

Even airflow eliminates the **microclimates and dead spots** that breed mildew. Two schools:

- **VAF (Vertical Airflow Fans)** pull air up and over in a slow "mushroom cloud" pattern, gently touching every leaf without blasting any single plant. Pairs well with letting warm air percolate up and out roof vents.
- **HAF (Horizontal Airflow Fans)** are arranged in a circular "racetrack" — fans offset on each side push air around the room in a big loop above the canopy, mixing and stirring.

Neither is wrong; Adam runs two greenhouses each way and hasn't found decisive evidence to convert. A second, lower airflow layer comes from **duct socks** ("duck socks") — fabric ducting laid in the aisle that diffuses a high *volume* of air at low *velocity* under the canopy. Because the ducts draw air from near the floor, they push CO₂-rich air (CO₂ sinks) back up to the stomata, evening out the under-canopy climate without the leaf desiccation a high-velocity fan would cause. Lowering plant count to open a center aisle plus heavier pruning (reducing transpiration and opening under-canopy airflow) were prerequisites that made the duct socks work.

## VPD (Vapor Pressure Deficit)

VPD is the balance of temperature and humidity that governs how fast plants transpire. Matching VPD across plant transitions matters: when moving clones from a 90%-humidity clone room toward a 70%-humidity bedroom, you step humidity down gradually (e.g. 85% on day one) to avoid transplant shock and shave days off the veg cycle. Misters can raise greenhouse humidity to match. The economic logic is sharp — a consultant helped Adam reframe a $10,000 mister system not as a cost but as 20 extra days of productive growth a year across four greenhouses, worth far more than the outlay. See [[transplanting]] and [[single-piece-flow-and-efficiency]] for the cost-benefit mindset.

The living-soil caveat: deep beds buffer VPD so well that plants stay productive even when VPD is "way off" on paper — something hydroponic growers cannot rely on.

## CO₂ in a living-soil greenhouse

Living soil is a continuous CO₂ source: microbial respiration in the beds produces so much CO₂ that an empty greenhouse builds past 1000 ppm overnight, and Adam's beds **out-produce his CO₂ burners**. Practical consequences:

- **Summer:** no CO₂ dosing — the high air-exchange rate (fresh air every minute on a hot day) makes burner CO₂ a drop in the ocean, and the controller locks the burner off whenever an exhaust fan runs.
- **Winter:** burners are available but used sparingly. Dosing CO₂ while *under-lit* is pointless and harmful (see [[limiting-factors-and-the-law-of-the-minimum]]) — Adam traced foxtailing to exactly this. The most useful burner window is sunny shoulder-season mornings before the exhaust fans start.
- **Nighttime venting:** Adam found no benefit to venting the high nighttime CO₂ that living soil produces, and venting would only exhaust expensive heat. (Note: in *indoor* rooms, CO₂ can climb to genuinely problematic levels — thousands of ppm — where nighttime venting does help; greenhouse living-soil levels stayed benign.)

## Backups and alarms

In a 40-below climate, a power failure on the wrong night is catastrophic. Insurance layers: a natural-gas backup generator sized to run the skeleton essentials (heaters, mom-room lights, light-dep curtains, a couple of exhaust fans), staged propane "sunflower" heaters next to each greenhouse, and temperature alarms that text then phone Adam and his head grower if the facility drops below 55°F or rises above 88°F.

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> This is commercial cannabis greenhouse practice, but the transferable lessons scale all the way down to a tent or backyard hoop house: (1) deep living soil buys you enormous forgiveness on VPD, heat, and CO₂ — a living-soil hoop house can match a climate-controlled room; (2) even, gentle airflow under the canopy prevents the mildew and bud rot that kill cannabis crops; (3) don't dose CO₂ unless light is already sufficient; (4) keep roots cool and biologically active and the plant tolerates heat far better. SOPs must be re-tuned by season — a winter playbook is useless in summer.

## Key Takeaways

- Stage controls cheapest-first: vents → small fans → exhaust fans → evaporative cooling; lock out tools that fight each other
- Wet walls (swamp coolers) excel in hot, dry climates and also raise humidity toward target VPD; useless in humid ones
- Even airflow kills microclimates and mildew; VAF (mushroom) and HAF (racetrack) both work; duct socks diffuse high-volume, low-velocity, CO₂-rich air under the canopy
- Deep living-soil beds buffer VPD, heat, and CO₂ supply far better than hydroponic systems
- Living soil out-produces CO₂ burners; don't dose CO₂ when light is the limiting factor
- Match VPD gradually across transplant transitions to avoid shock and shorten cycles
- In extreme climates, redundant heat (backup generator, staged propane) and temperature alarms are essential insurance
