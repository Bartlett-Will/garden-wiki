So what is the soil food web?
The soil food web represents the community of organisms, many of which are microscopic, that live in the soil and are a part of the ecosystem.
Now, why is it not the soil food chain?
The idea being that one organism gets eaten by another organism, and this continues in one direction, going from one trophic level to another and on up the chain.
Well, the reason it's called a web is because of the complexity of these processes.
In addition, because much of the soil food web is microscopic, it's been very difficult to research and fully understand.
In fact, we are constantly learning new things about how plants and microorganisms interact in the soil.
For example, Jeff Lowenfeld's new book, Teeming with Bacteria, I had him on here as a guest if you want to learn more on this topic, discusses new research from Dr. James White on rhizophagy, particular bacteria called endophytes
that are able to move into the plant roots, shed their cellular walls, release nutrients to the plant, and then move back out of the root hair tips into the soil, reform their cellular walls, where they can obtain nutrients from the soil, and then potentially repeat the process.
This is something we had no knowledge of until 2008, when some researchers at the University of Queensland discovered a relationship between a particular bacteria, a non-pathogenic E. coli, and a fungi, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, also known by most people as brewer's yeast.
The soil food web is a dynamic, constantly changing ecosystem, but the concept is relatively simple.
Plants take in sunlight in the form of photosynthesis, and convert this to a variety of different substances, which are then excreted as root exudates.
These exudates are comprised of sugars, amino acids, organic acids, vitamins, enzymes, and more.
These exudates not only feed symbiotic bacteria and archaea, but they work to suppress pathogens by attracting microorganisms that are antagonistic to these pathogens.
The exudates can adjust the pH of the rhizosphere, a fancy word for the area around the roots, so the pH can be buffered into a range that is more suitable for nutrient uptake.
That's one of the reasons why organic growers can get away with growing in greater pH ranges than, say, a hydroponic grower.
So the plant is releasing nutrients to feed the microbes in the rhizosphere.
These exudates are being consumed by the bacteria and archaea in the soil.
Archaea are single-celled organisms that initially were classified as bacteria, and originally discovered in deep sea vents.
They were able to survive in places where no life was thought to exist.
Now we know they exist everywhere, not just in the soil and the ocean, but even in our stomachs and on our skin, and have a variety of roles very similar to bacteria.
So from here on out, when I mention bacteria, you can safely assume that I'm talking about archaea too.
Getting back to the soil food web though, these bacteria are essentially consuming the root exudates,
and then some of what they excrete back into the soil is plant-available nutrients, meaning it's an ionic form like phosphate or nitrate,
identical to what we are adding when we use chemical fertilizers.
Now some of these bacteria are being eaten by larger microorganisms, like bacteria-feeding nematodes or flagellates or ciliates.
A bacteria-feeding nematode can feed on up to 5,000 bacterial cells per day, which is six and a half times their own body weight.
What they then poop out or excrete is plant-available nutrients as well.
This process is called the microbial loop, or referred to as nutrient cycling.
