# Episode 41: Applied Horticulture

> **Foundations Series #4** | Guest: [[dawn-marshall|Dawn Marshall]]

## Summary

[[tad-hussey|Tad Hussey]] sits down with [[dawn-marshall|Dawn Marshall]], a Certified Professional Horticulturist and Certified Arborist with 40+ years of teaching experience, for a conversation that keeps circling back to one question: *why?* Dawn explains the biology behind everyday gardening practices — why overwatering causes the same symptoms as underwatering (root hairs die, creating drought stress), why plants bend toward light (auxin degradation, not "reaching"), why progressive transplanting matters (air-water relationship), and why pruning timing depends on understanding seasonal physiology rather than following calendar rules.

## Key Topics

- [[root-hairs-and-nutrient-uptake]] — one-cell-thick extensions of root epidermis where all water and nutrient absorption happens; killed by overwatering, underwatering, and over-fertilization
- [[watering-and-the-air-water-relationship]] — roots need wet-dry cycles; saturated soil means no oxygen, no ATP, no nutrient uptake
- [[plant-hormones-and-growth]] — auxins and phototropism, apical dominance, and why removing the terminal bud triggers branching
- [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability]] — pH controls the ionic form of nutrients; wrong pH means nutrients are present but locked in unusable forms
- [[pruning-and-training]] — point-of-origin pruning, timing (February rejuvenation vs. July maintenance), and the biology behind each cut
- [[transplanting]] — progressive potting-up, root scoring (4 vertical cuts + bottom), and why going straight to a large container fails

## Key Takeaways

- **Overwatering is drought stress, not drowning.** Root hairs are one cell thick. When soil stays saturated, they die. Without root hairs, the plant cannot absorb water — even though it is surrounded by water. The symptoms (yellow leaves, marginal necrosis) are identical to underwatering.
- **Nutrient uptake is active, not passive.** Roots release H+ ions and take up nutrients through energy-requiring cation/anion exchange. No oxygen in the root zone means no cellular respiration, no ATP, and no nutrient uptake — regardless of what is in the soil.
- **Plants do not "reach for light."** Light degrades auxins on the illuminated side of the stem. The shady side retains more auxin, causing those cells to elongate faster. The resulting differential growth bends the plant toward the light source. Under indoor lights, edge plants bend inward.
- **Apical dominance** explains why topping works: the terminal bud produces auxins that suppress lateral growth. Remove the tip and laterals are freed. Successive pinching creates exponential branching — turning a "Christmas tree" into a "candelabra."
- **Root scoring works** because roots lack node/internode structure (unlike stems). Cut a root anywhere and it branches at the wound site.
- **Progressive transplanting** (small pot to intermediate to final) prevents the air-water problem where a small root mass sits in a large volume of perpetually wet soil.
- **Fungi on roots are often opportunistic**, decomposing tissue that was already dead from other causes. The distinction between cause and effect is critical for diagnosis.
- **Fertilize fruit trees after fruit set** (late May in the Pacific Northwest), not in winter or early spring — spring growth uses stored root reserves.
