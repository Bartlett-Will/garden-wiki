# Pruning and Training

> **Quick take:** Pruning isn't just cutting — it's manipulating [[plant-hormones-and-growth|plant hormones]] to control shape, direct energy, and promote productive growth, and the timing matters as much as the technique.

Every cut you make sends a hormonal signal through the plant. Understanding those signals — especially [[plant-hormones-and-growth|auxins]] and apical dominance — turns pruning from guesswork into a precise tool for shaping growth.

## The Science: Apical Dominance and Auxins

The terminal bud (the growing tip) of a stem produces **auxins** — hormones that flow downward and actively suppress the growth of lateral buds below. This is called **apical dominance**, and it's why an unpruned plant grows tall and narrow rather than bushy.

When you remove the terminal bud:

1. Auxin production at that point stops.
2. Lateral buds below are freed from suppression.
3. Two or more lateral branches emerge where there was one leader.

Successive pinching compounds this effect exponentially. One tip becomes two. Two become four. Four become eight. This is how you transform a single-stemmed plant into a bushy, multi-branched specimen.

Light also interacts with auxins: light degrades auxins on the lit side of a stem, causing them to concentrate on the shaded side. The shaded cells elongate faster, bending the stem toward light. Plants don't "reach" for the sun — it's a mechanical response driven by hormone redistribution. Under uniform indoor lighting, edge plants bend inward because the outer side receives more light and degrades more auxin.

## Two Pruning Approaches

### Point-of-Origin Removal

Remove the entire branch at its junction with the main stem. This is a clean removal — the plant doesn't try to replace what was taken. Use this when you want to permanently reduce the number of branches, improve air circulation, or remove unproductive wood.

**When to use:** Removing crossing branches, thinning dense canopy, eliminating damaged or diseased wood.

### Tipping (Heading Cuts)

Cut a branch partway along its length. This triggers branching at the cut point because you've removed the apical bud's auxin suppression but left the lateral buds intact. The plant responds by pushing new growth from buds below the cut.

**When to use:** When you *want* more branching — shaping hedges, encouraging bushiness, redirecting growth direction.

**The distinction matters:** If you're trying to thin a plant and you make tipping cuts instead of point-of-origin cuts, you'll get *more* growth, not less. This is one of the most common pruning mistakes.

## Seasonal Timing

Timing determines what the plant does with the energy freed by pruning. [[dawn-marshall]] breaks it down by season:

### February: Rejuvenation Pruning

Late winter, before the spring growth surge. The plant's energy reserves are in the roots, ready to fuel new growth. Heavy structural cuts made now will be powered by those reserves when spring arrives. This is the time for:

- Removing dead or damaged wood
- Reshaping the plant's structure
- Hard rejuvenation cuts on overgrown shrubs

### July: Maintenance Pruning

After the spring growth surge, when the plant's stored reserves are at their lowest (energy was spent on spring growth and fruit set). Light maintenance cuts now — removing suckers, shaping, thinning — won't trigger a strong growth response because the plant doesn't have the reserves to push hard.

### Fall: Don't Prune

The common advice that fall pruning "triggers growth that freezes" is, according to [[dawn-marshall]], "a crock" — that's not the real mechanism. The actual reason to avoid fall pruning is that nutrients are actively migrating from leaves into roots for winter storage. Removing branches in fall removes leaves that are still feeding the root reserves the plant needs to survive winter and fuel spring growth. Let the plant finish its natural nutrient-reclamation cycle.

### Fertilization Timing Connection

This seasonal logic extends to fertilization. Spring growth uses **stored root reserves**, not fertilizer you apply in February. The best time to fertilize fruit trees and perennials is after fruit set (late May in the Pacific Northwest), when reserves are depleted and the plant can actually use the input. See [[nitrogen-and-phosphorus]] for nutrient-specific guidance.

## Practical Pruning Technique

1. **Start with the dead.** Remove dead, damaged, and diseased wood first. This is never wrong regardless of season.
2. **Remove crossing branches.** Branches that rub create wound sites for disease entry.
3. **Open the center.** Air circulation through the canopy reduces fungal disease pressure (see [[biocontrols-and-ipm]]).
4. **Step back frequently.** Prune a few cuts, step back, assess. It's easier to cut more than to reattach.
5. **Clean cuts only.** Use sharp tools. Ragged cuts heal slowly and invite pathogens.

### Understanding Node and Internode Structure

Stems have a regular pattern of **nodes** (where leaves and buds emerge) and **internodes** (the stretches between). When you make heading cuts on stems, make them just above a node — that's where the new growth will emerge.

Roots, by contrast, do **not** have node/internode structure. Roots can branch from any point where they're cut. This is the biological basis for [[transplanting|root scoring]].

> [!cannabis] Cannabis Application
> Pruning and training are central to cannabis cultivation, where canopy management directly determines yield:
>
> - **Topping** (removing the terminal bud) transforms the natural "Christmas tree" shape into a **candelabra** — multiple main colas of roughly equal height. Top once to get two main stems, top each of those to get four, and so on. Each topping doubles your main bud sites.
> - **Defoliation and lollipop technique:** Removing lower branches and fan leaves that receive little light (lollipopping) redirects the plant's energy to upper bud sites. Point-of-origin removal is key here — tipping lower branches just creates weak, unproductive side shoots.
> - **Indoor canopy management** under flat light panels rewards flat, even canopies. Combine topping with low-stress training (LST) — bending and tying branches horizontally — to create a uniform canopy height that maximizes light interception.
> - **Timing matters in flower.** Most pruning and defoliation should be completed in late vegetative growth or the first 1-2 weeks of flower. Heavy pruning during mid-to-late flower stresses the plant when it should be focused on bud development.
> - The auxin science explains *why* these techniques work, not just that they do. Understanding the mechanism helps you prune with intention rather than by rote.

## Key Takeaways

- Apical dominance (auxin from the terminal bud suppressing lateral growth) is the mechanism behind all pruning responses
- Point-of-origin removal reduces growth; tipping (heading cuts) increases branching — know which you need before you cut
- February for heavy structural pruning (before spring surge), July for light maintenance (after surge, low reserves)
- Don't prune in fall — leaves are still transferring nutrients to roots for winter storage
- Successive topping compounds branching exponentially: 1 tip becomes 2, then 4, then 8
- Stems branch from nodes; roots branch from anywhere — different biology, different techniques
